## 1. Why this chapter comes first

The Civil Services Examination (CSE) is not one examination. It is a sequence of stages, each with a different purpose. Before you study history, geography or economics, you need a map of that sequence: what you are being selected for, which stage this paper belongs to, what the paper rewards, how its score is calculated, and how to read its syllabus and questions.

This chapter supplies that map. It teaches no subject content. It also gives you a short, low-pressure baseline so that you can see which study skills to build in Chapters 002–006. The baseline is not a mock test, not a predicted score and not an admission barrier.

Three boundaries are worth stating at the start:

- **Eligibility, age, attempts, fees and documents** are governed by the current examination notification, not by this chapter. Read the notification for the year in which you apply.
- **Mains strategy and optional-subject choices** belong to a later stage and a separate plan. This chapter explains only where Mains fits.
- **Exam-day logistics** can change. The admit card and the current UPSC instructions take precedence over any general description here.

## 2. The examination in one page

**UPSC** stands for the Union Public Service Commission, the body that conducts the CSE. **CSE** stands for the Civil Services Examination. It is the route to services such as the Indian Administrative Service (IAS), Indian Police Service (IPS), Indian Foreign Service (IFS) and many other central services. Your final rank and your preferences determine which service you may be allotted.

The selection process is usually described in three parts:

1. **Preliminary Examination (Prelims):** two objective papers, used as a screening test.
2. **Main Examination (Mains):** nine written papers of conventional essay type.
3. **Interview/Personality Test:** an oral assessment.

The notice formally groups the written Mains and the Interview/Personality Test as the second of two successive stages. The three-part description is a common study shorthand, not a different examination scheme.

The marks that decide your final rank come from Mains and the Interview, not from Prelims. The official scheme is summarised below ([UPSC CSE 2026 notice][notice], Appendix I, Sections I–II).

| Stage | Format | Marks | Role |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Prelims General Studies Paper I (GS-I) | Objective MCQs; 2 hours | 200 | Supplies the mark used for the Prelims selection list |
| Prelims General Studies Paper II (GS-II or CSAT) | Objective MCQs; 2 hours | 200 | Qualifying only: at least 33% (66/200) |
| Mains written examination | 9 papers (2 qualifying + 7 merit); 3 hours each | 1750 counted for merit | Merit |
| Interview/Personality Test | Oral | 275 | Merit |
| **Total for final rank** |  | **2025** |  |

Some details matter even at this early stage:

- **Prelims is a screening test.** The notice says that Prelims marks are not counted for the final order of merit. A strong Prelims score gets you into Mains; it does not carry forward.
- **Mains has nine papers.** Two are qualifying language papers (Paper A: an Indian language from the Eighth Schedule; Paper B: English), each of 300 marks, with a minimum of 25% in each under the 2026 notice. Their marks do not enter the rank. The seven merit papers are Essay, GS-I, GS-II, GS-III, GS-IV, and two optional-subject papers, each of 250 marks. The written total is 1750. The notice lists limited exemptions from Paper A for candidates from certain states and some candidates with benchmark disabilities; check the current notice if that may apply to you.
- **The Interview carries 275 marks.** The notice states that it has no minimum qualifying marks. Mains written marks plus Interview marks determine the final ranking.
- **The number admitted to Mains is about 12–13 times the approximate number of vacancies.** This is a selection-ratio rule, not a promise about your chances.
- **Both Prelims papers are compulsory.** The notice states that a candidate who does not appear in both Prelims papers is disqualified. You cannot skip CSAT because you are confident about GS-I.

The Prelims question papers are set in both Hindi and English. The syllabus is the same whichever medium you choose.

## 3. Prelims: two papers, two different jobs

The two Prelims papers are often confused because both are objective and both last two hours. Their roles are different.

| Feature | GS-I | GS-II (CSAT) |
|---|---|---|
| Main content | History, geography, polity, economy, environment, science and current events | Comprehension, reasoning, basic numeracy and data interpretation |
| Recent question count | 100 questions × 2 marks | 80 questions × 2.5 marks |
| Maximum marks | 200 | 200 |
| Duration | 2 hours | 2 hours |
| Role | Used for the Prelims selection mark | Qualifying only, at 33% (66/200) |
| Wrong answer | One-third of the question's marks deducted | Same rule |
| Compulsory | Yes | Yes |

The GS-I question counts are those recorded in the recent paper corpus ([2024][pyq2024], [2025][pyq2025]); the CSAT count is recorded in the [CSAT curriculum](../Prelims-CSAT/Prelims-CSAT-Curriculum.md). The official notice fixes 200 marks and two hours; the number of questions can be checked in the booklet for your year.

### 3.1 What each paper actually decides

- **CSAT is a gate.** You need at least 33%, which is 66 out of 200. Marks above 66 do not improve your Prelims selection mark or your final merit. Marks below 66 end the attempt, however well you did in GS-I.
- **GS-I supplies the selection mark.** Once CSAT is cleared, UPSC draws the list of candidates admitted to Mains using a minimum qualifying mark in GS-I determined for that year. This is what people usually mean by the “Prelims cutoff.”
- **There is no fixed GS-I pass percentage in the notice.** The Commission determines the qualifying mark. It varies by year and category and is published after the cycle. Do not plan around a “safe score.”
- **Prelims does not feed the final rank.** Your Mains and Interview marks decide the rank. Prelims is a door, not a scoreboard for the final result.

### 3.2 The naming trap: Prelims GS-I is not Mains GS-I

Both stages have a paper called “General Studies I.” They are different papers:

- **Prelims GS-I** is the 200-mark objective paper described in this chapter. Its syllabus includes current events, history, geography, polity, economy, environment and general science.
- **Mains GS-I** is a 250-mark descriptive paper on Indian heritage and culture, history and geography of the world and society. It is part of the Mains written examination.

Always attach the stage when you say “GS-I.” A book or coaching module labelled “GS-I” may belong to either stage, and the depth and answer style are different.

## 4. Reading the GS-I syllabus

The official syllabus is short. That is exactly why it needs careful reading: it is a boundary, not a list of chapters. The seven bullets below are reproduced verbatim from the [paper syllabus](Prelims-General-Studies-I-Syllabus.md), which is copied from the [official notice][notice], Appendix I, Section III, Part A.

| Official syllabus wording | What it asks for | Main chapters in this curriculum |
|---|---|---|
| Current events of national and international importance. | Recent developments that can be connected to a syllabus concept; not every item in the newspaper. | 002; 129–131; dynamic portions of 007–128 |
| History of India and Indian National Movement. | Ancient, medieval and modern Indian history, including the freedom struggle; culture where it overlaps. | 067–098; chronology bridge in 003–004 |
| Indian and World Geography-Physical, Social, Economic Geography of India and the World. | Earth processes, climate, oceans, India's physical and human geography, world regions and map locations. | 003–004; 007–021 |
| Indian Polity and Governance-Constitution, Political System, Panchayati Raj, Public Policy, Rights Issues, etc. | Constitutional design, institutions, rights, federalism, local government and policy. | 022–043 |
| Economic and Social Development-Sustainable Development, Poverty, Inclusion, Demographics, Social Sector Initiatives, etc. | Development concepts and indicators, poverty, inclusion, population, health, education and social protection. | 044–066 |
| General issues on Environmental ecology, Bio-diversity and Climate Change - that do not require subject specialization. | Conceptual ecology, biodiversity, pollution and climate, at a level that does not require a science degree. | 005–006; 099–109 |
| General Science. | Everyday physics, chemistry and biology, and applications of science and technology. | 005–006; 110–128 |

The chapter numbers are this curriculum's study plan. They are not an official division of the syllabus, and UPSC does not publish a chapter list.

### 4.1 How to read a syllabus bullet

Read each bullet in three steps:

1. **Underline the nouns.** They tell you what the topic is about. “Panchayati Raj” and “Public Policy” are two different nouns in the polity bullet.
2. **Ask what a question could look like.** A bullet is broad; a question is specific. “Sustainable Development” could produce a question on an indicator, a scheme, a court judgment or an international agreement.
3. **Find the boundary.** The syllabus says what may be asked, not everything that is interesting. “Current events of national and international importance” is not a licence to collect celebrity news or daily market movements.

Two phrases deserve special attention:

- **“etc.”** appears in the polity and economy bullets. It signals that the list is illustrative. Do not ignore a topic merely because it is not named.
- **“that do not require subject specialization”** appears in the environment bullet. It is a difficulty instruction: understand the process and the trade-offs, without specialist mathematics or laboratory science.

There is no official subject-wise weightage in the syllabus. Analyses that count questions by subject are useful for calibration, but they are not a blueprint. A topic that was rare in the last three papers can still appear in 2027.

### 4.2 A five-minute syllabus exercise

Write one possible question for each of the seven bullets. Do not answer the questions. The aim is to turn a broad phrase into a concrete task.

Examples of the kind of question each bullet can generate:

| Bullet | A possible question (not a prediction) |
|---|---|
| Current events | Why did a recent international agreement on shipping emissions matter to India? |
| History | How did the events of 1857 change British administration in India? |
| Geography | Why does the Thar Desert receive so little rainfall? |
| Polity | What is the difference between a Money Bill and a Financial Bill? |
| Economy | How is poverty measured in India, and what are the limitations of the measure? |
| Environment | Why can an invasive species reduce local biodiversity? |
| General Science | Why does a pressure cooker cook food faster than an open vessel? |

If you cannot write a question for a bullet, that is useful information: it identifies a vocabulary gap, not a failure. The matching chapters in the table above tell you where to start.

## 5. How GS-I is scored

### 5.1 The rules

The official rules for the objective papers are in the [notice][notice], Appendix I, Section I, Note II, and Section II-A:

- Each question has **four alternatives**.
- A correct answer earns the marks assigned to that question.
- A wrong answer deducts **one-third of the marks assigned to that question**.
- If you mark **more than one answer**, it is treated as wrong, even if one of the marked answers is correct.
- If you leave a question **blank**, there is no penalty.

In recent GS-I papers, each question carries **2 marks** ([2024][pyq2024], [2025][pyq2025]). The penalty for a wrong answer is therefore:

**1/3 × 2 = 2/3 ≈ 0.67 mark**

The notice writes the deduction as “one-third (0.33)” of the question's marks. For a 2-mark question, that is the same rule as 2/3 mark, rounded in the notice's decimal example.

| Your response | Effect on score |
|---|---:|
| Correct | +2 |
| Wrong | −2/3 |
| Blank | 0 |
| More than one response | −2/3 (treated as wrong) |

### 5.2 The score formula

Let:

- **C** = number of correct answers
- **W** = number of wrong answers
- **U** = number of unattempted questions

Then:

**Net score = 2C − (2/3)W**

and **C + W + U = 100** in a 100-question paper. Unattempted questions contribute zero. The maximum is 200.

**Worked example 1.** You answer 58 correctly, 27 wrongly and leave 15 blank.

**2(58) − (2/3)(27) = 116 − 18 = 98**

Your practice net score is **98/200**. The 15 blanks do not reduce the score.

**Worked example 2.** You answer 70 correctly, 25 wrongly and leave 5 blank.

**2(70) − (2/3)(25) = 140 − 16.67 = 123.33**

Your practice net score is **123.33/200**. Keep the decimal rather than rounding it to 123; small differences matter when you compare attempts.

**Worked example 3.** Two different attempt profiles can produce the same net score:

- 62 correct, 21 wrong, 17 blank: **124 − 14 = 110**
- 65 correct, 30 wrong, 5 blank: **130 − 20 = 110**

The first profile is more accurate; the second attempts more questions. Both reach 110. This is why you should record correct, wrong and blank separately, not just the final score.

### 5.3 What negative marking really means

A correct answer earns 2 marks; a wrong answer costs 2/3. Therefore:

**2 ÷ (2/3) = 3**

**One correct answer pays for three wrong answers.** If you attempt every question, your net score is positive when **C > W/3**, which is an accuracy above 25%. That is a mathematical break-even point, not a competitive target. A score just above zero is far below the level needed for selection.

Negative marking is not a reason to avoid all attempts. It is a reason to attempt when you have evidence. The expected value of a guess depends on how many options you can eliminate:

| Situation | Probability of being correct | Expected marks per question |
|---|---:|---:|
| Blind guess among four options | 1/4 | (1/4)(2) + (3/4)(−2/3) = 0 |
| One option eliminated | 1/3 | (1/3)(2) + (2/3)(−2/3) = 2/9 ≈ +0.22 |
| Two options eliminated | 1/2 | (1/2)(2) + (1/2)(−2/3) = 2/3 ≈ +0.67 |
| No information; leave blank | 0 | 0 |

These are long-run averages, not guarantees. A blind guess and a blank have the same expected value, but the guess adds variance. An informed guess after genuine elimination has a positive expected value. The method for making elimination genuine is in Section 6; the full treatment is in Chapter 132.

### 5.4 What “cutoff” means

A **cutoff** is the minimum qualifying standard UPSC fixes for a stage in a particular year. For Prelims, it is the GS-I mark associated with the last candidate admitted to Mains in each category, once CSAT is cleared. It is published after the cycle, not in advance.

Two recent historical observations give a sense of scale:

| Year | General-category Prelims cutoff (GS-I, out of 200) |
|---|---:|
| 2024 | 87.98 |
| 2025 | 92.66 |

These figures are from UPSC's published cut-off notices ([CSE 2024 cut-off][cutoff2024]; [CSE 2025 cut-off][cutoff2025]). They are historical minima for those years, not targets, not forecasts and not a universal “safe score.” The cutoff can move from year to year, and category-wise cutoffs differ.

The CSAT threshold is different: it is **fixed at 33%**, or 66 out of 200. That is a qualifying rule, not a competitive cutoff.

## 6. Reading a GS-I question

### 6.1 The anatomy of an MCQ

A GS-I question usually contains:

1. **A stem** — the topic and the task.
2. **Qualifiers** — words that change what counts as a correct answer.
3. **Options** — four choices, sometimes built from numbered statements or pairs.
4. **One correct answer** — unless the paper explicitly says otherwise.

The most common failure is not a lack of facts. It is misreading the task. A candidate who knows the topic can still choose the wrong option because they missed “not,” “only,” or “incorrect.”

### 6.2 Common question formats

The 2024 paper illustrates several formats ([2024][pyq2024]):

| Format | Example from the corpus | What the task requires |
|---|---|---|
| Direct definition | [2024 Q3][pyq2024]: “A Writ of Prohibition is an order issued by the Supreme Court or High Courts to:” | Identify the correct definition or recipient. |
| Statement-based | [2024 Q6][pyq2024]: “With reference to the Government of India Act, 1935, consider the following statements...” | Decide which numbered statements are correct. |
| Statement I and Statement II | [2024 Q2][pyq2024]: two statements about the Indian Flying Fox, followed by options combining truth and explanation. | Evaluate each statement, then decide whether II explains I. |
| Counting | [2024 Q1][pyq2024]: “How many of the above are considered fifth generation fighter aircraft?” | Evaluate each listed item and count the true ones. |

Other formats include matching pairs, arranging events in order, and “which of the following is/are correct.” The reading method is the same: identify the task, locate the qualifiers, then evaluate the options against evidence.

### 6.3 Qualifiers: the words that change the answer

| Qualifier | What it does | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **only** | Excludes other possibilities | Check whether any other item also satisfies the condition. |
| **all** | Requires every item | One exception makes “all” false. |
| **correct** | Asks for the true statement(s) | Underline it; do not drift into “incorrect.” |
| **incorrect** | Reverses the task | Find the false statement, not the true one. |
| **not / except** | Asks for the exception | Mark the one option that does not fit. |
| **how many** | Asks for a count | Evaluate each item; the options are counts. |
| **best** | Asks for the most complete or accurate choice | A true but incomplete option may lose to a fuller one. |
| **with reference to** | Sets the context | Stay inside the named Act, institution or period. |
| **Statement-I / Statement-II** | Two separate claims | Evaluate truth separately; only then test the explanatory link. |
| **consider the following** | A list follows | Evaluate every listed item, even if an option mentions only some. |

### 6.4 Three worked parses (without answers)

These parses use 2024 questions. Do not try to answer them yet; the subject content belongs to later chapters. The aim is to see how the question is built.

**Parse 1 — [2024 Q2][pyq2024], Statement I and Statement II.**

- **Task:** Decide whether each statement is correct and, if both are correct, whether Statement-II explains Statement-I.
- **Qualifiers:** “correct” and “explains.”
- **Option structure:** (a) both correct and II explains I; (b) both correct but II does not explain I; (c) I correct, II incorrect; (d) I incorrect, II correct.
- **Reading strategy:** Judge Statement-I on its own. Judge Statement-II on its own. Only if both are correct do you need to test the explanatory link. A true Statement-II does not automatically explain Statement-I.

**Parse 2 — [2024 Q6][pyq2024], statement-based.**

- **Task:** Decide which of the numbered statements about the Government of India Act, 1935 are correct.
- **Qualifiers:** “1 only,” “2 only,” “both,” “neither.”
- **Reading strategy:** Evaluate statement 1; evaluate statement 2. Eliminate any option that requires a statement you have found false. If both are true, “both” is the answer; it is not a compromise choice.

**Parse 3 — [2024 Q1][pyq2024], counting.**

- **Task:** Count how many of the three listed aircraft are fifth-generation fighter aircraft.
- **Qualifiers:** “how many”; the options are “only one,” “only two,” “all three,” “none.”
- **Reading strategy:** Define the category first. Test each item against the definition. Count. Do not stop at the first true item, and do not assume the answer is “all” because the list is short.

### 6.5 Evidence-based elimination

Elimination is useful only when it is based on a reason you can state. A good reason identifies a specific defect in an option.

| Good reason to eliminate | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| Definition mismatch | The option gives a power to the wrong institution. |
| Scope mismatch | The option says “all states” when the rule applies only to some. |
| Date mismatch | The option places an event in the wrong year or decade. |
| Logical contradiction | Two parts of the option cannot both be true. |
| One half of a pair is wrong | In a matching question, one pair is mismatched. |
| Quantifier error | “Only” or “all” is false because of an exception. |

| Weak reason to eliminate | Why it is unreliable |
|---|---|
| “The option is long.” | Length is not evidence. |
| “The word is unfamiliar.” | Unfamiliarity is not falsity. |
| “It is option (c); (c) is rarely correct.” | Option order carries no information. |
| “It sounds like a government scheme.” | Plausibility is not proof. |
| “I have never heard of it.” | Your reading has gaps; the syllabus is broad. |

The most important distinction is between **unknown** and **wrong**. If you cannot verify a statement, mark it “unknown.” Do not eliminate it as if it were false. You can still eliminate options that require a statement you have positively disproved.

For example, in a question with options “1 only / 2 only / both / neither,” suppose you establish that statement 2 is false. You can eliminate “2 only” and “both” immediately, even if statement 1 is still unknown. You have reduced the choice from four options to two without pretending to know more than you do. If you later establish statement 1, choose accordingly; if it remains unknown, decide whether to attempt based on your confidence and the scoring rules.

### 6.6 Answer the question that is asked

Prelims questions often include a context that is wider than the task. A question “with reference to the Government of India Act, 1935” wants the position under that Act, not the position under the present Constitution. A question about “the Indian Flying Fox” wants the stated legal and biological claims, not a general essay on bats.

Before choosing, restate the task in one sentence: “I must decide whether statement 2 is true and whether it explains statement 1.” If your restatement does not match the stem, you have misread the question.

### Part F — Calculate a sample practice score

Suppose a practice paper has 100 questions of 2 marks each. A learner answers 62 correctly, 21 wrongly and leaves 17 blank.

1. Calculate the net score.
2. Find a second profile with a different number of correct and wrong answers that produces the same net score.
3. Explain why the two profiles can have the same score.

<details>
<summary>Show answers and reasoning</summary>

1. 2(62) − (2/3)(21) = 124 − 14 = 110. Net score: **110/200**.
2. One example is 65 correct, 30 wrong and 5 blank: 130 − 20 = 110. Another is 59 correct, 12 wrong and 29 blank: 118 − 8 = 110. Many profiles are possible.
3. The score depends on both accuracy and the number of attempts. A more accurate learner can attempt fewer questions and still match a less accurate learner who attempts more. This is why a single net score does not tell you whether to improve accuracy or attempt more; you need the correct, wrong and blank counts.

</details>

The baseline is now complete. It cannot be converted into a Prelims score, because it is not a 100-question timed paper. Its value is the list of skills it reveals.

The GS-I curriculum is organised into ten phases. This is the map, not a deadline schedule.

| Phase | Chapters | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| 0. Orientation and bridges | 001–006 | Exam, study habits, maps, chronology, numbers, basic science |

| 5. Environment | 099–109 | Ecosystems, biodiversity, conservation, pollution, climate |
| 6. Science and technology | 110–128 | Everyday science, health, biotechnology, energy, space, digital systems |
| 7. Current-affairs consolidation | 129–131 | National, international and annual updates |
| 8. Applied PYQ work | 132–134 | Question formats, cross-subject links, thematic workshops |
| 9. Revision and exam readiness | 135–136 | Error repair, timed practice, final update check |

A workable loop for each chapter:

1. Read the curriculum entry for scope, outcome and prerequisites.
2. Read the chapter and explain the mechanism in ordinary language without looking.
3. Make one compact map, timeline or comparison table.
4. Attempt the cited PYQs untimed, writing the reason for each answer before checking the key.
5. Record why each rejected option fails.
6. Revisit the errors after a gap.

The PYQ corpus covers 2013–2026, but it is not flawless. Each year file records its source, access date, question count and caveats; some recent files have missing questions. Use the corpus for calibration and practice, not as an infallible source of official wording or answers. If a key conflicts with a worked solution, record the conflict and check the official booklet rather than silently learning an unexplained answer.

Tests are generated only on request. A chapter-part test requires substantive authored teaching content; a full-length mock follows the official paper pattern independently of chapter completion. A practice score is not an official mark or a predicted cutoff.

Current affairs starts in Chapter 002 and continues throughout. It is not a separate subject to be postponed until Phase 7; Phase 7 consolidates a habit that should already exist.

<a id="9-common-beginner-mistakes"></a>

## Common confusions

| Mistake | Correction |
|---|---|
| Treating Prelims as the whole examination | Prelims is a screening stage. Mains and the Interview decide the final rank. |
| Thinking GS-I means “current affairs only” | The syllabus has seven areas. Current events is one bullet and also a lens on the others. |
| Ignoring CSAT because GS-I is the scoring paper | CSAT is compulsory and qualifying. A score below 66 ends the attempt. |
| Chasing a fixed “safe score” | The GS-I cutoff varies by year and category. No fixed percentage is promised. |
| Attempting every question because “more attempts means more marks” | A wrong answer costs one-third. Net score depends on accuracy as well as attempts. |
| Refusing to guess at all | Blind guessing has zero expected value, but an informed guess after elimination has positive expected value. |
| Reading news without the syllabus | Link each item to a syllabus concept and a dated source. |
| Checking answer keys without analysing wrong options | The learning is in why the other options fail. |
| Treating a coaching weightage table as an official blueprint | It is an analysis, not a UPSC document. |
| Memorising facts before learning to read the question | Prelims rewards precise discrimination, not recitation alone. |

## 1. Why maps and time belong together

A map answers **where**. A timeline answers **when**. Prelims questions often need both: a place named in the news, a historical event tied to a region, a boundary that changed, a date that decides the order of events. If either skill is weak, the question becomes guesswork.

This is a method chapter. It teaches how to read a map and how to handle dates. It does not replace the geography and history chapters, which teach the actual content. The aim is to make you fluent in four operations:

1. Read a map's **scale**, **legend**, **direction** and **coordinates**.
2. Locate a place on a blank map or from a pair of coordinates.
3. Place a date in the correct **century** and order events on a timeline.
4. Record both the **where** and the **when** when you study a news item or a historical topic.

A beginner does not need to memorise every place and date. A beginner needs a reliable method for finding, checking and organising them.

## 2. A map is a selected, simplified picture

A map is not a photograph. It is a **selective, symbolised and generalised representation** of the whole or part of the Earth at a reduced scale ([NCERT, *Practical Work in Geography*, Chapter 1][ncert-maps]). That definition contains three useful ideas:

- **Selective:** the mapmaker chooses what to show. A road map may omit soil types; a soil map may omit roads.
- **Symbolised:** features are shown by signs, colours and lines whose meanings are given in the legend.
- **Generalised:** detail is simplified to suit the scale and purpose. A small-scale map cannot show every house.

A network of lines and shapes without a scale is a **sketch**, not a map. Scale is what makes a map measurable.

### 2.1 The five things to check first

Before answering a map question, check:

| Element | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| **Title** | What area and theme does the map show? |
| **Scale** | How much ground does one centimetre represent? |
| **Legend/key** | What do the colours, lines and symbols mean? |
| **Orientation** | Where is north? Is there a north arrow? |
| **Source and date** | Who made the map, and when? Are the borders current? |

The last item matters especially for historical maps. A map of 1947 Europe and a map of 2026 Europe show different countries. Always use the map's own date, not today's.

### 2.2 Large-scale and small-scale maps

| Type | Shows | Detail | Typical example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Large-scale** | A small area | More detail | Village map, city plan, topographical sheet |
| **Small-scale** | A large area | Less detail | Atlas map, wall map, world map |

A large-scale map has a **smaller denominator** in its representative fraction. For example, 1:25,000 is a larger scale than 1:250,000, because one centimetre on the first map represents only 250 metres on the ground, while one centimetre on the second represents 2.5 kilometres ([NCERT, Chapter 1][ncert-maps]; [NCERT, Chapter 2][ncert-scale]).

## 3. Directions and bearings

The four **cardinal points** are north (N), south (S), east (E) and west (W). Between them are the intermediate directions: north-east (NE), south-east (SE), south-west (SW) and north-west (NW). Together they make an eight-point compass ([NCERT, Chapter 1][ncert-maps]).

### 3.1 Read the north arrow

Most maps place north at the top, but not all do. A map may be rotated to fit a page or to follow a route. Before using "up", "down", "left" or "right", find the **north arrow** or the north line. If the map is rotated, "up" may be north-east or south.

A **bearing** is an angle measured clockwise from north:

| Direction | Bearing |
|---|---|
| North | 0° (or 360°) |
| East | 90° |
| South | 180° |
| West | 270° |

Bearings are useful because they avoid vague words like "left" and "right". A bearing is fixed to the map, not to the reader's body.

### 3.2 Relative direction

Direction depends on the starting point.

- If A is west of B, then B is east of A.
- If A is south of B, then B is north of A.

A common exam trap is to give the direction from the wrong end. Read the question carefully: "from A to B" is not the same as "from B to A".

**Worked example.** A is 3 cm west of B on a map with north at the top. What is the bearing from A to B? From B to A?

- From A to B: east, bearing 90°.
- From B to A: west, bearing 270°.

**Check.** If you walk from A to B, you go east. If you walk back, you go west. The two bearings differ by 180°.

## 4. Scale: turning map distance into ground distance

**Scale** is the relationship between a distance on the map and the corresponding distance on the ground. It is the single most important number on a map. Without it, you can describe shapes but not measure distances ([NCERT, Chapter 2][ncert-scale]).

### 4.1 Three ways to write a scale

| Form | Example | What it means | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Statement** | 1 cm = 50 km | One centimetre on the map represents 50 kilometres on the ground | Becomes invalid if the map is enlarged or reduced |
| **Representative fraction (RF)** | 1:50,000 | One unit on the map represents 50,000 of the same units on the ground | Needs conversion for everyday units |
| **Graphical or bar scale** | A line marked 0, 10, 20, 30 km | A ruler printed on the map | Must be read carefully; remains valid when the map is resized |

The **graphical scale** has a practical advantage: if the whole map is enlarged or reduced, the scale bar changes with it and remains valid. A statement scale does not: if a map with "1 cm = 50 km" is enlarged, one centimetre no longer represents 50 kilometres ([NCERT, Chapter 2][ncert-scale]).

### 4.2 Converting a representative fraction

An RF such as 1:50,000 is unit-independent. It means:

- 1 cm on the map = 50,000 cm on the ground.
- 50,000 cm = 500 m = 0.5 km.

So 1:50,000 is the same as "1 cm = 0.5 km".

Useful conversions:

- 1 m = 100 cm.
- 1 km = 1,000 m = 100,000 cm.
- To turn an RF into a statement in kilometres, divide the denominator by 100,000.

**Worked example 1.** Convert 1:250,000 into a statement scale in kilometres.

- 1:250,000 means 1 cm on the map = 250,000 cm on the ground.
- 250,000 cm ÷ 100,000 = 2.5 km.
- Answer: **1 cm = 2.5 km**.

**Worked example 2.** A map has a scale of 1 cm = 50 km. Two towns are 4.2 cm apart on the map. What is the ground distance?

- Ground distance = 4.2 × 50 = **210 km**.

**Worked example 3.** A map has an RF of 1:50,000. Two points are 3 cm apart. What is the ground distance?

- 1 cm = 0.5 km.
- 3 cm = 3 × 0.5 = **1.5 km**.

**Worked example 4.** Which is the larger scale: 1:25,000 or 1:250,000?

- 1:25,000 means 1 cm = 0.25 km.
- 1:250,000 means 1 cm = 2.5 km.
- The first map shows a smaller area in more detail, so **1:25,000 is the larger scale**.

**Worked example 5.** Convert 1 cm = 50 km into a representative fraction.

- Write both sides in the same unit: 50 km = 50 × 100,000 cm = 5,000,000 cm.
- 1 cm : 5,000,000 cm = **1:5,000,000**.

### 4.3 Measuring a curved route

A straight line can be measured with a ruler. A river, road or coastline is usually curved. Use one of these methods:

- **Thread:** lay a thread along the curve, mark the ends, then straighten the thread and measure it against the scale.
- **Dividers:** step the dividers along the curve in short straight segments, then add the segments.
- **Graphical scale:** compare the measured length directly with the scale bar.

The result is an approximation. State that it is approximate, and do not report more precision than the map allows.

### 4.4 Scale traps

- **Statement versus RF:** "1 cm = 50 km" and "1:50,000" are not the same. The first is a small-scale map; the second is a large-scale map.
- **Units:** convert centimetres to metres or kilometres before multiplying.
- **Enlargement:** a statement scale is invalid after resizing; a graphical scale is not.
- **Area:** if a map is enlarged, distances scale by the enlargement factor, but areas scale by the square of that factor. Chapter 004 treats area and percentage change separately.

## 5. Legend, symbols and map types

The **legend** (or key) explains the map's symbols. A legend may show:

- **Point symbols:** a triangle for a peak, a dot for a town, a circle for a city.
- **Line symbols:** a blue line for a river, a dashed line for a boundary, a double line for a railway.
- **Area symbols:** green for forest, yellow for cultivated land, blue for water.
- **Lettering:** names of places, regions and physical features.

Never assume a symbol's meaning from its shape alone. A red line may mean a road on one map and a boundary on another. Read the legend.

**Worked example.** A legend shows a dashed line as a state boundary, a blue line as a river, and a triangle as a peak. What does a dashed line represent? **A state boundary.** The answer comes from the legend, not from the fact that the line is dashed.

### 5.1 Common map types

| Map type | Main purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical** | Shows relief, rivers, seas and natural features | A map of the Himalayas |
| **Political** | Shows boundaries and administrative units | A map of Indian states |
| **Thematic** | Shows one theme, such as rainfall, soil or population | A rainfall map |
| **Topographical** | Shows detailed relief and cultural features at a large scale | A Survey of India topographical sheet |
| **Cadastral** | Shows property boundaries at a very large scale | A village land map |

For Prelims, the most common formats are political maps, physical maps, thematic maps and place-in-news maps. Topographical maps are treated in later geography chapters; here, the priority is the basic reading skill.

## 6. Coordinates: latitude and longitude

A **coordinate** is a pair of numbers that fixes a point on the Earth's surface. The two numbers are **latitude** and **longitude**. Together they form the geographical grid ([NCERT, Chapter 3][ncert-time]).

### 6.1 Latitude

**Latitude** is the angular distance of a point north or south of the equator, measured in degrees from the centre of the Earth.

- The **equator** is 0° latitude.
- The **North Pole** is 90°N; the **South Pole** is 90°S.
- Lines of latitude are called **parallels**. They run east–west and are parallel to one another.
- Latitude is written with **N** or **S**: 28.6°N means 28.6 degrees north of the equator.

### 6.2 Longitude

**Longitude** is the angular distance of a point east or west of the **prime meridian**, measured in degrees.

- The **prime meridian** is 0° longitude and passes through Greenwich, London.
- Longitude runs from 0° to 180° east and 0° to 180° west.
- Lines of longitude are called **meridians**. They run north–south and converge at the poles.
- Longitude is written with **E** or **W**: 77.2°E means 77.2 degrees east of Greenwich.

### 6.3 Writing and reading coordinates

By convention, **latitude comes first**, then longitude:

> 28.6°N, 77.2°E

This means 28.6 degrees north of the equator and 77.2 degrees east of the prime meridian. Reversing the order is a common mistake and puts the point in the wrong place.

Each degree is divided into **60 minutes** (′), and each minute into **60 seconds** (″):

- 1° = 60′.
- 1′ = 60″.

A coordinate such as 28°36′N is the same as 28.6°N (because 36′ = 0.6°).

To locate a point on a map:

1. Find the latitude line or the position between two latitude lines.
2. Find the longitude line or the position between two longitude lines.
3. Mark the intersection.
4. Check the N/S and E/W labels.

### 6.4 Distance and the convergence of meridians

The spacing of the grid is not uniform in kilometres:

- The distance between two latitudes is approximately **111 km** ([NCERT, Chapter 3][ncert-time]).
- The distance between two longitudes is **maximum at the equator (about 111.3 km)** and **minimum at the poles (0 km)**. At 45° latitude it is about **79 km** ([NCERT, Chapter 3][ncert-time]).

Why? Lines of latitude are parallel, so the gap between them stays roughly constant. Lines of longitude converge at the poles, so the same angular gap represents a smaller ground distance as you move away from the equator.

**Worked example.** Two points lie on the same meridian, 5° apart in latitude. What is the approximate ground distance?

- 5 × 111 = **555 km**.

**Worked example.** Two points lie on the equator, 10° apart in longitude. What is the approximate ground distance?

- 10 × 111.3 ≈ **1,113 km**.

The same 10° gap at 45° latitude is about 10 × 79 = **790 km**. The angular gap is the same; the ground distance is not.

### 6.5 Coordinates and time

Longitude also determines local time. The Earth rotates from west to east through 360° in 24 hours:

- 360° ÷ 24 = **15° per hour**.
- 15° ÷ 60 = **1° in 4 minutes**.

Places to the **east** see the sun earlier, so their local time is **ahead**. Places to the **west** are **behind** ([NCERT, Chapter 3][ncert-time]).

**Worked example.** A place is 30° east of the prime meridian. When it is 12:00 at the prime meridian, what is the local solar time at the place?

- 30° × 4 minutes = 120 minutes = 2 hours.
- The place is east, so it is ahead: **14:00**.

**Worked example.** A place is 90° west of the prime meridian. When it is 12:00 at the prime meridian, what is the local solar time?

- 90° × 4 minutes = 360 minutes = 6 hours.
- The place is west, so it is behind: **06:00**.

### 6.6 Local time and standard time

**Local solar time** changes continuously as you move east or west. A country usually chooses a **standard meridian** and uses its local time across a whole region, so that clocks agree.

India's standard meridian is **82°30′E**, passing near Mirzapur. Indian Standard Time (IST) is **UTC+5:30** ([NCERT, Chapter 3][ncert-time]).

- When it is 12:00 UTC, IST is **17:30**.
- A place at 90°E has a local solar time 30 minutes ahead of IST, because 90°E is 7.5° east of 82°30′E: 7.5 × 4 = 30 minutes.
- But the clock at that place still shows IST unless it is officially in another time zone.

This distinction between **local solar time** and **standard time** is a common source of errors.

### 6.7 The International Date Line

The **International Date Line** is approximately the **180° meridian**. It marks the place where the date changes.

- Travelling **eastward** across the line, **subtract one day**: Tuesday becomes Monday.
- Travelling **westward** across the line, **add one day**: Tuesday becomes Wednesday.

The 180° meridian is only an approximation ([NCERT, Chapter 3][ncert-time]).

**Check.** The direction is easy to reverse. Anchor it with a concrete route: flying east from Asia toward the Americas, you cross the line and the date goes back one day. Flying west from the Americas toward Asia, the date goes forward one day.

## 7. Blank maps and map matching

A **blank map** is an outline map with few or no labels. It is a recall tool: you supply the names, boundaries and features from memory, then check an atlas.

### 7.1 A blank-map routine

1. **Start with the grid.** Mark the equator, the Tropic of Cancer, the Tropic of Capricorn, the prime meridian and, for India, the standard meridian.
2. **Add the large shapes.** Coastlines, major mountain ranges, major rivers, seas and neighbouring countries.
3. **Add political units.** States, countries or regions, using the map's date.
4. **Add the places in the news.** Mark each place, then write its coordinates or its position relative to a known feature.
5. **Check and correct.** Compare with an atlas. Mark errors in a different colour and revisit them after a gap.

The order matters. If you start with a random list of places, you will memorise isolated dots. If you start with the grid and the large shapes, each new place has an anchor.

### 7.2 Map matching

Map-matching questions ask you to pair a feature with a larger unit:

- a region with its country;
- a reservoir with its state;
- a city with its river or coast;
- a pass with its mountain range.

The method is always the same:

1. **Locate the feature**, not just the name. A similar-sounding name can belong to a different country.
2. **Use an atlas index** or a gazetteer to check.
3. **Eliminate** pairs you know are wrong.
4. **Do not guess from language or spelling.** Names can be transliterated in different ways.

The 2022 paper included a region–country matching question and a reservoir–state matching question ([2022 Q71, Q76][pyq2022]). The exact pairs and answers are in the corpus; the skill to practise is locating each feature on a map, not memorising the pairs.

## 8. Historical time: BCE, CE and centuries

Historical dates use a numbering system with a **BCE/CE** convention:

- **BCE** means *Before Common Era*.
- **CE** means *Common Era*.
- They are numerically equivalent to **BC** and **AD**: 1947 CE is the same year as 1947 AD; 300 BCE is the same year as 300 BC ([NCERT, *Exploring Society: India and Beyond*, Chapter 4][ncert-timeline]).

The BCE/CE form is used here because it is the neutral convention in modern scholarship.

### 8.1 There is no year zero

The year **1 BCE** is followed immediately by **1 CE**. There is no year 0 in the BCE/CE system ([NCERT, Chapter 4][ncert-timeline]; [NASA, *Year Dating Conventions*][nasa-dates]).

This single fact explains most chronology errors. A timeline that places 0 between 1 BCE and 1 CE is wrong. Astronomers use a year zero in their own calculations, but that is a separate convention; do not import it into historical dates.

### 8.2 Which century?

A **century** is a period of 100 years. Centuries are counted from year 1, not year 0.

| Period | Years |
|---|---|
| 1st century CE | 1–100 CE |
| 2nd century CE | 101–200 CE |
| 19th century CE | 1801–1900 CE |
| 20th century CE | 1901–2000 CE |
| 21st century CE | 2001–2100 CE |
| 1st century BCE | 100–1 BCE |
| 2nd century BCE | 200–101 BCE |
| 3rd century BCE | 300–201 BCE |
| 4th century BCE | 400–301 BCE |

([NCERT, Chapter 4][ncert-timeline])

Two consequences are easy to miss:

- **2000 CE is in the 20th century**, because the 20th century ends in 2000. The 21st century begins in 2001.
- **300 BCE is in the 3rd century BCE**, because the 3rd century BCE runs from 300 BCE to 201 BCE. The 4th century BCE begins at 301 BCE.

A quick rule for a three- or four-digit year: add 1 to the number formed by all but the last two digits, unless the last two digits are 00, in which case do not add 1. For 1947: 19 + 1 = 20th. For 2000: 20th. For 2001: 21st. For a BCE year, use the same rule and add the label BCE: 300 BCE → 3rd century BCE; 301 BCE → 4th century BCE.

### 8.3 Measuring an interval

- **Within BCE:** subtract the smaller number from the larger. 500 BCE to 300 BCE = 200 years.
- **Within CE:** subtract the earlier year from the later year. 1857 to 1947 = 90 years.
- **Across BCE and CE:** add the two numbers and subtract 1, because there is no year zero. 300 BCE to 200 CE = 300 + 200 − 1 = **499 years**. 500 BCE to 500 CE = 500 + 500 − 1 = **999 years** ([NCERT, Chapter 4][ncert-timeline]).

**Check.** From 1 BCE to 1 CE is one year, not two. The subtraction of 1 fixes the missing year zero.

### 8.4 Related terms

- **Decade:** 10 years.
- **Century:** 100 years.
- **Millennium:** 1,000 years.
- **Circa (c. or ca.):** approximately. Use it when a date is uncertain or when historians give a range.
- **BP:** before present. This is a different convention used in archaeology and geology; do not mix it with BCE/CE without checking the source.

## 9. Ordering events and building timelines

A **timeline** is a diagram that places events in order. It is not a measured scale; equal spacing on the page does not always mean equal intervals of time. The order is the point.

### 9.1 A four-step method

1. **List the events with their dates.** If a date is a range, use the start, the end or the midpoint and say which.
2. **Convert the dates to a common form.** Keep BCE and CE labels visible.
3. **Place them on a line.** BCE goes to the left of the BCE/CE boundary; CE goes to the right. Put 1 BCE immediately next to 1 CE.
4. **Check the order.** For BCE, a larger number is earlier. For CE, a larger number is later.

### 9.2 Worked example: the 2017 chronology question

The 2017 paper asked you to order three freedom-struggle events ([2017 Q20][pyq2017]):

1. Mutiny in the Royal Indian Navy.
2. Quit India Movement.
3. Second Round Table Conference.

The dates are:

- Second Round Table Conference: **1931** ([Britannica, *Round Table Conference*][britannica-rtc]).
- Quit India Movement: **1942** ([Britannica, *Quit India Movement*][britannica-quit]).
- Royal Indian Navy mutiny: **February 1946** ([UK Parliament, Hansard, 22 February 1946][hansard-rin]).

Placing them on a timeline gives 1931 → 1942 → 1946, which is **3 → 2 → 1**. The answer is option (c) in the corpus.

The method matters more than the answer. If you know the approximate order of the three events, you can answer even if you have forgotten one exact year. If you know only isolated years, you may still mis-order them under exam pressure.

### 9.3 Another ordering example

Place these in chronological order ([NCERT, Chapter 4][ncert-timeline]):

- Alexander's invasion of the north-west: c. 327–325 BCE.
- Ashoka's reign: c. 268–231 BCE.
- Establishment of the Gupta empire: c. 320 CE.
- Harshavardhana's rule: 606–647 CE.

Because all the BCE dates come before all the CE dates, the order is:

1. Alexander (327–325 BCE).
2. Ashoka (268–231 BCE).
3. Gupta empire (320 CE).
4. Harshavardhana (606–647 CE).

Notice that 327 BCE is earlier than 268 BCE even though 327 is the larger number. For BCE, the larger number is earlier.

### 9.4 Period labels are not dates

Labels such as **ancient**, **medieval**, **early medieval**, **modern** and **colonial** are convenient, but their boundaries vary by region and by historian. A process such as the growth of a city, the spread of a religion or the development of a language may span several centuries and does not begin on a single date ([NCERT, *Our Pasts III*, Chapter 1][ncert-periodisation]).

Use period labels for orientation, then anchor the event with a date or a date range. If a question uses a period label, check what the question means by it rather than assuming a universal definition.

## 10. Maps and time together

A historical map and a timeline answer different parts of the same question. When you study an event, record both:

| Question | Tool | What to record |
|---|---|---|
| Where did it happen? | Map | Place, region, country, coordinates if useful |
| When did it happen? | Timeline | Date, century, order relative to other events |
| What changed? | Historical map | Borders, names, routes and settlements at the time |
| Why does it matter? | Syllabus link | The concept or theme the event illustrates |

A historical map must be read with its own date. Borders and place names change; a city that is in one country today may have been in another in 1900. If a map has no date, treat its political information as uncertain.

For current affairs, the same habit applies. When you read about an event, write one line for the place and one line for the date. That is the **source/date ledger** from [Chapter 002](002-learning-notes-and-a-first-newspaper-routine.md), applied to geography and history. A news item without a location and a date is hard to connect to the syllabus.

## 11. Common mistakes

| Mistake | Correction |
|---|---|
| Measuring map distance without using the scale | Multiply by the scale; convert units first. |
| Confusing "1 cm = 50 km" with "1:50,000" | The first is small-scale; the second is large-scale. |
| Assuming north is always at the top | Look for the north arrow. |
| Reversing latitude and longitude, or attaching N/S to the wrong number | Write latitude (N/S) first, longitude (E/W) second. |
| Treating 1° of longitude as 111 km everywhere | It shrinks toward the poles; only the equator is about 111.3 km. |
| Mixing local solar time with standard time | Local time varies with longitude; standard time uses a chosen meridian. |
| Crossing the International Date Line in the wrong direction | Eastward: subtract a day. Westward: add a day. |
| Counting 2000 as the 21st century | 2000 is in the 20th century; 2001 begins the 21st. |
| Treating 300 BCE as the 4th century BCE | 300 BCE is in the 3rd century BCE. |
| Ordering BCE dates from small to large | For BCE, the larger number is earlier. |
| Adding BCE and CE years without subtracting 1 | There is no year zero; subtract 1 across the boundary. |
| Relying on a period label without a date | Period labels vary; anchor the event with a date or range. |
| Using a historical map as if it were current | Check the map's date and read its borders accordingly. |

5. **25°N, 80°E**: 25° north of the equator; 80° east of the prime meridian. The convention is latitude first, longitude second.
6. 5 × 111 = **555 km** (approximately).
7. 30° × 4 minutes = 120 minutes = 2 hours. The place is east, so it is ahead: **14:00 local solar time**.
8. 1857 CE: **19th century**. 300 BCE: **3rd century BCE**. 2000 CE: **20th century**. 2001 CE: **21st century**.
9. 500 + 500 − 1 = **999 years**.
10. **Alexander (327–325 BCE) → Ashoka (268–231 BCE) → Gupta empire (320 CE) → Harshavardhana (606–647 CE).**
11. **C**.

</details>

The answers are models, not the only acceptable wording. For scale and time questions, show the multiplication or division so that you can check your own reasoning.

## 13. Readiness check

You are ready to move to Chapter 004 when you can:

- identify a map's title, scale, legend, orientation and date before answering;
- convert a statement scale to a representative fraction and back;
- measure a straight or curved distance and state that it is approximate;
- use cardinal and intermediate directions and bearings correctly;
- write a coordinate with latitude first and longitude second;
- explain why 1° of longitude is not a constant distance on the ground;
- calculate a simple time difference from longitude and distinguish local time from standard time;
- state the century for a CE or BCE year and calculate an interval across the BCE/CE boundary;
- order events on a timeline, including BCE dates;
- locate a feature on a blank map and check it against an atlas.

If one of these is weak, repeat the relevant section with a different map or a different set of dates. The skills improve with practice, not with memorising a list of facts.

## 1. Orientation

You cannot see Earth's interior, yet two things let us study it: the behaviour of seismic waves generated by earthquakes and the rocks that plate tectonics brings to the surface or melts and re-erupts. This chapter connects the inside to the outside — from the structure of the core to the fold mountains and volcanoes you can visit. It also anchors Chapter 009 (weathering begins at the surface of rocks that formed deep or at plate boundaries) and Chapter 015 (India's physical landscape makes sense only once you know its plate history).

For the exam the chapter rewards process understanding over memorisation. Knowing *why* P waves arrive before S waves lets you answer a statement about seismographs; knowing *what* happens at a subduction zone tells you which rocks form there, which landforms appear and which hazards to expect — all from a single mechanism.

## 3. How we know what is inside the Earth

Earth's radius is about 6,370 km. The deepest mine (South African gold mines) reaches barely 4 km and the deepest borehole (Kola, Russia) about 12 km. Everything below that is inaccessible, so direct sampling is very limited.

**Direct sources of information:**
- Surface rock outcrops, mines and quarries reveal the uppermost crust.
- Drilling projects (Deep Ocean Drilling Project; Integrated Ocean Drilling Project) sample ocean-floor sediments and basalt.
- Volcanic eruptions bring magma from the upper mantle to the surface, though the exact depth of its source is hard to pin down.

**Indirect sources:**
- **Seismic waves** are the most powerful tool. When an earthquake occurs, waves travel through the planet and are recorded at distant seismograph stations. The speed, direction and arrival time of these waves change as they pass through materials of different density, state (solid vs. liquid) and composition. By studying these changes, scientists can map layers they have never touched.
- **Temperature, pressure and density** all increase with depth. Pressure (the weight of overlying material) increases steadily; temperature rises rapidly in the crust and more slowly in the lower mantle.
- **Gravity** measurements vary slightly across the surface; denser material beneath the surface pulls harder, allowing inferences about composition.
- **Meteorites** composed of iron, nickel and silicate minerals are thought to resemble early planetary material, giving clues about Earth's bulk composition.
- **Earth's magnetic field**, generated by convection in the liquid outer core, confirms that the outer core is both liquid and conducting.

## 4. Earth's interior: layers and boundaries

### 4.1 The three layers

Earth is divided into three main layers by composition:

**Crust**

The crust is the outermost, thinnest layer — less than 1 % of Earth's volume ([NCERT Ch. 3][ncert-int]).

| Property | Continental crust | Oceanic crust |
|---|---|---|
| Thickness | ~30 km average; up to 70 km under the Himalayas | ~5–10 km |
| Composition | Rich in silica and aluminium — called **Sial** | Rich in silica and magnesium — called **Sima** |
| Density | Lower (~2.7 g/cm³) | Higher (~3.0 g/cm³) |
| Rock type | Granite dominant | Basalt dominant |
| Age | Very old (up to ~4 billion years) | Relatively young (rarely >200 million years) |

The denser oceanic crust sits lower; the lighter continental crust rides higher — which is why continents stand above sea level and ocean basins sit below ([NCERT Ch. 3][ncert-int]).

**Mantle**

The mantle extends from the base of the crust to about 2,900 km depth and makes up about 83 % of Earth's volume and 67 % of its mass. It is composed mainly of iron and magnesium silicate minerals.

The upper part of the mantle, together with the crust above it, forms the rigid **lithosphere** (from Greek *lithos*, rock). Below the lithosphere, from about 100 km to 350 km depth, lies the **asthenosphere** — a zone of predominantly solid rock that can deform and flow slowly over geological time; small melt fractions occur in some settings ([USGS][usgs-mantle]). This distinction between the rigid lithosphere and the plastic asthenosphere is fundamental to plate tectonics.

**Core**

The core extends from about 2,900 km to Earth's centre (~6,370 km). It is composed mainly of iron and nickel — called **Nife**. The core is divided into:

- **Outer core** (2,900–5,100 km): liquid. S-waves cannot travel through it, confirming its fluid state. Convection currents in this liquid iron–nickel layer generate Earth's magnetic field.
- **Inner core** (5,100–6,370 km): solid, despite the very high temperature (~5,000–6,000 °C), because the extreme pressure compresses the iron–nickel into a crystalline solid.

### 4.2 Discontinuities — the layer boundaries

Scientists named the layer boundaries after the researchers who identified them through wave behaviour:

| Discontinuity | Depth | Separates |
|---|---|---|
| **Mohorovičić (Moho)** | ~35 km under continents; ~5–10 km under oceans | Crust from mantle |
| **Gutenberg** | ~2,900 km | Mantle from outer core |
| **Lehmann** | ~5,100 km | Outer core from inner core |

At each discontinuity, seismic wave speeds change abruptly, which is how the boundary is detected.

## 5. Seismic waves — reading the planet with earthquakes

An earthquake releases energy as waves from a point inside the earth called the **focus** (or **hypocenter**). The point on the surface directly above the focus is the **epicentre**. Three types of waves travel outward:

### 5.1 Body waves (travel through Earth's interior)

**P waves (Primary waves)**
- Travel the fastest — they arrive first at any seismograph station.
- Travel through solids, liquids and gases.
- Particle motion: the material compresses and expands in the same direction as wave travel (a push–pull motion). Imagine a spring being squeezed and stretched along its length.
- Because they can pass through the liquid outer core (though they slow down there), P waves arrive at seismograph stations all around the world.

**S waves (Secondary waves)**
- Slower than P waves — arrive second.
- Travel only through solids. They cannot travel through liquids (the outer core blocks them).
- Particle motion: the material moves at right angles to the direction of wave travel (a shake motion, like a rope being waved up and down).
- The fact that S waves cannot pass through the outer core is the main evidence that the outer core is liquid.

> **[2023 Q96][pyq2023] (Answer: c):** "In a seismograph, P waves are recorded earlier than S waves" — TRUE. "In P waves, particles vibrate to and fro in the direction of propagation whereas in S waves particles vibrate at right angles" — TRUE. Both statements are correct.

### 5.2 Surface waves (travel along Earth's surface)

**L waves (Love and Rayleigh waves)**
- Travel only along Earth's surface.
- Slower than body waves.
- Cause the most damage in an earthquake — their back-and-forth and rolling motion shakes structures, opens cracks and triggers landslides.

### 5.3 The shadow zone

When you map which parts of the world receive P waves and S waves from a given earthquake, you find:

- A **P-wave shadow zone** (roughly 103°–142° from the epicentre) where P waves arrive only weakly or not at all.
- An **S-wave shadow zone** (beyond ~103°) where S waves do not arrive at all.

The S-wave shadow zone exists because S waves cannot cross the liquid outer core. The P-wave shadow zone results from P waves being refracted (bent) as they enter and exit the denser core. These zones are the clearest evidence for the core's existence and its liquid outer portion.

## 6. Minerals and rocks

### 6.1 What is a mineral?

A **mineral** is a naturally occurring, homogeneous substance with a definite chemical composition and an orderly atomic structure (crystalline). Rocks are aggregates of one or more minerals; unlike minerals, rocks have no fixed chemical composition ([NCERT Ch. 5][ncert-rocks]).

Minerals are identified by properties including crystal form, cleavage, hardness (Mohs scale 1–10, from talc to diamond), lustre, colour and streak.

### 6.2 Rock families

**Igneous rocks** — formed by cooling and solidification of magma or lava. Called *primary rocks* because the other two families derive from them.

| Type | Where formed | Cooling speed | Texture | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Intrusive (plutonic)** | Deep underground | Slow | Coarse-grained (crystals visible) | Granite, gabbro, diorite |
| **Extrusive (volcanic)** | At or near the surface | Rapid | Fine-grained or glassy | Basalt, obsidian, pumice, tuff |

India's Deccan Traps is one of Earth's largest basalt provinces — an extrusive igneous formation.

**Sedimentary rocks** — formed from sediments (fragments of older rocks, mineral precipitates or organic remains) deposited in layers, compacted and cemented. This process is called **lithification**.

| Sub-type | How formed | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Mechanically formed (clastic)** | Fragments transported and deposited by water, wind or ice | Sandstone, shale, conglomerate, loess |
| **Chemically formed** | Mineral precipitation from solution | Limestone (from CaCO₃), rock salt (halite), gypsum, chert |
| **Organically formed** | Accumulation of organic material | Coal (plant matter), chalk and some limestone (marine shells/organisms) |

Sedimentary rocks are layered (**stratified**) and often contain **fossils** — which makes them important for history, biology and even climate reconstruction.

**Metamorphic rocks** — formed when pre-existing rocks are changed by heat, pressure or chemical action without completely melting. The result is usually harder and more compact than the original.

| Original rock | Metamorphic product |
|---|---|
| Shale | Slate → phyllite → schist (with increasing grade) |
| Granite | Gneiss |
| Limestone | Marble |
| Sandstone | Quartzite |

Metamorphic rocks are classified as **foliated** (layered structure visible — slate, schist, gneiss) or **non-foliated** (no layering — marble, quartzite).

### 6.3 The rock cycle

No rock type is permanent. The rock cycle describes the continuous pathways of transformation:

```
        Uplift / exposure
            ↑
Metamorphic rock ←─ Heat + pressure ─← Igneous or sedimentary rock
     ↓                                       ↑
Weathering/erosion               Compaction and cementation
     ↓                                       ↑
Sediment ─────────────────────────────→ Sedimentary rock
                   ↓
         Subduction into mantle (melting)
                   ↓
                Magma ─── Eruption or intrusion ──→ Igneous rock
```

Key transformations:
- **Magma → igneous rock:** cooling and solidification (slow = intrusive; fast = extrusive).
- **Any rock → sediment → sedimentary rock:** weathering breaks the rock down, erosion transports fragments, deposition and lithification consolidate them.
- **Any rock → metamorphic rock:** heat and pressure, without melting, transform structure and mineralogy.
- **Any rock → magma:** sufficiently high temperature, reduced pressure or added water can permit partial melting. Subduction commonly supplies water to the mantle above the descending slab; the whole slab does not simply melt as it sinks ([USGS][usgs-mantle]).

> **[2021 Q64][pyq2021] (Answer: b):** Black cotton soil (Regur) of the Deccan formed primarily from the weathering of **fissure volcanic rock** — the basalt of the Deccan Traps. This directly illustrates the rock cycle: extrusive igneous rock (basalt) weathers into a distinctive clay-rich soil.

## 7. Continental drift, seafloor spreading and plate tectonics

### 7.1 Continental drift — Wegener's insight and its limits

Alfred Wegener, a German meteorologist, proposed in 1912 that all continents were once joined in a single supercontinent he called **Pangaea**, surrounded by a single ocean (**Panthalassa**) ([NCERT Ch. 4][ncert-cont]). Around 200 million years ago Pangaea split into **Laurasia** in the north and **Gondwanaland** in the south; these in turn fragmented into the continents we see today.

**Evidence Wegener marshalled:**

1. **Jigsaw fit:** The Atlantic coastlines of South America and Africa align closely.
2. **Matching rock belts:** Ancient rock formations along the Brazil coast are geologically identical to formations along the West African coast. If the Atlantic did not exist, these would be the same continuous belt.
3. **Fossil distribution:** Identical fossils of *Mesosaurus* (a freshwater reptile), *Glossopteris* (a fern), and *Lystrosaurus* appear on continents now separated by oceans — implying those continents were once joined.
4. **Glacial evidence:** Glacial deposits (tillite) are found in South Africa, India, Australia and Antarctica, suggesting all were once in a cold, high-latitude position as a single landmass.
5. **Gondwana sediment system:** A distinctive sequence of sedimentary layers (the Gondwana System) found in India also appears in South America, Africa, Antarctica and Australia — all once connected parts of Gondwanaland.
6. **Matching mineral deposits:** Gold placer deposits in Ghana (West Africa) are matched by gold deposits in Brazil — the same geological formation split by the opening of the Atlantic.

> **[2025 Q45][pyq2025] (Answer: c — all three):** All three statements describe genuine evidence for continental drift: (I) matching rock belts in Brazil and West Africa, (II) the Ghana–Brazil gold connection, and (III) the Gondwana System across six southern landmasses.

**Why the theory was initially rejected:** Wegener proposed two forces — a *pole-fleeing force* from Earth's rotation and a *tidal force* from the Moon and Sun. Both were shown to be far too weak to move continents. Without a convincing mechanism, most geologists dismissed the theory until mid-20th-century oceanographic discoveries revived it.

### 7.2 Seafloor spreading — Harry Hess's mechanism

Post-World War II ocean surveys mapped mid-oceanic ridges (long submarine mountain chains) and deep-sea trenches. Harry Hess proposed in 1961 that:

- Molten material (magma) rises at mid-oceanic ridges, erupts, cools and becomes new oceanic crust.
- This pushes older oceanic crust sideways on both sides of the ridge.
- When old oceanic crust reaches a trench, it sinks back into the mantle (subduction), completing the cycle.

**Key evidence:**
- **Magnetic stripe symmetry:** Earth's magnetic field periodically reverses polarity. As new basalt cools at a ridge, it records the current polarity. This produces symmetric magnetic stripes on either side of the ridge — like a magnetic tape recorder. These stripes are unmistakable evidence of seafloor spreading.
- **Rock ages:** Ocean floor rocks are youngest near ridge crests and get older with distance — exactly what spreading predicts. No ocean-floor rock is older than about 200 million years; continental rocks can be 4 billion years old.

### 7.3 Plate tectonics — the unified theory

Plate tectonics, formalised in the late 1960s, unifies continental drift and seafloor spreading ([NCERT Ch. 4][ncert-tect]). Its core statement: *the lithosphere is divided into rigid plates that move over the plastic asthenosphere; it is plates, not continents alone, that move.*

**Major plates (7):** Pacific, North American, South American, African, Eurasian, Indo-Australian, and Antarctic.

**Minor plates (selected):** Nazca, Cocos, Philippine, Arabian, Caribbean.

Plates move at 2–10 cm per year, driven by **mantle convection currents** — hot, less-dense mantle material rises, cools, becomes denser and sinks, dragging the overlying plate along.

> **[2013 Q22][pyq2013] (Answer: d — all six):** Electromagnetic radiation, geothermal energy, gravitational force, plate movements, rotation and revolution of Earth *are all* responsible for dynamic changes on Earth's surface. This seemingly comprehensive question tests whether students recognise that geothermal energy and plate movements are independent, internal drivers alongside the external ones.

### 7.4 Plate boundaries — the engine of geology

Everything important in this chapter — mountains, volcanoes, earthquakes, rock types, ocean basins — can be traced to what happens at plate boundaries. There are three kinds:

#### Divergent boundaries — plates move apart

Magma wells up to fill the gap, creating new crust and pushing the plates further apart.

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| **Mid-oceanic ridges** | Long submarine mountain chains along the spreading centre. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge divides the Atlantic from north to south; the ridge system is the longest mountain chain on Earth (though mostly underwater). |
| **Rift valleys** | On land, divergent forces create elongated valleys (grabens). The East African Rift Valley is the continent-based equivalent of a mid-ocean ridge. |
| **Volcanism** | Basalt erupts where magma reaches the surface. Lava pours out in long fissures rather than through a single vent — called **fissure eruption**. |
| **Earthquakes** | Shallow and moderate; rock fractures as plates pull apart. |

#### Convergent boundaries — plates move together

The denser plate sinks under the lighter one — **subduction**. Three subtypes:

**Oceanic–continental convergence:**
- Dense oceanic plate subducts under lighter continental plate.
- Forms a deep **trench** where subduction occurs (e.g., Peru–Chile Trench, Japan Trench).
- As the oceanic plate descends, its water-bearing minerals release water. This lowers the melting temperature of the hot **mantle wedge** above the slab, allowing partial melting. Buoyant magma rises through the overriding continental plate and can feed explosive **stratovolcanoes** (e.g., the Andes). Slab melting can occur in some settings, but is not the standard explanation for an ordinary volcanic arc ([USGS][usgs-subduction]).
- Intense earthquakes — shallow near the trench, deepening inland along the subducting slab.

**Oceanic–oceanic convergence:**
- The older, denser oceanic plate subducts.
- Forms deep trenches (e.g., Mariana Trench — deepest point on Earth at ~11 km).
- Water released by the subducting slab promotes partial melting in the mantle wedge. Magma rises through the overriding oceanic plate to build **island arcs** (curved chains of volcanic islands, e.g., the Aleutians).

**Continental–continental convergence:**
- Neither plate is dense enough to subduct easily.
- Crust crumples and buckles upward, forming high **fold mountain ranges**.
- Himalayas: formed when the Indian plate collided with the Eurasian plate; the collision started about 40–50 million years ago and continues today, causing the Himalayas to still rise and generating frequent Himalayan earthquakes.
- No typical oceanic-subduction volcanic arc along the main collision zone. Continental underthrusting/subduction and local melting can still occur; “no melting anywhere” would be too absolute.

**Cross-section through an ocean–continent subduction zone** — original schematic, not to scale; the mechanism follows [USGS][usgs-subduction].

```text
       ocean                    trench             continent
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~ V ________________ /\ volcano
oceanic plate moves right --->    \                /  \
                                  \     magma ↑
                       descending  \ water ↗  ↑
                       solid slab   \   MANTLE WEDGE
                                     \  partial melting
                                      \ (water lowers melting point)
```

The **slab** is the descending plate; the **mantle wedge** lies above it and below the overriding plate. Keeping those two regions separate explains where most arc magma originates.

#### Transform boundaries — plates slide past each other

- Crust is neither created nor destroyed.
- Rocks grind against each other; stress builds and releases as earthquakes.
- Classic example: **San Andreas Fault** (California) — Pacific Plate sliding NW past the North American Plate.
- Transform faults also cut mid-ocean ridges, connecting offset ridge segments.

## 8. Mountains — how the land was raised

Different processes raise mountains; recognising the type tells you the plate setting:

| Mountain type | Mechanism | Plate setting | Indian / world example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fold mountains** | Compression buckles rock into anticlines and synclines | Continental–continental convergence (also oceanic–continental) | Himalayas, Andes, Alps, Rockies |
| **Block mountains** | Tensional faulting: a block rises (horst) while adjacent blocks drop (graben) | Rifting (divergent tendency) | Vindhya Range (partly); Rhine Graben of Germany |
| **Volcanic mountains** | Erupted material accumulates | Convergent (subduction volcanoes) or hot spots | Deccan Traps (plateau not mountain); Hawaii (hotspot); Fuji (subduction) |
| **Residual / relict mountains** | Harder rocks remain after long erosion of a formerly higher plateau | Passive margin; old stable cratons | Eastern Ghats, Aravalli (among the world's oldest fold mountains, now heavily eroded) |

## 9. Volcanoes

### 9.1 Structure and products

A **volcano** is an opening in Earth's crust through which molten material (magma), gases and debris erupt. Once magma reaches the surface it is called **lava**.

A **caldera** is a large, roughly circular depression formed when the top of a volcanic cone collapses inward after a large eruption empties the magma chamber below.

**Volcanic products:**

- **Lava flows:** Molten rock that pours out; basaltic lava is thin and flows far; silica-rich (rhyolitic) lava is thick and tends to build domes that explode.
- **Pyroclastic material:** Fragments of solidified lava and country rock blasted out — from large blocks (bombs) to fine ash and dust.
- **Gases:** Water vapour (dominant), carbon dioxide, sulphur compounds (SO₂, H₂S), and nitrogen compounds.
- **Ash and dust:** Can remain suspended for months and years, cooling global temperatures by reflecting sunlight (the "volcanic winter" effect).

> **[2024 Q91][pyq2024] (Answer: d — all four):** Pyroclastic debris, ash and dust, nitrogen compounds and sulphur compounds are all products of volcanic eruptions. Worth noting: sulphur and nitrogen compounds are both emitted; the former is better known but students sometimes forget nitrogen.

### 9.2 Types of volcanoes

| Type | Lava composition | Shape | Eruption style | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Shield volcano** | Basaltic (low silica, low viscosity) | Broad, gently sloping dome | Non-explosive; lava flows freely | Mauna Loa (Hawaii) |
| **Composite (stratovolcano)** | Andesitic to rhyolitic (higher silica) | Steep, cone-shaped | Explosive; alternating lava and pyroclastic layers | Vesuvius, Krakatau, Fuji |
| **Cinder cone** | Basaltic to intermediate | Small, steep cone | Short, explosive eruptions; mainly cinders | Paricutín (Mexico) |
| **Fissure / flood basalt** | Basaltic | Plateau | Lava pours from long cracks | Deccan Traps (India), Siberian Traps |

### 9.3 Distribution

Volcanoes are not random — they follow plate boundaries and hot spots:

- **Mid-oceanic ridges (divergent):** Mostly submarine; Iceland is a surface example.
- **Subduction zones (convergent):** The **Ring of Fire** — the chain of volcanoes encircling the Pacific Ocean (from Chile up through Central America, the Cascades, Alaska, the Aleutians, Japan, the Philippines, Papua New Guinea, New Zealand). Home to ~75 % of Earth's active volcanoes.
- **Continental rift zones (divergent on land):** East African Rift.
- **Hot spots:** Localised plumes of exceptionally hot mantle material, independent of plate boundaries. As a plate moves over a fixed hot spot, a chain of volcanic islands forms — older islands move away; Hawaii is the current hot spot position.

### 9.4 India's volcanoes

**Barren Island** in the Andaman Sea (Andaman and Nicobar Islands) is India's only active volcano. It sits above the subduction zone where the Indian Plate dives under the Burma Plate — a classic convergent, island-arc volcano. It has erupted several times since 1991 and remains intermittently active.

**Narcondam Island** (also in the Andamans) is classified as a dormant volcano.

> **[2018 Q52][pyq2018] (Answer: d — 1 only):** Statement 1 is correct: Barren Island is an active volcano in Indian territory. Statement 2 is incorrect: Barren Island lies about 140 km NE of Port Blair, not east of Great Nicobar (Great Nicobar is the southernmost island, much further south). Statement 3 is incorrect: Barren Island has erupted repeatedly since 1991; it has not been inactive since then.

## 10. Earthquakes

### 10.1 Focus and epicentre

- **Focus (hypocenter):** The point inside the earth where the earthquake actually occurs — where rocks slip or fracture.
- **Epicentre:** The point on the surface directly above the focus. The epicentre is what news reports usually give as "the earthquake's location." Ground shaking is typically strongest here.

### 10.2 How earthquakes are measured

Two different things are being measured:

**Magnitude** measures the energy released at the source.
- **Richter scale** (logarithmic): each unit represents ~31.6 times more energy. A Magnitude 7.0 is about 1,000 times more energetic than a 5.0.
- Measured from seismograph records at a standard distance.

**Intensity** measures the effect felt at a specific place.
- **Modified Mercalli Intensity (MMI) scale** (I–XII): based on how people feel shaking, what objects move, and what structures are damaged.
- Same earthquake can have different intensity values at different locations — intensity decreases with distance from the epicentre; it also depends on local geology (soft sediment amplifies shaking).

### 10.3 Distribution

Earthquakes, like volcanoes, follow plate boundaries:
- **Shallow-focus earthquakes** (focus < 70 km): at all three boundary types; most common at mid-ocean ridges and transform faults.
- **Intermediate and deep-focus earthquakes** (up to ~700 km): only at subduction zones, following the descending slab.

### 10.4 India's seismic zones

India is divided into four seismic zones under Bureau of Indian Standards (IS 1893) ([seismic zones][wiki-seismic]):

| Zone | Risk level | Key areas |
|---|---|---|
| **Zone V** (highest) | Very high | Kashmir Valley, Western and Garhwal Himalayas, North Bihar, North-East India, Rann of Kutch, Andaman and Nicobar Islands |
| **Zone IV** | High | J&K, Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Sikkim, Delhi, parts of Indo-Gangetic plain (N Punjab to Sundarbans) |
| **Zone III** | Moderate | Kerala, Mumbai, Chennai, Kolkata, Pune, Lucknow, Ahmedabad |
| **Zone II** (lowest) | Low | Bangalore, Hyderabad, Nagpur, Jaipur |

The pattern matches the tectonic setting: Zone V is concentrated where the Indian Plate actively collides with and subducts under adjacent plates (Himalayas, Andamans), and in the Rann of Kutch (site of the 1819 and 2001 great earthquakes, related to intraplate stress).

## 11. Tsunamis

A **tsunami** (Japanese: harbour wave) is a series of very long ocean waves triggered by a sudden large displacement of the ocean floor.

**Causes:**
- **Submarine earthquakes** (most common): when a section of seafloor abruptly rises or falls at a subduction zone — the ocean above is suddenly displaced upward or downward.
- **Submarine landslides or volcanic eruptions:** less common, but can generate local tsunamis.

**Key properties:**
- In deep ocean: wavelength hundreds of kilometres, wave height only a few metres but moving at 700–900 km/h (like a jet aircraft). Sailors barely notice one passing.
- As the wave enters shallow water: speed decreases, wavelength compresses, height amplifies dramatically — to tens of metres in extreme cases.
- The first sign at the coast is often the sea pulling back (a trough of the wave arrives before the crest) — a deadly warning sign to move inland immediately.

**Indian Ocean tsunami, 26 December 2004:**
- A M9.1–9.3 earthquake off northern Sumatra (Indian–Australian plate subducting under the Burma Plate) displaced a ~1,200-km section of seafloor.
- Waves crossed the Bay of Bengal within hours; Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Kerala, Andaman and Nicobar Islands, and Sri Lanka bore the worst impact in the region.
- Over 230,000 killed across 14 countries.
- Led to the establishment of the Indian Ocean Tsunami Warning System.

## 12. Connecting plate setting to rocks, landforms and hazards

The chapter's learning outcome is to perform this connection fluently. The table below is not a list to memorise but a demonstration of the reasoning process:

| Plate setting | Main process | Characteristic rocks formed | Characteristic landforms | Expected hazards |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Mid-ocean ridge (divergent)** | Seafloor spreading; basalt erupts; new oceanic crust forms | Basalt (extrusive igneous); gabbro (intrusive) | Submarine ridge and rift valley; Iceland above water | Shallow earthquakes; submarine volcanic activity |
| **Oceanic–continental subduction (convergent)** | Oceanic slab sinks; released water promotes mantle-wedge melting; magma rises through continent | Andesitic/rhyolitic extrusive (stratovolcanoes) on continent; blueschist (metamorphic) in subduction zone | Deep trench (oceanic side); volcanic mountain chain (continental side); e.g., Andes | Explosive volcanic eruptions; deep and shallow earthquakes; tsunamis from large subduction quakes |
| **Oceanic–oceanic subduction (convergent)** | Older slab subducts; magma erupts through oceanic crust | Basalt–andesite island arc volcanics | Deep trench; island arc (curved chain of volcanic islands) | Deep earthquakes; explosive volcanism; tsunamis |
| **Continental–continental collision (convergent)** | Neither subducts easily; rock crumples; crust thickens | Typical outcome is crustal thickening and metamorphism (schist, gneiss), rather than a continuous oceanic-subduction volcanic arc | Fold mountains (Himalayas, Alps, Zagros) | Frequent, often large earthquakes; no typical continuous volcanic arc along the main collision front |
| **Transform fault** | Plates grind horizontally; no creation or destruction | No new rock; older rocks ground and altered | Low ridges and sag ponds; offset river valleys | Shallow, strong earthquakes (no tsunamis unless fault is offshore and has vertical component) |
| **Hot spot (intraplate)** | Mantle plume melts overlying plate; successive eruptions as plate moves | Basalt (shield volcanoes) | Chain of volcanic islands (older → younger → current hot spot); e.g., Hawaii–Emperor chain | Moderate volcanic activity; minor local earthquakes |
| **Rift valley (continental divergent)** | Continent stretches and thins; block faulting | Basalt; rift-related sedimentary fill | Graben (rift valley); horst (flanking plateau or range) | Moderate earthquakes; minor volcanism |

## Common confusions

| Confusion | Clarification |
|---|---|
| **Epicentre vs focus (hypocenter)** | Focus is the actual rupture point inside the earth; epicentre is the surface point above it. The epicentre locates the focus at the surface; the strongest damage need not occur exactly there, because rupture geometry, depth, local ground and buildings also matter. |
| **Magnitude vs intensity** | Magnitude (Richter) measures energy at the source — one value per earthquake. Intensity (Mercalli) measures felt effect at a place — different values at different locations for the same earthquake. |
| **Magma vs lava** | Magma is molten rock underground. The same material becomes lava once it reaches the surface. |
| **P waves travel through liquid** | P waves slow down in liquid but do not stop — they still travel through the outer core. S waves cannot travel through the outer core at all. The S-wave shadow zone confirms the outer core is liquid; the P-wave shadow zone results from refraction, not complete blockage. |
| **Continental drift vs plate tectonics** | Continental drift (Wegener) described the *observation* (continents moved) but lacked a mechanism. Plate tectonics is the *mechanism* (lithospheric plates move on the asthenosphere driven by mantle convection), confirmed by seafloor spreading evidence. |
| **Fold mountains always volcanic?** | No. Pure continental–continental collision produces fold mountains without volcanic activity (Himalayas have no active volcanoes along the main collision zone). Volcanoes form at subduction zones, not pure collision zones. |
| **Transform faults rarely cause tsunamis** | Transform faults generate mainly horizontal motion; tsunamis need a large vertical displacement of the ocean floor. Subduction-zone earthquakes (vertical uplift/subsidence of large seafloor areas) are the primary tsunami source. |
| **Ring of Fire vs all volcanoes** | The Ring of Fire encircles the Pacific and accounts for most of the world's active volcanoes and large earthquakes, but significant volcanic and seismic activity also occurs along the Alpine–Himalayan belt (Mediterranean, Iran, Himalayas) and at mid-ocean ridges. |

## 14. India through this chapter's lens

**The Deccan Traps:** One of Earth's largest igneous provinces — about 500,000 km² of basalt plateaux in western and central India. Formed by massive fissure eruptions around 65 million years ago, as India passed over a hot spot (the Réunion hotspot). The black cotton soil (Regur) of the Deccan is the weathering product of this basalt.

**Himalayan formation:** India was once part of Gondwanaland. It broke away about 140–200 million years ago and drifted north, with the Tethys Sea between it and Asia. The collision with the Eurasian plate (starting ~40–50 million years ago) closed the Tethys Sea, lifting its sediments into the Himalayas (which is why marine fossils appear on Himalayan peaks). Northward movement of the Indian Plate continues at ~5 cm/year, explaining the ongoing seismicity of the Himalayan region.

**The Peninsular Craton:** Most of Peninsular India (the Deccan plateau south of the Vindhya–Satpura line) is an ancient, stable block of some of the world's oldest rocks (Archean, 2.5–4 billion years old). Its long erosional history — billions of years of wearing down — explains the low, residual highlands (Eastern Ghats, Aravallis) and gently rolling plateaux described in Chapter 015.

**Barren Island and Narcondam:** India's tectonic activity in the northeast extends into the Andaman Sea: Barren Island (active, island-arc type, convergent boundary) and Narcondam (dormant) are the surface expression of the Indian Plate subducting under the Burma Plate along the Sunda subduction zone.

**Seismic vulnerability and geography:** The concentration of Zone IV and V hazard in the Himalayan arc, the North-East, and the Rann of Kutch aligns directly with active plate boundaries. The Indo-Gangetic Plain sits in a region of elevated risk (Zone III–IV) because stress is transmitted from the collision zone into the foreland. The stable Deccan plateau is Zone II, reflecting its cratonic character.

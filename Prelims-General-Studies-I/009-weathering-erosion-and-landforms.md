## 1. Orientation

Chapter 008 explained how plate movement and volcanism *build* relief — mountains, plateaus, rift valleys. This chapter explains how relief is then *worn down and reshaped*: how rock breaks apart where it stands, how gravity, rivers, groundwater, ice, wind and waves move the debris, and what shapes that leaves behind. Chapters 007–008 gave you the raw material (rock types, plate settings); this chapter gives you the toolkit to read what happened to that material afterwards — which is exactly what a map, a photograph or a PYQ description of "a deep, flat-bottomed valley with hanging side-valleys" is asking you to do.

Two questions organise the whole chapter:

- **What breaks rock down, and what carries it away?** (weathering, mass movement, and the five erosional agents)
- **Given a landform, which agent and which stage produced it?** (the chapter's own learning outcome)

## 2. Learning outcomes

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

- Distinguish weathering, mass movement, erosion, transportation and deposition, and explain how all four fit under "denudation".
- Classify a weathering example as physical, chemical or biological, and name the reaction behind a chemical example.
- Classify a slope failure as a slow or a rapid mass movement, and list what makes a slope failure-prone versus what triggers an actual failure.
- For each of rivers, groundwater, glaciers, wind and waves: name two erosional and two depositional landforms, and explain the mechanism that produces each.
- Given a short description of a landform, name it and reconstruct the agent and (for rivers) the stage that produced it.
- Place a few standard Indian examples — Gandikota canyon, Kanwar Lake, the Chambal badlands, black cotton soil, the Peninsular Block's residual hills — inside the general mechanism, rather than as isolated named facts.

## 3. Foundations: the vocabulary of landscape change

### 3.1 Denudation and its four parts

**Denudation** is the umbrella term for every process that wears the land surface down; the word means "to strip off or uncover" ([NCERT, Geomorphic Processes][ncert-gp]; [study notes][bharatnotes-gp]). It has four parts, and keeping them separate matters for the exam:

| Term | What happens | Where the material ends up |
|---|---|---|
| **Weathering** | Rock breaks down or decomposes *in place* ("in-situ"), with little or no movement | Stays roughly where it started |
| **Mass movement** | Weathered or unweathered material moves downslope under gravity alone, with no carrying medium | Moves, but only as far as gravity and slope allow |
| **Erosion** | A mobile agent (water, ice, wind, waves) picks up and carries material away, wearing down the surface it acts on | Leaves the source |
| **Deposition** | The agent loses energy and drops its load, usually on a gentler gradient or in calmer water | Settles elsewhere, coarsest material first |

Weathering helps erosion along but is not a precondition for it: a river or a wave can erode solid rock directly by hydraulic action and abrasion ([NCERT, Geomorphic Processes][ncert-gp]). **Transportation** is the leg in between — the actual carrying — and **gradation** is the net result: the wearing down of relief highs (**degradation**) and the filling of basins and depressions (**aggradation**) ([study notes][bharatnotes-gp]).

A **geomorphic process** is a force doing the work; a **geomorphic agent** is the mobile medium carrying it out — running water, groundwater, glacial ice, wind, waves and currents. For the exogenic (surface) processes this chapter covers, the two labels usually point at the same thing ([study notes][bharatnotes-gp]).

### 3.2 Recap: endogenic versus exogenic (from Chapter 008)

Chapter 008 introduced **endogenic forces** (internal — radioactivity, friction, primordial heat; they build relief through diastrophism and volcanism) against **exogenic forces** (external — ultimately solar energy, plus the gradients endogenic forces create; they wear relief down) ([study notes][bharatnotes-gp]). This chapter is entirely about the exogenic side: the Earth's surface is, in effect, "a playfield for two opposing groups of geomorphic processes" ([study notes][bharatnotes-gp]), and everything from here on describes the land-wearing team.

### 3.3 Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Denudation | All exogenic wearing-down processes together: weathering, mass movement, erosion, transportation |
| Gradation | The net wearing-down of relief; degradation (cutting down highs) plus aggradation (filling lows) |
| Erosion | Removal and transport of rock debris by a mobile agent |
| Deposition | Settling of transported material as the agent loses energy |
| Karst | A limestone or dolomite landscape shaped by the dissolving action of groundwater |
| Moraine | Debris deposited directly by glacial ice, named by its position (lateral, medial, terminal, ground) |
| Dune | A mound or ridge of wind-deposited sand, shaped by wind direction and sand supply |

## 4. Weathering

**Physical (mechanical) weathering** breaks rock into smaller pieces of the same composition, driven by applied stress rather than a chemical reaction:

- **Unloading.** Erosion strips away overlying rock, pressure on the rock beneath drops, and the rock expands and fractures in sheets parallel to the surface — producing **exfoliation domes** ([NCERT, Geomorphic Processes][ncert-gp]).
- **Temperature change.** Daily heating and cooling expands and contracts minerals unevenly, most effectively in dry climates and at high elevations; repeated over time this contributes to rounded residual shapes called **tors** ([NCERT, Geomorphic Processes][ncert-gp]).
- **Frost action.** Water in rock pores and cracks freezes, expands by about 9%, and widens the crack; repeated freeze–thaw cycles are most effective at high elevations in the middle latitudes ([NCERT, Geomorphic Processes][ncert-gp]).
- **Salt weathering.** Salts crystallise, hydrate or thermally expand inside pores; crystallisation is the most effective of the three and causes granular disintegration ([NCERT, Geomorphic Processes][ncert-gp]).

**Chemical weathering** decomposes rock by altering its mineral composition:

- **Solution** — a mineral dissolves directly in water or a weak acid.
- **Carbonation** — carbon dioxide dissolved in rainwater forms weak carbonic acid, which attacks feldspars and carbonate minerals; this is also the reaction behind cave formation (Section 7) ([NCERT, Geomorphic Processes][ncert-gp]).
- **Hydration** — a mineral absorbs water and expands (anhydrite taking up water to become gypsum is the standard example).
- **Oxidation and reduction** — minerals combine with oxygen (where air and oxygenated water reach them) or lose it again (where oxygen is absent, as below the water table or in waterlogged ground) ([NCERT, Geomorphic Processes][ncert-gp]).

**Biological weathering** works through living things: plant roots wedging into cracks, burrowing animals (earthworms, termites, rodents) exposing fresh rock, and humic and carbonic acids released by decaying organic matter; ploughing and cultivation count too, since they expose new surfaces to the other two kinds of weathering ([NCERT, Geomorphic Processes][ncert-gp]; [study notes][bharatnotes-gp]). In practice the three kinds usually act together, with one typically dominant in a given setting.

**Weathering in the exam.** Three PYQs turn on exactly this material:

- *"Rainfall is one of the reasons for weathering of rocks... rainwater contains carbon dioxide in solution"* ([2024 Q100][pyq2024]) is testing carbonation directly: CO₂ dissolved in rainwater forms carbonic acid, which is what does the weathering — not the oxygen rainwater also contains.
- Phosphorus has no significant gas phase, so it enters the **phosphorus cycle** almost entirely through the weathering of phosphate-bearing rock ([2021 Q79][pyq2021]) — contrast this with carbon and nitrogen, whose cycles have an atmospheric gas phase.
- **Black cotton soil (regur)** forms from the chemical weathering of basalt — fissure (flood) volcanic rock, the Deccan Traps being the standard Indian case ([2021 Q64][pyq2021]). The soil itself, and where it occurs, belongs to [Chapter 017](017-indian-soils-vegetation-and-regional-links.md); this chapter only needs the weathering mechanism behind it.

## 5. Mass movement

**Mass movement** is the transfer of weathered or unweathered rock debris downslope under gravity alone — no water, ice or wind carries it. Weathering is not a precondition (gravity can move solid bedrock too), but weathered, loosened material is far more prone to moving ([study notes][bharatnotes-gp]).

**Slow movements** are imperceptible on a human timescale and are usually noticed only from their long-term effects — a leaning fence post, a tilted tree trunk:

- **Soil creep** — the very slow downslope creep of soil-covered material on moderately steep slopes.
- **Solifluction** — the slow flow of water-saturated soil or fine debris, common where deeply frozen ground thaws, or over an impervious layer that traps water above it ([study notes][bharatnotes-gp]).

**Rapid movements** are visible as they happen:

| Type | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Slump** | Debris slips with a backward rotation relative to the slope |
| **Debris slide / rockslide** | Debris or rock masses slide or roll, without the backward rotation of a slump; a rockslide follows a bedding, joint or fault surface to substantial depth |
| **Debris fall / rockfall** | Material falls nearly freely from a vertical or overhanging face; affects only superficial layers |
| **Earthflow / mudflow** | Saturated clayey, silty or weathered material flows, often in a channel |
| **Debris avalanche** | A rapid flow down a narrow track on a steep, humid slope; frequent in the Himalaya ([study notes][bharatnotes-gp]) |

**What controls mass movement** splits into two kinds of factor, a distinction worth keeping separate in an answer: conditions that make a slope *prone* to failure, and events that *trigger* an actual failure on a slope that was already prone.

- **Predisposing conditions:** weak or unconsolidated material, thinly bedded rock, faults, steeply dipping beds, a steep or vertical face, heavy or torrential rainfall as a climate, and sparse vegetation.
- **Triggers:** removal of support from below (natural undercutting or excavation); a steeper or taller slope from added material; overloading by natural deposition, artificial fill, or saturation from heavy rain; removal of material from the slope face; earthquakes, explosions or machinery vibration; excessive seepage; a sudden drawdown of a reservoir or river level; and removal of natural vegetation ([study notes][bharatnotes-gp]).

**An Indian case that pushes mass movement toward erosion: the Chambal badlands.** Along the lower Chambal valley (Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh and Rajasthan), small rills on erodible alluvium grow by **headward erosion** into a dense network of ravines, deepened further by the removal of vegetation and overgrazing ([Sharma and Pani 2017][chambal]). The ravine zone covers roughly 4,989 km² — India's largest single ravine tract — with individual ravines 5 to 60 metres deep ([Sharma and Pani 2017][chambal]). A 2025 national assessment found gully erosion a high or very high priority in 77 districts, concentrated in Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh and Rajasthan, and distinguished India's gully terrain into three kinds: *gully systems*, *badlands* (which form only after gully erosion has gone on long enough), and *denuded hill slopes* ([Down To Earth, 2025][gully-report]). This is erosion by concentrated surface runoff rather than mass movement in the strict sense, but the underlying vulnerability — weak material, steep micro-relief, bare ground — is exactly the predisposing-conditions list above, which is why the two topics sit next to each other in most textbooks.

## 6. A common pattern across every agent

Before going agent by agent, notice the pattern that repeats for rivers, groundwater, glaciers, wind and waves alike:

1. The agent picks up material it can carry (its **erosional work**), producing erosional landforms — typically meant to look cut, scooped or worn.
2. The agent loses velocity or energy — a gentler gradient, a wider channel, calmer water, dying wind — and drops the coarsest material first, finer material later (its **depositional work**), producing depositional landforms — typically meant to look built, filled or piled.

Reading any landform question starts with asking: *is this shape the product of something being removed, or something being added?*

## 7. Running water: rivers

### 7.1 Stages of a valley — and how firmly to hold the idea

Textbooks since the late nineteenth century describe a river valley as passing through **youth**, **maturity** and **old age**, "somewhat comparable to the stages of life" ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]):

| Stage | Valley shape | Dominant process |
|---|---|---|
| Youth | Narrow, steep-sided, V-shaped valley; rapids and waterfalls at hard-rock outcrops | Vertical (downward) erosion dominates |
| Maturity | Valley widens; the river starts to meander across a developing floodplain | Erosion and deposition in balance |
| Old age | Broad floodplain, wide meanders, oxbow lakes; the river approaches a flat, low-lying surface | Deposition dominates; very gentle gradient |

> **Fact, interpretation, debate.**
> *Fact:* a young river valley is typically narrow and V-shaped; an old one is typically broad and nearly flat.
> *Textbook picture:* the river valley as a whole landmass passes through a single, predictable life cycle ending in a near-flat **peneplain**, an idea set out by the American geographer **William Morris Davis** between 1884 and 1934 ([Britannica, "geomorphic cycle"][britannica-cycle]).
> *Research view:* Davis treated time as the dominant control, but later work found rock type, structure and the balance of processes matter just as much; landscapes more often settle into a **dynamic equilibrium** — a state where erosional and depositional processes roughly balance and change becomes nearly imperceptible — than march through fixed stages to a single endpoint. Because climate and tectonics keep shifting, Earth's surface systems are open ones, and landscapes are more often "polygenetic" (shaped by more than one episode) than the product of one uninterrupted cycle ([Britannica, "Davisian dynamic equilibrium"][britannica-dynamic]).
> For the exam, use youth/maturity/old age as a description of *shape*, not as proof that every valley has walked through all three stages in order; read the actual evidence (gradient, valley cross-section, what the rock does) rather than assuming the label.

**A direct test of this.** [2026 Q34][pyq2026] asks which features belong to India's **Peninsular Block** — the ancient, geologically stable shield that makes up most of peninsular India (Chapter 015 covers it fully). The correct statements are the **submergence of parts of the western coast** and the presence of **residual mountain ranges** such as the Veliconda and Mahendragiri hills: resistant rock left standing after prolonged denudation wore down everything softer around it — an old-age signature. The statement the key rejects is "**deep, V-shaped river valleys formed by fast-flowing rivers**" — a youth-stage signature, more typical of the geologically young, actively uplifting Himalaya than of the long-stable Peninsular Block. The question is really asking you to match a landform shape to a stage, exactly as Section 7.1 describes.

### 7.2 Erosional landforms

- **V-shaped valley.** The signature cross-section of the youth stage, cut mainly by downward (vertical) erosion.
- **Gorge and canyon.** A **gorge** is a deep valley with steep-to-vertical sides of roughly equal width top to bottom; a **canyon** is a variant that is markedly wider at the top than the bottom, with step-like side slopes, usually where the rock resists erosion ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]). **Gandikota**, on the Pennar river in Andhra Pradesh — sometimes called the "Grand Canyon of India" — is cut through quartzite of the Erramala hills and is about 200 m wide; the river formed it ([Kadapa district administration][kadapa]), confirmed as the correct answer to [2022 Q99][pyq2022].
- **Waterfalls and rapids.** Form where a band of harder rock crosses the river's course and resists downcutting, leaving a step the water falls or rushes over.
- **Potholes and plunge pools.** Swirling water trapped in an irregularity on a rocky bed spins rock fragments that grind out a near-circular **pothole**; a large, deep pothole-like hollow at the foot of a waterfall is a **plunge pool** ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]).
- **Meanders and incised meanders.** A loop-like channel pattern — strictly "not a landform but... a type of channel pattern" ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]) — that in most rivers develops on a floodplain, but can also be cut down into hard rock as a deep **incised** or **entrenched** meander where the land has been uplifted after the meander already existed.
- **River terraces.** Old valley-floor or floodplain surfaces left standing above the current floodplain after the river has cut down further ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]).

### 7.3 Depositional landforms

- **Alluvial fan.** A broad, low-to-high cone of sediment dropped where a river leaves a steep gradient (commonly a mountain front) for a much gentler one and abruptly loses carrying capacity ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]).
- **Floodplain, natural levees and point bars.** A **floodplain** is the main depositional feature of a river's lower, mature-to-old course, built up by repeated flooding. **Natural levees** are low, parallel ridges of coarser material along the riverbanks, built where floodwater loses velocity fastest right at the channel edge. **Point bars** form on the inside (convex-ward, lower-velocity) bank of a meander ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]).
- **Oxbow lake.** As a meander loop grows, its neck narrows until the river cuts straight across it, abandoning the loop as a crescent-shaped lake. **Kanwar Lake** (Kabar Tal) in Bihar, Asia's largest freshwater oxbow lake and a Ramsar wetland, formed this way from the meandering of the **Gandak** river — confirmed by [2023 Q42][pyq2023].
- **Delta.** A roughly triangular depositional feature at a river's mouth, built the same way as an alluvial fan but where a river meets standing water (a lake or sea) rather than a gentler land gradient ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]).

## 8. Groundwater: karst landforms

**Karst topography** is "any limestone or dolomitic region showing typical landforms" produced by groundwater, chiefly through **solution** (Section 4) ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]). It needs two conditions together: rock that is both **soluble** (limestone, dolomite, some evaporites) and sufficiently **permeable** for water to reach it, which is why karst is lithology-controlled rather than climate-controlled the way river or glacial landforms are ([study notes][bharatnotes-gp]). Dissolution is most intense exactly where groundwater first meets the rock surface; the dissolved carbonate is carried away, and a depression gradually opens ([USGS, sinkholes][usgs-sink]).

**Erosional (solutional) landforms:**

- **Sinkhole (swallow hole).** Roughly circular at the top, funnelling narrower toward the bottom ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]); USGS describes the same feature as ground with no natural external drainage, so rainwater simply disappears into the subsurface ([USGS, sinkholes][usgs-sink]).
- **Doline, uvala and valley sink.** A **doline** is sometimes used specifically for a collapse-type sinkhole; where several sinkholes merge into a long, narrow-to-wide trench, the result is a **valley sink** or **uvala** ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]).
- **Lapies and limestone pavement.** Differential solution along joints cuts a maze of grooves and ridges called **lapies**; a lapies field that solution has smoothed further becomes a **limestone pavement** ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]).
- **Caves and tunnels.** Continued solution along a line of weakness widens it into a cave; a cave open at both ends is a **tunnel** ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]). As dissolution continues underground, cavities enlarge until, if the roof loses support, the surface above can collapse — the usual way a sinkhole suddenly opens ([USGS, sinkholes][usgs-sink]).

**Depositional landforms** (inside caves, where dripping water evaporates and redeposits the dissolved calcium carbonate):

- **Stalactite** — hangs from the cave roof, broad at its base and tapering down.
- **Stalagmite** — rises from the cave floor, built by drips falling from above.
- **Pillar (column)** — a stalactite and a stalagmite that have grown until they fuse ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]).

## 9. Glaciers

Glacial landforms are climate-controlled, confined to high latitudes and high mountain elevations where snow accumulates faster than it melts.

**Erosional landforms:**

- **Cirque.** A deep, steep-walled, armchair-shaped basin where a glacier originates, carved back into a mountainside; a lake that later occupies an abandoned cirque is a **cirque** or **tarn lake** ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]).
- **Horn and arête.** Where several cirques cut back into the same peak from different sides, they leave a sharp, pyramidal **horn**; a sharp, saw-toothed ridge between two cirques is an **arête** ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]).
- **Glacial trough (U-shaped valley) and hanging valley.** A glacier moving down a pre-existing river valley straightens and over-deepens it into a broad-floored, steep-sided **trough**, distinctly U-shaped rather than a river's V-shape. A smaller tributary glacier, with less erosive power, cuts a shallower trough that is left "hanging" above the main trough's floor once both glaciers melt ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]).
**Valley cross-sections** — original sketches based on the preceding NCERT descriptions; not to scale. These are shapes across a valley, not maps of a river's course.

The sketches show diagnostic tendencies, not proof of origin by shape alone; rock structure and later modification also matter.

- **Fjord.** A deep glacial trough that has been flooded by the sea, forming the deep, steep-sided inlets typical of high-latitude coastlines such as Norway ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]).

**Depositional landforms:**

- **Till and moraine.** **Till** is the unsorted mix of coarse and fine debris a melting glacier simply drops in place. Organised ridges of till are **moraines**, named by position: **terminal** (a ridge marking the glacier's farthest advance), **lateral** (along the valley sides), **medial** (down the centre, where two glaciers' lateral moraines merge after the glaciers join), and **ground** (a variable-thickness sheet left under the ice) ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]).
- **Outwash and outwash plain.** Meltwater streams sort and rework till beyond the ice front into layered **glacio-fluvial** (outwash) deposits; where these spread and merge, they form a broad, flat **outwash plain** ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]).
- **Esker.** A long, winding (sinuous) ridge of sand and gravel, marking the former course of a stream that ran inside or beneath the ice ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]).
- **Drumlin.** A smooth, oval, whale-backed ridge composed mainly of glacial till, shaped by ice moving over it ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]).

## 10. Wind: landforms of arid and semi-arid lands

Sparse vegetation leaves much desert sediment exposed to wind and occasional flash floods. Temperature changes and salt crystallisation can contribute to mechanical breakdown. Do not infer that chemical weathering is fastest here: it generally favours **warm, humid** conditions, where water supports mineral reactions; dry climates limit it ([Hong Kong Geological Survey][hkg-weathering]).

**Erosional landforms:**

- **Pediment and pediplain.** A **pediment** is a gently sloping rock floor at the foot of a mountain front, cut mainly by sheet flow and lateral stream erosion rather than by wind alone; where pediments from different mountain fronts merge over time into one low, nearly featureless surface, the result is a **pediplain** ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]).
- **Inselberg.** An isolated hill or hill-remnant standing above a pediplain, left behind as the more resistant mass around which erosion wore everything else down ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]) — conceptually the desert cousin of the Peninsular Block's residual hills (Section 7.1).
- **Deflation hollow, blow-out and wind caves.** Wind picks up (deflates) loose, fine material, scooping out shallow depressions called **deflation hollows** or **blow-outs**; the same abrasive action, directed sideways against a rock face, can hollow out **wind caves** ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]).
- **Mushroom, table and pedestal rocks.** Wind-driven sand abrades hardest near the ground (where sand grains are densest in the air), undercutting a rock into a mushroom shape with a slender stalk; a flat-topped remnant is a **table rock**, and any such undercut remnant standing on a narrower base is loosely a **pedestal rock** ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]).

**Depositional landforms:**

- **Playa and alkali flat.** Rare desert rainfall collects in shallow, temporary lakes called **playas**; once dry, a playa floor crusted with salts is an **alkali flat** ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]).
- **Sand dunes.** Shape follows wind direction and sand supply: **barchans** are crescent-shaped with the crescent's points (horns) trailing downwind, where sand supply is limited and wind direction steady; **seifs** are similar but drawn out into a single long wing; **parabolic dunes** curve the other way, held back at the tips by vegetation; **longitudinal dunes** align parallel to a steady wind where sand supply is poor; **transverse dunes** form as ridges at right angles to the wind where sand is abundant ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]).
- **Loess.** Not wind-*eroded* but wind-*built*: a gritty, porous deposit of fine, wind-carried silt, now known to be wind-deposited after early geologists mistook it for a river or lake deposit. Thick loess occurs in parts of the United States and in Shaanxi, China, where accumulated layers reach roughly 300 ft (about 90 m) ([USGS, loess][usgs-loess]). India's Peninsular rock record has no equivalent wind-blown loess sheet; the NCERT landforms chapter used for this section does not cover loess at all, so treat it as a commonly taught comparison rather than something tied to a specific Indian location.

## 11. Waves: coastal landforms

Wave-built and wave-cut landforms depend on a particular location — the land–sea interface — rather than on climate the way river, glacial and wind landforms do ([study notes][bharatnotes-gp]). The wave mechanics themselves (how a wave forms, breaks and refracts) belong with [Chapter 013](013-ocean-properties-and-circulation.md); here the concern is only what waves build and cut once they reach a coast.

**Erosional landforms:**

- **Sea cliff.** A steep coastal rock face, typically a few metres to about 30 m high, cut back by wave action at its base ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]).
- **Wave-cut platform (terrace).** A gently sloping rock shelf at the foot of a retreating cliff, left behind — above the average wave height — as the cliff is cut back further ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]).
- **Sea cave and stack.** Waves exploit a weakness in the cliff face, hollowing and deepening it into a **sea cave**; where a once-continuous headland is cut through and partly removed, the surviving resistant remnant standing apart from the cliff is a **sea stack** ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]).

**Depositional landforms:**

- **Beach and coastal dune.** A **beach** is the characteristic depositional shoreline feature; where onshore wind then reworks beach sand inland, it can build **coastal dunes** as ridges parallel to the shore ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]).
- **Offshore bar, barrier bar and spit.** An **offshore bar** is a ridge of sand and shingle built in the surf zone; once it is built up enough to stand exposed above water, it becomes a **barrier bar**; where such a bar is attached to the coast at one end, trailing off into open water, it is a **spit** ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]).
- **Lagoon.** Where a barrier bar or spit grows across a bay and seals it off from the open sea, the enclosed water body is a **lagoon** ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]).
- **Wave-built terrace.** A gently sloping apron of deposited sediment built out in front of a wave-cut terrace ([NCERT, Landforms and their Evolution][ncert-lf]).

## 12. Recognise the landform, reconstruct the process

The chapter's stated learning outcome, worked through five short descriptions. In each case, ask: which agent, and is this erosional or depositional?

| Description | Landform | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| "A deep, flat-bottomed valley with smaller side-valleys ending abruptly high up on its walls" | Glacial trough with a hanging valley | A U-shaped cross-section is the glacial signature (Section 9); a tributary glacier with less erosive power could not cut down as deep as the main glacier, leaving its valley "hanging" |
| "A crescent-shaped sand ridge, its two pointed ends trailing in the same direction" | Barchan dune | Points (horns) trail downwind; a crescent with limited sand supply and a steady wind direction is the textbook barchan (Section 10) |
| "A steep rock face at the shore, with a flat, gently sloping rock shelf at its foot, bare at low tide" | Sea cliff with a wave-cut platform | The platform is what remains as waves cut the cliff back further and further; its flatness and its position below the cliff are both diagnostic (Section 11) |
| "A nearly circular depression in a limestone area, funnelling narrower toward the bottom, with no stream flowing out of it" | Sinkhole (swallow hole) | No surface drainage out of a depression in soluble rock points to water disappearing into a solution-widened opening below (Section 8) |
| "A crescent-shaped lake beside a river, no longer connected to the main channel" | Oxbow lake | A meander loop has been cut off at its neck and abandoned (Section 7.3) — the mechanism behind Bihar's Kanwar Lake |

## Common confusions

| Easily confused | The difference |
|---|---|
| Weathering vs. erosion | Weathering breaks rock down where it stands; erosion is an agent actually carrying the debris away. Some landforms are erosional, others depositional or shaped by weathering; do not assume that every landform requires removal by an erosional agent. |
| Mass movement vs. erosion | Mass movement is gravity alone moving debris downslope, with no carrying medium; erosion always involves a mobile agent (water, ice, wind, waves) doing the moving. |
| Gorge vs. canyon | Both are deep, steep-sided valleys; a canyon is specifically wider at the top than the bottom, with step-like sides, while a gorge is closer to equally wide top to bottom. |
| Sinkhole vs. doline vs. uvala | All are karst depressions at increasing scale: a sinkhole is the basic funnel-shaped form; "doline" is sometimes reserved for a collapse-type sinkhole; an uvala is several sinkholes merged into one long trench. |
| Cirque vs. corrie vs. cwm | Regional names for the same landform (a glacier's armchair-shaped source basin) — corrie in Scotland, cwm in Wales, cirque elsewhere. |
| Lateral, medial, terminal, ground moraine | All are debris deposited by ice, distinguished only by *position*: side of the glacier, centre (where two glaciers merge), the farthest point reached, and beneath the ice sheet, respectively. |
| Pediment vs. pediplain vs. inselberg | A pediment is one gently sloping rock floor at a single mountain front; a pediplain is several pediments merged into one low surface; an inselberg is a resistant hill left standing on a pediplain. |
| Barchan vs. seif vs. longitudinal dune | All form with limited sand and a fairly steady wind; a barchan is crescent-shaped, a seif stretches that crescent into one long wing, and a longitudinal dune is a straight ridge aligned with the wind rather than curved. |
| Spit vs. barrier bar vs. offshore bar | Stages of the same feature: an offshore bar is submerged, a barrier bar has grown to stand exposed, and a spit is a barrier bar attached to the coast at one end. |

## 14. India through this chapter's lens

A short list of standing Indian examples, each explained by a mechanism taught above rather than memorised as an isolated fact. Full regional detail for each belongs to the chapter named alongside it.

- **Gandikota canyon** (Pennar river, Andhra Pradesh) — river erosion through resistant quartzite (Section 7.2); see [Chapter 016](016-indian-rivers-lakes-and-water-geography.md) for the river system.
- **Kanwar Lake** (Gandak river, Bihar) — an oxbow lake left by a cut-off meander (Section 7.3); see [Chapter 016](016-indian-rivers-lakes-and-water-geography.md).
- **The Chambal badlands** (Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan) — gully erosion on weak alluvium, intensified by deforestation and overgrazing (Section 5); see [Chapter 017](017-indian-soils-vegetation-and-regional-links.md) for the soil-conservation angle.
- **Black cotton soil (regur)** — the chemical weathering product of Deccan Trap basalt (Section 4); see [Chapter 017](017-indian-soils-vegetation-and-regional-links.md) for where it occurs and what it supports.
- **The Peninsular Block's residual hills**, such as the Veliconda and Mahendragiri ranges — resistant rock left standing after prolonged denudation reduced the surrounding shield, an old-age landscape signature (Section 7.1); see [Chapter 015](015-india-s-location-and-physical-regions.md) for India's physical regions as a whole.

## 1. Orientation

This chapter answers one question: **why are some places hotter than others?** The answer lies in the atmosphere — a thin envelope of gas that surrounds Earth, intercepts sunlight, traps heat and distributes energy around the planet.

Understanding the atmosphere requires three levels:

1. **What it is made of and how it is structured** — composition and layers.
2. **How it receives and distributes energy** — the heat budget.
3. **What that means for temperature** — why altitude, latitude and surface type all matter.

This chapter builds the foundation for Chapter 011 (winds) and Chapter 012 (climate and the Indian monsoon). PYQs test this topic repeatedly, particularly on how the atmosphere is actually heated — a mechanistic question with a counterintuitive answer — and on land-versus-sea temperature differences.

## 2. Composition of the atmosphere

The atmosphere is a mixture of gases, water vapour and suspended particles ([NCERT Cl. 11, Ch. 8][ncert11-comp]).

### 2.1 Gases

Dry air is composed of:

| Gas | Approximate share by volume | Key role |
|---|---|---|
| **Nitrogen (N₂)** | 78.08% | Major component; relatively inert; dilutes oxygen |
| **Oxygen (O₂)** | 20.95% | Essential for respiration and combustion |
| **Argon (Ar)** | 0.93% | Inert; no major atmospheric role |
| **Carbon dioxide (CO₂)** | ~0.04% (about 427 ppm as of 2025) | Absorbs and re-emits longwave (terrestrial) radiation; key greenhouse gas |
| **Other trace gases** | Methane, nitrous oxide, ozone, etc. | Various greenhouse and chemical roles |

**Water vapour** is variable (less than 1% in deserts and polar regions; up to 4% in humid tropical areas). Despite being small in quantity, it is critical: it absorbs outgoing longwave radiation and drives the global water cycle.

About 99% of the atmosphere's mass lies within 32 km of the surface.

### 2.2 Dust and aerosols

The atmosphere also contains dust particles — sea salt, soil, smoke, soot, pollen and ash. These:
- Act as condensation nuclei, the tiny surfaces around which water vapour condenses to form cloud droplets (and therefore rain).
- Scatter and absorb sunlight, affecting how much energy reaches the surface.
- Concentrate near the surface and in areas with high human or desert dust activity.

> **[2025 Q46][pyq2025a] (Answer: c):** Dust particles are more abundant in subtropical and temperate areas than in equatorial or polar regions — TRUE. The stated reason (subtropical/temperate areas have less dry winds) is INCORRECT. Those zones actually have abundant dry winds. The correct explanation is that subtropical and temperate areas include large dry landmasses, sparse vegetation, and human activity that generate dust, while equatorial regions are dominated by moisture and dense vegetation that anchor soil particles, and polar regions are icy. Statement I is correct; Statement II is not.

## 3. Structure of the atmosphere — the layers

The atmosphere is divided into layers based on how temperature changes with altitude ([NCERT Cl. 11, Ch. 8][ncert11-comp]).

### 3.1 Troposphere (0–13 km average)

- The lowest layer; contains about 75–80% of the atmosphere's total mass.
- **Temperature decreases with altitude** at the normal lapse rate (~6.5°C per 1,000 m), because the surface is the primary heat source for this layer.
- Thickness varies: about **8 km at the poles** and **18 km at the equator**. This matters — see the PYQ below.
- Contains virtually all water vapour and dust; almost all ordinary weather (clouds, rain, storms and fog) occurs here.
- Bounded at the top by the **tropopause** (a transition to a much smaller temperature decrease, or an inversion, that usually limits deep convection).

> **[2024 Q64][pyq2024b] (Answer: a — both correct, Statement II explains Statement I):** The troposphere is thicker at the equator (18 km) than at the poles (8 km). This is explained by strong convectional currents at the equator: the equator receives intense insolation, the surface heats powerfully, and convection carries heat (and therefore the troposphere boundary) much higher than at the poles where heating is weak.

### 3.2 Stratosphere (13–50 km)

- Temperature **increases with altitude** in the upper stratosphere, warming from about −60°C to 0°C.
- This warming happens because the **ozone layer** (located here, between roughly 10 and 50 km, with peak concentration at 30–35 km) absorbs ultraviolet (UV) radiation from the Sun.
- The stratosphere is relatively dry and cloudless; horizontal winds dominate (no strong vertical convection, because warmer air sits above cooler air — a stable configuration).
- The ozone layer's UV absorption is what makes life on Earth's surface possible.

### 3.3 Mesosphere (50–80 km)

- Temperature **decreases with altitude** again, reaching about −100°C at 80 km.
- Meteors burn up in this layer.
- Upper limit: mesopause.

### 3.4 Thermosphere (above roughly 80–85 km)

Temperature rises sharply as gases absorb high-energy solar radiation. The thermosphere extends to several hundred kilometres; its upper boundary changes with solar conditions. High temperature here does not mean a dense, hot atmosphere like an oven: particles are extremely sparse ([NOAA layers][noaa-layers]). Auroras commonly occur at these heights.

### 3.5 Exosphere

Above the thermosphere, the extremely sparse **exosphere** gradually merges into space. Its lower boundary is variable; a single universal 400-km boundary is misleading.

### 3.6 The ionosphere overlaps these layers

The **ionosphere** is named for electrically charged particles, not for a temperature trend. It overlaps the upper mesosphere, thermosphere and part of the exosphere; its extent changes with sunlight and solar activity ([NASA][nasa-ionosphere]). It affects radio-wave propagation. Do not substitute “ionosphere” for “thermosphere” in a list classified by temperature.

**Vertical arrangement** — original schematic; height intervals are approximate and not drawn to scale. Read temperature trends upward.

```text
     SPACE
     Exosphere          extremely sparse gas       } ionised regions
     Thermosphere       temperature rises ↑        } overlap these
~85 km -- mesopause --                              } layers; they are
     Mesosphere         temperature falls ↑        } not a sixth slab
~50 km -- stratopause --
     Stratosphere       temperature rises ↑; ozone absorbs UV
variable tropopause, about 8–18 km
     Troposphere        temperature usually falls ↑; most weather
0 km ---------------- EARTH'S SURFACE ------------------------
```

“Temperature falls ↑” means temperature falls **as height increases**. The ionosphere is a different classification superimposed on the temperature layers, not another layer stacked between them.

## 4. Insolation — solar energy incoming

**Insolation** means *incoming solar radiation* — the energy Earth receives from the Sun in the form of electromagnetic waves ([NCERT Cl. 11, Ch. 9][ncert11-heat]).

### 4.1 Shortwave radiation

Solar radiation is primarily **shortwave** (visible light, near-infrared, and ultraviolet). The atmosphere is largely **transparent** to shortwave radiation — most of it passes straight through without being absorbed by the air itself and reaches the Earth's surface.

> **[2023 Q1][pyq2023a] (Answer: c):** Which statement about Earth's atmosphere is correct? Option (c) is correct: *Infrared waves are largely absorbed by water vapour that is concentrated in the lower atmosphere.* The other options are wrong: insolation at the equator is not 10 times that at the poles (the ratio depends on season, location and the averaging used); infrared contributes a large share of solar energy, but not a fixed two-thirds; the split depends on the wavelength boundaries and whether it is measured above the atmosphere or at the surface; and infrared waves are *not* part of the visible spectrum (they are longer wavelengths, invisible to the human eye).

### 4.2 Factors that affect how much insolation a surface receives

1. **Latitude:** Near the equator, the Sun is nearly overhead — its rays strike a small area at a steep angle, concentrating energy. Near the poles, the same energy spreads over a larger area and is weaker. This is the fundamental reason temperatures are highest at the equator and lowest at the poles.
2. **Duration of sunshine:** At higher latitudes, day length varies dramatically (see Chapter 007). A longer day delivers more total energy.
3. **Angle of incidence:** Even at the same latitude, a slope facing the Sun receives more energy than a flat surface (important for landform heating).
4. **Transparency of the atmosphere:** Clouds, dust and water vapour reflect or absorb some insolation before it reaches the surface.

## 5. Albedo and the heat budget

### 5.1 Albedo

**Albedo** is the fraction of incoming solar radiation that is reflected back into space without being absorbed. Surfaces differ greatly:

| Surface | Approximate albedo |
|---|---|
| Fresh snow / ice | 80–95% (very high — reflects most light) |
| Cloud tops | ~70% |
| Desert sand | ~30–40% |
| Forests | ~10–20% |
| Oceans | ~6% (low — absorbs most light) |
| Average for the whole Earth | ~30–35% |

High-albedo surfaces stay cooler; low-albedo surfaces absorb more energy and warm up. This is why snow and ice amplify cooling, and why dark forests and oceans warm up efficiently.

### 5.2 The global heat budget

The heat budget describes how incoming energy is accounted for globally. Taking 100 units as the insolation reaching the top of the atmosphere ([NCERT Cl. 11, Ch. 9][ncert11-heat]):

**Outgoing (reflected / returned to space):** 35 units total
- ~27 units reflected by clouds
- ~2 units reflected by snow and ice
- ~6 units reflected/scattered by dust and gases

**Absorbed:** 65 units total
- ~14 units absorbed directly by the atmosphere (mainly by ozone, water vapour and clouds)
- ~51 units absorbed by the Earth's surface

**Transfers and escape to space must be counted separately.** In this simplified NCERT account:

| Energy path | Units | How to count it |
|---|---:|---|
| Surface directly to space as longwave radiation | 17 | Leaves the Earth–atmosphere system |
| Surface to atmosphere: absorbed radiation, turbulent/sensible heat and latent heat | 34 | Internal transfer; not an additional escape to space |
| Atmosphere to space as longwave radiation | 48 | Leaves the system; accounts for its 14 solar units plus the 34 received from the surface |

Thus the surface account is **51 = 17 + 34**, the atmosphere's net account is **48 = 14 + 34**, and the outward account is **100 = 35 reflected + 17 + 48**. Adding 17 + 34 + 48 as if all three leave the system double-counts the internal transfer. The 34 units include 6 radiative, 9 convection/turbulence and 19 latent-heat units in the textbook's simplified presentation; they are not all infrared radiation.

These rounded textbook numbers illustrate accounting, not an exact contemporary measurement. Fuller energy budgets include radiation emitted downward by the atmosphere and larger two-way surface–atmosphere exchanges ([NOAA energy budget][noaa-energy]). A steady balance is an idealisation; a persistent incoming–outgoing imbalance changes stored energy.

> **[2025 Q49][pyq2025c] — unresolved key conflict, flagged 23 November 2026:** The local corpus records **(c), all three**, including “below freezing everywhere” without an atmosphere. The earlier chapter endorsed that literal claim. A global-average estimate near −18°C does **not** establish that every place would remain below freezing: sunlit and dark surfaces can differ greatly ([NOAA greenhouse lesson][noaa-greenhouse]). Learn the physical distinction between **average** and **everywhere**. The corpus's answer is preserved, but this revision does not certify it or replace it with a new official key; compare the exact wording and booklet series with UPSC's official paper and key when accessible.

## 6. How is the atmosphere actually heated?

This is the most exam-tested process in this chapter. The answer is **not** what you might expect.

### 6.1 The indirect heating sequence

1. The Sun emits **shortwave radiation**.
2. The atmosphere is largely **transparent** to shortwave radiation — only about 14 units (of the incoming 100) are absorbed directly by the air.
3. About 51 units reach and warm the **Earth's surface**.
4. The warmed surface emits **longwave (infrared) radiation** — **terrestrial radiation** — and also transfers energy through sensible heat and evaporation.
5. Greenhouse gases (CO₂, water vapour, methane) in the atmosphere **absorb longwave radiation** efficiently.
6. This warms the lower atmosphere **from below, not from above**.

**Conclusion:** the lower atmosphere receives substantial energy from the warmed surface, through longwave absorption, sensible heat and latent heat. Direct absorption of sunlight also occurs, especially by ozone higher up. Do not extend “heated from below” into a claim that sunlight never heats atmospheric gases.

> **[2024 Q16][pyq2024a] (Answer: d):** Statement I says the atmosphere is heated *more* by incoming solar radiation than by terrestrial radiation — this is **FALSE** (the indirect heating sequence above shows the reverse). Statement II says CO₂ and other greenhouse gases are good absorbers of longwave radiation — this is **TRUE**. Answer (d): Statement I is incorrect, Statement II is correct. This is a critical thinking question: the common intuition ("sunlight heats the air") is wrong at the mechanistic level.

### 6.2 Mechanisms of heat transfer in the atmosphere

Once the lower atmosphere is warmed, heat is redistributed by three mechanisms:

| Mechanism | How it works | Where it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Conduction** | Heat moves by molecule-to-molecule contact; slow | Thin layer immediately above the surface |
| **Convection** | Warm air rises, cold air sinks; vertical movement | Dominant in the troposphere; drives clouds and storms |
| **Advection** | Horizontal transfer of heat by wind | Most day-to-day weather change in mid-latitudes; e.g., warm/cold waves |

### 6.3 The greenhouse effect

Greenhouse gases (CO₂, water vapour, methane, nitrous oxide) allow shortwave solar radiation to pass through but **trap** some outgoing longwave (terrestrial) radiation. This is the natural **greenhouse effect** — it keeps Earth's average surface temperature at about +15°C rather than −18°C (what it would be without an atmosphere). This is an estimate of a global average, not a claim that every location would be frozen at every time.

The **enhanced greenhouse effect** (from rising CO₂ and other gases due to human activity) is driving climate change — treated further in [Chapter 108](108-climate-science-impacts-and-responses.md).

## 7. Temperature distribution — why some places are hotter

### 7.1 By latitude

Since insolation decreases from equator to poles (angle and day-length effects), surface temperatures generally also decrease from equator to poles.

**Isotherms** are lines joining places of equal temperature on a map. In an ideal world they would run perfectly parallel to latitudes. In reality they bend because:
- Land heats and cools faster than water (see below).
- Ocean currents warm or cool coastal regions.
- Altitude reduces temperatures.

> **[2025 Q47][pyq2025b] (Answer: a — both correct, Statement II explains Statement I):** In January in the Northern Hemisphere, isotherms bend **equatorward** (southward) over landmasses, and **poleward** (northward) over oceans. Statement II explains why: in January (NH winter), oceans are **warmer** than the continents (water loses heat more slowly). Warmer ocean air pushes isotherms poleward; colder continental air pushes them equatorward. Both statements are correct, and Statement II is the correct explanation for Statement I.

### 7.2 By altitude — the lapse rate

Temperature **usually** decreases with height in the troposphere. A standard reference value is **6.5°C per 1,000 m**, about **1°C per 154 m**. The actual **environmental lapse rate** is the observed vertical temperature change at a place and time; it is not always 6.5°C/km and can reverse in an inversion.

Do not confuse that environmental profile with the cooling of a **rising parcel** of air. As pressure decreases with height, a rising parcel expands and does work on its surroundings, which cools it without requiring heat loss: **adiabatic cooling**. Unsaturated air cools at about 9.8°C/km; condensation releases latent heat, so saturated air generally cools more slowly, at a variable rate ([NOAA lapse-rate definitions][noaa-lapse]; see also the aviation-meteorology course in Sources). These rates determine whether a rising parcel stays warmer than its surroundings and continues rising. Mountain cold is not adequately explained as simply “farther from the ground's heat.”

### 7.3 By surface type — land vs. sea

This distinction is vital for the exam. It comes down to the **specific heat** of water versus land:

- **Water has a high specific heat** — it needs more energy to heat up by 1°C, and it also gives up heat more slowly. This moderates temperature change.
- **Land has a low specific heat** — it heats up and cools down quickly.

Consequences:
- **Continentality:** Areas deep inside large landmasses (e.g., central Asia, Siberia, interior India) experience extreme temperatures — very hot in summer, very cold in winter. The annual temperature range can exceed 60°C in northeastern Eurasia.
- **Maritime climate:** Coastal areas have mild, equable climates — not too hot in summer, not too cold in winter.

> **[2013 Q25][pyq2013] (Answer: a — 1 only):** Why is the annual temperature range higher in continental interiors than on coasts? Option 1 — *Thermal difference between land and water (specific heat)* — is correct. Option 2 (altitude variation) is incorrect (continents and oceans don't have fixed altitude differences). Option 3 (strong interior winds) is incorrect. Option 4 (heavy interior rains) is incorrect — interiors often receive less rain than coasts.

> **[2023 Q12][pyq2023b] (Answer: d — Statement I incorrect, Statement II correct):** Statement I says the temperature contrast between continents and oceans is greater in summer than in winter — this is **INCORRECT**. Here is why: in winter, water cools slowly (retains heat), so oceans stay relatively warm while continents cool sharply → the contrast is **larger in winter**. In summer, both heat up, but water heats more slowly, so continents are warmer — but the absolute difference is less extreme. Statement II (specific heat of water is higher than that of land) — CORRECT. The key exam trap is assuming summer is when land–sea contrast peaks. It doesn't; winter is when the difference is most stark.

### 7.4 Effect of ocean currents

- **Warm ocean currents** (e.g., Gulf Stream off Europe's Atlantic coast) raise the temperature of adjacent coastal land — western Europe is far warmer in winter than its latitude would predict.
- **Cold ocean currents** (e.g., Benguela off South Africa's west coast, Labrador off Canada) cool adjacent coastlines and can cause fog or aridity.

## 8. Temperature inversion

Under normal conditions, temperature decreases with altitude. A **temperature inversion** is when this reverses: **temperature rises with height** for some layer. This is the exception, not the rule.

### 8.1 How inversions form

The most common type forms on **long, clear, calm winter nights**:
1. The surface loses heat rapidly by radiation (no cloud cover to trap it).
2. The ground becomes colder than the air above.
3. A shallow layer of cold, dense air sits at the surface; warmer air sits above. Temperature rises with height — an inversion.

Also common in **polar regions** throughout the year.

### 8.2 Why inversions matter

- **Stability:** Inversions trap the lower air in place, preventing vertical mixing and convection.
- **Air quality:** Smoke, dust and pollutants concentrate in the inversion layer, causing dense smog and reduced visibility.
- **Fog:** Winter fog in Indo-Gangetic Plain and Delhi is partly a consequence of surface inversions — cold, dense air at the surface holds moisture and particles.
- **Agriculture:** Cold air drains downhill (like water) and pools in valleys, causing frost at the valley floor while upper slopes stay frost-free. Farmers and orchardists on slopes have an advantage.

## 9. Connecting the whole picture

| Why is this place hotter/colder? | The mechanism |
|---|---|
| Equator vs. poles | Latitude → angle and duration of insolation → more energy per unit area at equator |
| Mountain summit vs. valley floor | Observed tropospheric lapse rate; rising air expands and cools as pressure falls, with radiation and mixing also shaping the profile |
| Continental interior vs. coast | Land-sea specific heat difference → continents amplify extremes; coasts are moderated |
| Western Europe warm for latitude | Warm ocean current (Gulf Stream) moderating winters |
| Cold Namib / Atacama coasts | Cold upwelling ocean current reducing coastal temperature |
| Valley frost, upper slope safe | Temperature inversion trapping cold dense air at the valley floor |
| NH January: oceans warmer than land | Land loses heat fast in winter; water retains heat → oceans lag behind cooling |
| Troposphere thicker at equator | Strong convection at equator pushes tropopause higher |

<a id="common-confusions"></a>

## 10. Common confusions

| Confusion | Clarification |
|---|---|
| **Only sunlight heats the lower air** | The surface supplies energy through longwave radiation, sensible heat and latent heat. Some solar radiation is also absorbed directly by the atmosphere. |
| **Temperature always decreases with altitude** | Only in the troposphere (normal lapse rate). In the stratosphere, temperature *increases* (ozone absorbs UV). Inversions also reverse the normal trend locally. |
| **Summer → bigger land–sea contrast** | Wrong. The contrast is bigger in winter. In winter, water stays warm while land cools rapidly; this is when the gap is largest. |
| **High albedo = warm** | Opposite. High-albedo surfaces (snow, ice) reflect more energy and stay cooler. Low-albedo surfaces (oceans, forests) absorb more and warm more. |
| **The ozone layer blocks all UV** | No. It absorbs most *harmful* UV (UV-B and UV-C), while most UV-A reaches the surface. Without the ozone layer, UV radiation would be intense enough to destroy most surface life. |
| **Greenhouse effect is unnatural** | The natural greenhouse effect is what keeps Earth habitable. The *enhanced* (anthropogenic) greenhouse effect — from rising CO₂ — is the climate problem. |
| **Environmental and adiabatic lapse rates are identical** | No. The 6.5°C/km reference environmental rate describes surroundings; the dry adiabatic parcel rate is about 9.8°C/km. Actual environmental and saturated-parcel rates vary. |

## 11. India lens

India's temperature patterns illustrate this chapter's mechanisms directly:

- **Rajasthan desert in summer:** Dry ground and sparse vegetation limit evaporative cooling; strong sunshine and continentality favour intense heating. Sand is generally more reflective than water or dark vegetation, so “low albedo sand” is not a sound explanation for desert heat.
- **Kerala coast in summer:** Kerala lies substantially south of Rajasthan. Maritime influence and humidity moderate its temperature pattern; coastal and inland places at comparable latitudes provide a cleaner comparison of continentality alone.
- **Himalayas:** Altitude-driven cold at any latitude. Peaks above 8,000 m are permanently glaciated even though they lie at 28–36°N.
- **Winter fog in the Indo-Gangetic Plain:** A product of temperature inversion. Cold, stable air near the surface traps moisture and pollutants; dense fog disrupts transport from December to February.
- **Peninsular India — equable climate:** Surrounded by sea on three sides; annual temperature range is small compared to the North Indian plains.
- **Central India — continental extremes:** Far from the sea, Nagpur and Bhopal experience high summer temperatures and cold winters relative to coastal cities at similar latitudes.

## 12. Summary of key concepts and takeaways

- Atmospheric layers are distinguished by temperature trends; the ionosphere describes ionisation and overlaps those layers rather than forming a separate temperature slab.
- The surface and atmosphere exchange energy through radiation, sensible heat and latent heat. Internal transfers must not be counted again as energy escaping to space.
- Temperature patterns arise from solar angle/day length, height, land–water response, currents and local conditions. A global-average statement does not describe every place.
- An environmental lapse rate describes the surrounding air; an adiabatic rate describes a moving parcel. Inversions reverse the usual local temperature trend and suppress mixing.

## 1. Orientation

Wind, rain and storms are not random. They follow from two things established in Chapter 010: the unequal heating of the Earth's surface, and the properties of air. This chapter builds the mechanism connecting heat to movement — why air flows, why it rises or sinks, why it carries moisture, and what happens when unlike air masses meet.

Understanding pressure drives the whole story. Air always moves from high pressure to low pressure, like water flowing downhill. The speed of this flow depends on how steep the pressure gradient is. What complicates things — and makes the exam interesting — is the **Coriolis force**, which deflects that movement and creates the rotating patterns we call winds, cyclones and weather systems.

---

## 2. Atmospheric pressure and pressure belts

**Atmospheric pressure** is the weight of the overlying column of air pressing down on a surface. Where air warms and rises, pressure is low; where air cools and sinks, pressure is high.

The **pressure gradient force** drives air from high pressure toward low pressure. The steeper the gradient (the closer the isobars on a map), the stronger the wind.

### 2.1 Global pressure belts

Because insolation is unequal across latitudes, four distinct pressure belts form around the Earth ([NCERT Cl. 11, Ch. 10][ncert11-circ]):

| Belt | Approximate latitude | Why it forms | Air movement |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Equatorial Low (ITCZ)** | Near 0° (equator) | Intense solar heating; air rises by convection | Rising; low pressure; convergence of trade winds |
| **Subtropical High** | ~30° N and S | Rising equatorial air cools, subsides | Sinking; high pressure; dry, desert-forming |
| **Sub-polar Low** | ~60° N and S | Cold polar air meets warm westerlies; air rises | Rising; low pressure; stormy, variable weather |
| **Polar High** | 90° N and S (poles) | Extreme cold; air sinks and spreads out | Sinking; high pressure; cold and dry |

**Key rule:** Rising air → low pressure → cloudy, rainy, unstable weather. Sinking air → high pressure → clear skies, dry, stable weather. The great subtropical deserts (Sahara, Arabian, Australian) exist under permanent high pressure where dry, subsiding air prevents rainfall.

**Seasonal shift:** The pressure belts are not fixed — they shift northward in Northern Hemisphere summer and southward in winter, tracking the Sun's apparent migration. This seasonal shift is what drives the monsoon (see Chapter 012).

### 2.2 The Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ)

The ITCZ is where the trade winds of the two hemispheres converge near the equator. It is a zone of intense uplift, cloud formation and heavy rainfall. It is not a fixed line — it migrates with the seasons, reaching as far as 20°N over South Asia in July and retreating to near the equator in January.

## 3. Global wind belts

From the pressure belts, three pairs of planetary winds emerge ([NCERT Cl. 11, Ch. 10][ncert11-circ]):

| Wind belt | From | To | Hemisphere | Modified by Coriolis to become |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Trade winds** | Subtropical High (~30°) | Equatorial Low (0°) | Both | NE trades (NH); SE trades (SH) |
| **Westerlies** | Subtropical High (~30°) | Sub-polar Low (~60°) | Both | Blow from SW in NH; from NW in SH |
| **Polar easterlies** | Polar High (90°) | Sub-polar Low (~60°) | Both | Blow from NE in NH; from SE in SH |

> **[2015 Q36][pyq2015a] (Answer: b — 2 only):** Statement 1 says westerlies blow between 30°N and 60°S — this is INCORRECT. Westerlies blow between 30° and 60° in BOTH hemispheres (not from one to the other). Statement 2 says moist air masses causing winter rains in NW India are part of the westerlies — this is CORRECT. Western Disturbances are extra-tropical cyclones embedded in the upper-level westerly flow that bring winter precipitation to north-west India and the western Himalayas.

> **[2021 Q51][pyq2021] (Answer: c — both):** In the tropical zone, the western sections of oceans are warmer than eastern sections owing to trade winds — TRUE. Trade winds blow from east to west, pushing warm surface water westward, so the western ocean accumulates warm water (e.g., western Pacific is warmer than the eastern Pacific). In the temperate zone, westerlies make eastern sections of oceans warmer than western sections — TRUE. Westerlies blow from west to east, pushing warm water toward eastern ocean boundaries (e.g., eastern Pacific/Atlantic receive warm currents, while western sections receive cold return flows).

> **[2015 Q80][pyq2015c] (Answer: b — Equatorial):** The passage describes: each day nearly the same, mornings clear and bright with sea breeze, afternoon heat, dark clouds with thunder and lightning, then rain that is soon over. This is **convectional rainfall** in the **equatorial climate** — daily intense afternoon storms driven by intense surface heating and rising moist air.

## 4. The three-cell circulation model

Each hemisphere has three convective cells linking the pressure belts ([NCERT Cl. 11, Ch. 10][ncert11-circ]):

- **Hadley cell (0°–30°):** Air rises at the ITCZ (~14 km), moves poleward at altitude, sinks at the subtropical high, and returns to the equator as trade winds. It is the most vigorous cell, driven by intense equatorial heating.
- **Ferrel cell (30°–60°):** Air at the surface flows poleward as westerlies, rises at the sub-polar low, returns equatorward at altitude, and descends at the subtropical high. This is an indirect thermal cell — thermally driven by the cells on either side.
- **Polar cell (60°–90°):** Cold air sinks at the poles, flows equatorward as polar easterlies, and rises at the sub-polar low.

Where the cells meet at the sub-polar low, warm westerly air (from the Ferrel cell) meets cold polar air (from the Polar cell) — this boundary is the **polar front**, the birthplace of temperate cyclones.

## 5. The Coriolis effect

The **Coriolis force** is not a real force — it is an apparent deflection caused by Earth's rotation, experienced by any object moving across Earth's surface ([NCERT Cl. 11, Ch. 10][ncert11-circ]).

**How it works:** Earth rotates from west to east. A parcel of air moving from the equator toward the North Pole moves over Earth below it, which is rotating faster at lower latitudes. From an observer on the surface, the air parcel appears to deflect to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere.

**Key properties:**
- Deflects air **to the right in the Northern Hemisphere** and **to the left in the Southern Hemisphere**.
- **Maximum at the poles** (Coriolis force = 2mv Ω sin φ; at φ = 90°, sin 90° = 1, maximum).
- **Absent at the equator** (at φ = 0°, sin 0° = 0 — no deflection). This is why tropical cyclones cannot form within about 5° of the equator; there is insufficient Coriolis deflection to initiate rotation.
- Increases with **wind velocity** (the faster the air moves, the greater the apparent deflection).
- Acts at right angles to the direction of motion.

**Ferrel's law:** Winds are deflected to the right of their direction of motion in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere. This applies to ALL moving fluids (wind and ocean currents).

> **[2024 Q60][pyq2024] (Answer: c — both):** Statement 1: Coriolis force increases with increase in wind velocity — TRUE. Statement 2: Coriolis force is maximum at the poles and absent at the equator — TRUE. Both statements are correct.

**Effect on winds:** Instead of flowing straight from high to low pressure, winds are deflected until (in the upper atmosphere) they flow **parallel to isobars** — the **geostrophic wind**. Near the surface, friction slows the wind and reduces the Coriolis deflection, so surface winds cross isobars at a small angle toward low pressure.

**Effect on cyclones:** Coriolis deflection causes air flowing into a low-pressure centre to rotate — counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere, clockwise in the Southern Hemisphere. This is the basis of cyclonic circulation.

## 6. Jet streams

**Jet streams** are narrow, fast-moving ribbons of wind in the upper troposphere (6–12 km altitude), blowing from west to east at speeds of 100–300 km/h ([NCERT Cl. 11, Ch. 10][ncert11-circ]).

**Types:**

| Jet stream | Location | Season in India | Key role |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Westerly (Polar) jet** | Along the polar front, ~30–60°N | Winter | Steers Western Disturbances into north-west India; also influences monsoon withdrawal |
| **Tropical Easterly jet** | ~15°N, over peninsular India | Summer (June–September) | Linked to onset and maintenance of the south-west monsoon |
| **Sub-tropical jet** | ~25–30°N | — | Influences monsoon onset/withdrawal over India |

> **[2020 Q37][pyq2020] (Answer: c — 2 only):** Statement 1: Jet streams occur in the Northern Hemisphere only — FALSE. Jet streams exist in both hemispheres (and in the atmospheres of other planets). Statement 2: Only some cyclones develop an eye — TRUE. Well-developed tropical cyclones develop an eye; weaker tropical cyclones and all temperate (extra-tropical) cyclones do not have a defined eye. Statement 3: The temperature inside the eye of a cyclone is nearly 10°C *lesser* than surroundings — FALSE. The eye is **warmer** than its surroundings (tropical cyclones are warm-core systems). The 10°C difference exists but the direction is reversed: the eye is warmer, not colder.

## 7. Humidity and condensation

### 7.1 Humidity

**Humidity** is the amount of water vapour in the air ([NCERT Cl. 11, Ch. 11][ncert11-water]).

| Type | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Absolute humidity** | Mass of water vapour per unit volume of air (g/m³) |
| **Relative humidity** | Water vapour present as a percentage of the maximum the air can hold at that temperature |
| **Specific humidity** | Mass of water vapour per unit mass of moist air (g/kg) |

Warm air can hold more water vapour than cold air. When air cools without losing any water vapour, its relative humidity rises — eventually reaching 100% at the **dew point temperature**, at which condensation begins.

Condensation requires **hygroscopic nuclei** — tiny particles like dust, salt, pollen or smoke around which water vapour can condense. This is why polluted air produces more cloud and fog than clean air.

### 7.2 Condensation forms

| Form | Where it appears | Condition |
|---|---|---|
| **Dew** | On cool surfaces (grass, leaves) | Clear, calm nights; surface cools to dew point above freezing |
| **Frost** | On surfaces that cool below freezing | Dew point at or below 0°C |
| **Fog** | Near the ground | Air temperature falls to dew point; visibility under 1 km |
| **Mist** | Near the ground | Thinner than fog; visibility 1–2 km |
| **Clouds** | In free air at altitude | Rising air cools to dew point; droplets or ice crystals form |

> **[2019 Q44][pyq2019] (Answer: b):** Why are dewdrops not formed on a cloudy night? Because clouds **reflect back the Earth's radiation** (longwave), keeping the surface from cooling to the dew point. On a clear night, the surface radiates heat freely into space, cools below the dew point, and dew forms. Clouds act as a thermal blanket — they trap outgoing radiation and prevent the surface cooling that condensation requires.

### 7.3 Cloud types

Clouds are named by altitude and form ([NCERT Cl. 11, Ch. 11][ncert11-water]):

| Cloud type | Altitude | Appearance | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cirrus** | Very high (8,000–12,000 m) | Thin, wispy, feathery | Ice crystals; indicate approaching fronts |
| **Cumulus** | Medium (4,000–7,000 m) | Cotton-wool heaps, flat base | Fair weather; grows to cumulonimbus in instability |
| **Stratus** | Low sheets | Layered, uniform grey | Drizzle; forms by gentle lifting or cooling |
| **Nimbus** | Low to medium | Dark, formless, dense | Rain cloud; heavy continuous rain |
| **Cumulonimbus** | Ground to 12,000+ m | Towering anvil-shaped | Thunderstorms, lightning, hail |

> **[2022 Q57][pyq2022] (Answer: d — neither):** Statement 1: High clouds primarily reflect solar radiation and cool the surface — FALSE. High thin clouds (cirrus) allow most solar radiation through but efficiently trap outgoing longwave radiation from below. Their net effect is **warming**, not cooling. Statement 2: Low clouds have high absorption of infrared radiation from Earth's surface and cause warming — FALSE. Low clouds (stratus, stratocumulus) primarily **reflect** incoming solar radiation (high albedo), which cools the surface. Their net effect is **cooling**. Neither statement correctly describes these clouds.

> **[2013 Q95][pyq2013] (Answer: d — none of the above):** Thunder in a thunderstorm is produced by the rapid expansion of superheated air around a lightning bolt — not by meeting clouds, nor by lightning "separating" clouds, nor by upward air movement. The electrical discharge of lightning superheats the surrounding air to ~30,000°C in a fraction of a second; the air expands explosively, producing a pressure wave we hear as thunder. None of the given options (1, 2, or 3) correctly describes this mechanism.

> **[2025 Q65][pyq2025] (Answer: a):** Artificial cloud seeding to cause rainfall uses **silver iodide** and **potassium iodide** — both have a crystal structure similar to ice that promotes ice nucleation in supercooled clouds. Silver iodide is the primary reagent; it was first used in cloud seeding experiments by Bernard Vonnegut in 1946.

## 8. Rainfall types

Rainfall requires three ingredients: a moisture source, a mechanism to lift air to its dew point, and condensation nuclei. The lifting mechanism determines the type of rainfall ([NCERT Cl. 11, Ch. 11][ncert11-water]):

### 8.1 Convectional rainfall

**Mechanism:** Intense solar heating warms the surface; warm moist air rises rapidly, cools to its dew point, and condenses into towering cumulonimbus clouds.

**Characteristics:** Heavy, sudden, short-lived; often accompanied by thunder and lightning; in the afternoon or early evening; localized.

**Where:** Equatorial regions (daily); continental interiors in summer; monsoon India.

### 8.2 Orographic (relief) rainfall

**Mechanism:** Moist air is forced up a mountain barrier (orographic lifting), cools adiabatically, and condenses on the windward side. As the air descends on the leeward side, it warms and dries — the **rain shadow**.

**Where:** Windward slopes of mountain ranges globally (Western Ghats, Himalayas, Khasi Hills receive huge orographic rainfall; Deccan plateau sits in the rain shadow of the Western Ghats).

**Example:** Mawsynram and Cherrapunji (Meghalaya) are among the wettest places on Earth due to orographic lifting of south-west monsoon winds against the Khasi Hills.

### 8.3 Cyclonic (frontal) rainfall

**Mechanism:** Warm air is lifted over cold air along a front (boundary between air masses). The warm air cools to its dew point and produces widespread, steady rain.

**Where:** Extra-tropical (temperate) cyclones in mid-latitudes; Western Disturbances in India.

## 9. Air masses and fronts

### 9.1 Air masses

An **air mass** is a large body of air (thousands of kilometres across) with uniform temperature and humidity throughout. Air masses form by sitting over a source region for weeks, taking on the region's characteristics.

| Source region | Air mass type | Properties |
|---|---|---|
| Tropical oceans | Maritime Tropical (mT) | Warm, moist |
| Tropical deserts | Continental Tropical (cT) | Hot, dry |
| Polar oceans | Maritime Polar (mP) | Cold, moist |
| Polar/Arctic land | Continental Polar (cP) | Cold, dry |

### 9.2 Fronts

A **front** is the boundary between two air masses of different temperature and humidity. Fronts are zones of weather change — clouds, precipitation and wind shifts. Types:

| Front | What moves | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| **Warm front** | Warm air advancing over cold | Warm air slides gently over cold; slow, gradual lifting; widespread cloud (cirrus → stratus); steady, prolonged rain; warm weather follows |
| **Cold front** | Cold air advancing under warm | Cold air wedges sharply under warm; rapid, vigorous lifting; cumulonimbus; heavy brief rain; squalls; cold weather follows |
| **Occluded front** | Cold front overtakes warm front | Warm air completely lifted off the surface; complex weather |
| **Stationary front** | Neither air mass advancing | Prolonged rain along the boundary |

## 10. Weather systems: tropical cyclones

### 10.1 Formation conditions

A tropical cyclone needs ([Anantam IAS][anantamias-cyc]):

1. **Sea surface temperature ≥ 27°C** to a depth of at least 50–60 m — to supply the latent heat that drives the storm.
2. **Location between 5° and 30° latitude** — below 5°, Coriolis force is too weak; above 30°, SSTs are usually too cold.
3. **Low vertical wind shear** — strong shear would disrupt the storm's vertical structure.
4. **High relative humidity** at mid-tropospheric levels (~5 km) to fuel thunderstorm clusters.
5. **A pre-existing disturbance** — an easterly wave or monsoon trough to trigger the system.
6. **Upper-level divergence** to allow continuous outflow at the top of the system.

### 10.2 Structure

- **Eye:** Calm, clear centre, 20–50 km wide. Light winds; lowest pressure; subsiding air; counterintuitively warm and clear — this is the most dangerous misconception for anyone caught in a cyclone.
- **Eyewall:** Ring of towering cumulonimbus immediately around the eye. Strongest winds (can exceed 220 km/h) and heaviest rain.
- **Rainbands:** Spiral bands of thunderstorms extending hundreds of km outward.
- **Warm-core structure:** The cyclone is warmest at its centre throughout its depth (unlike temperate cyclones, which have different air masses side by side).

**Rotation:** Counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere, clockwise in the Southern Hemisphere (Coriolis deflection).

**Energy source:** Latent heat released when water vapour (evaporated over warm ocean) condenses. The cyclone weakens rapidly when it moves over land or cold water (fuel cut off).

### 10.3 Naming conventions

| Region | Local name |
|---|---|
| Bay of Bengal / Arabian Sea | Cyclone |
| Western North Pacific | Typhoon |
| North Atlantic / Eastern Pacific | Hurricane |

All are the same phenomenon; the name varies only by ocean basin.

### 10.4 Why some regions have no tropical cyclones

> **[2015 Q78][pyq2015b] (Answer: b):** The South Atlantic and South-Eastern Pacific do not generate tropical cyclones. The reason is that the **ITCZ seldom occurs** in those regions. Without the ITCZ, there is no sustained low-pressure trough to provide the initial disturbance that can develop into a cyclone. Additionally, those ocean basins have relatively cool SSTs and high wind shear — but the ITCZ absence is the primary stated reason. (Option c — Coriolis too weak — is wrong; those regions are well away from the equator.)

**Indian coasts:** Tropical cyclones most commonly affect the east coast of India (Bay of Bengal) in the post-monsoon season (October–December). The Arabian Sea also generates cyclones, though less frequently; they tend to be more intense in recent years.

## 11. Weather systems: temperate cyclones

### 11.1 Formation and structure

Temperate (extra-tropical) cyclones form at the **polar front** (60° latitude), where warm westerlies meet cold polar air. Their energy source is the temperature contrast between air masses, not ocean heat ([Anantam IAS][anantamias-cyc]).

**Norwegian cyclone model (six stages):**
1. A stationary front separates warm and cold air.
2. A wave disturbance forms — warm air pushes poleward; cold air pushes equatorward.
3. An open wave develops: a **warm front** ahead of the low, a **cold front** behind.
4. Cold front tightens the warm sector as it moves faster.
5. Cold front overtakes the warm front — **occlusion**; warm air is completely lifted.
6. The surface energy source is cut off; the system dissipates.

### 11.2 Comparison: tropical vs. temperate cyclones

| Feature | Tropical cyclone | Temperate cyclone |
|---|---|---|
| Latitude | 5°–30° | 35°–65° |
| Energy source | Latent heat (ocean evaporation) | Temperature contrast (fronts) |
| Core temperature | Warm-core | Cold-core (temperature contrast across front) |
| Eye | Yes (in well-developed systems) | No |
| Size | 100–500 km | 1,000–2,000 km |
| Wind speed | Very high (can exceed 300 km/h) | Moderate (rarely above 150 km/h) |
| Movement | Generally west to east (steered by trade winds and then westerlies) | West to east (westerlies) |
| Season | Mainly post-monsoon and pre-monsoon | All year (more active in winter) |
| Rainfall | Heavy concentrated near centre | Widespread along fronts |
| Duration | 7–10 days | Days to weeks |
| India link | Bay of Bengal/Arabian Sea cyclones | Western Disturbances |

### 11.3 Western Disturbances

Western Disturbances are extra-tropical cyclones that form over the Mediterranean, Caspian or Black Sea and travel eastward embedded in the upper westerly flow, reaching north-west India in winter. They bring rain to the Punjab, Himachal Pradesh, Jammu & Kashmir and snow to the western Himalayas — vital for the winter wheat crop and Himalayan glaciers.

## 12. Anticyclones

An **anticyclone** is a high-pressure system with outward-spiralling winds. Air descends in the centre (warming adiabatically), flows outward, and is deflected by the Coriolis effect:

- **Clockwise** in the Northern Hemisphere.
- **Counterclockwise** in the Southern Hemisphere.

Anticyclones bring settled, calm, clear weather because descending air inhibits cloud formation. The subtropical high-pressure belts (~30° N and S) are semi-permanent anticyclones — they produce the world's major hot deserts.

## 13. Connecting the whole picture

Tracing pressure and moisture through a weather system is the chapter's learning outcome. Here is the full sequence for a mid-latitude weather system arriving in north-west India:

1. **Mediterranean Sea:** Cold polar air meets warm tropical air → polar front forms → Western Disturbance triggers.
2. **Upper troposphere:** Westerly jet stream steers the system eastward at 100–300 km/h.
3. **Ahead of the warm front:** Cirrus clouds appear first, followed by thickening stratus; steady rain begins.
4. **Cold front passage:** Cumulonimbus; heavy brief rain; wind shift; temperature drop.
5. **Post-frontal anticyclone:** Clear, cold, calm weather.

The same process, faster and more violent, produces Bay of Bengal cyclones in October–November — but driven by latent heat rather than front contrast.

## 14. Common confusions

| Confusion | Clarification |
|---|---|
| **Cyclone rotates clockwise in NH** | Wrong. Cyclones rotate counterclockwise in the NH (air deflects right into the low, creating counterclockwise flow). Anticyclones rotate clockwise in the NH. |
| **Eye is the coldest part of a cyclone** | Wrong. The eye is the warmest part — a tropical cyclone is warm-core. The dangerous misconception is thinking the calm eye means the storm has passed. |
| **Trade winds blow from east to west** | Correct, but Coriolis deflects them to north-east (NH) and south-east (SH) — they blow TOWARD the equator, not parallel to it. |
| **Westerlies blow from west to east** | Correct. They blow from the subtropical high (~30°) toward the sub-polar low (~60°), deflected to become south-westerlies in the NH. |
| **Orographic rain falls on leeward side** | Wrong. Orographic rain falls on the windward side. The leeward side is the rain shadow — dry. |
| **High cloud = cooling** | Not necessarily. Thin high clouds (cirrus) trap outgoing longwave radiation and have a net warming effect. Thick high clouds (tops of cumulonimbus) can reflect insolation and cool. |
| **Tropical cyclones form at the equator** | Wrong. They need Coriolis force to rotate — which is absent at the equator. They form between 5° and 30° latitude. |
| **Relative humidity falls when it rains** | Counterintuitively, relative humidity is 100% when it rains, not lower. It falls when air warms (capacity increases) or when dry air mixes in. |

## 15. India lens

India sits at the junction of multiple circulation systems — this is what makes its climate so distinctive:

- **South-west monsoon winds:** SE trade winds of the Southern Hemisphere cross the equator, deflect right (Coriolis, NH) and become south-west monsoon winds bringing 75–90% of India's annual rainfall. The tropical easterly jet at ~15°N helps sustain the monsoon circulation.
- **North-east monsoon:** After the ITCZ retreats south in October, dry north-east winds blow from the land toward the ocean — bringing rain only to the Tamil Nadu coast (Coromandel), which gets its main rainfall in October–December from the north-east monsoon and Bay of Bengal cyclones.
- **Western Disturbances:** Extra-tropical cyclones from the Mediterranean arrive in December–March, bringing winter rain to north-west India and snow to the western Himalayas — essential for Rabi crops (wheat, mustard).
- **Bay of Bengal cyclones:** Most active in October–November (post-monsoon); strike Odisha, Andhra Pradesh and Bangladesh. Some cross to the Arabian Sea. Bay of Bengal cyclones are more frequent; Arabian Sea cyclones have become more intense in recent years.
- **India's single ITCZ position:** In July, the ITCZ lies roughly over the Gangetic plain, dragging monsoon winds deep into the subcontinent. In January, it retreats to ~10°S.

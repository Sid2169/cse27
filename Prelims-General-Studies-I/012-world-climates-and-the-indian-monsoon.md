## 3. Foundations and key terms

### 3.1 Weather, climate and climate regions

**Weather** is the state of the atmosphere at a place over hours to days: today's temperature, a thunderstorm, a heat wave. **Climate** is the typical pattern of weather over many years, described by averages and by how conditions swing through the year. The convention is to average over about 30 years; NOAA's ENSO index, for example, uses 30-year base periods ([CPC][cpc-oni]). A **climate region** is an area whose averages and seasonal rhythm are alike enough to share one label.

A cold, foggy morning in Delhi is weather; the fact that Delhi's winters are far cooler than Mumbai's is climate. A monsoon that ends 13% short of its average, as in 2026, is a departure from the climate, not a new climate. Sections B10 and C5 return to this distinction.

### 3.2 Six controls on a place's climate

Six controls explain most differences between places. Each is taught in depth elsewhere; here they are only named, because Parts A and B keep using them.

1. **Latitude** sets the Sun's angle and day length, and so the heat received ([Chapter 010](010-atmospheric-structure-and-heat-balance.md)). NCERT notes that places nearer the equator are hot all year, while areas north of the Tropic of Cancer have greater temperature extremes ([NCERT][ncert-ipe]).
2. **Altitude.** Temperature falls with height; the rate is the *lapse rate* ([Chapter 010](010-atmospheric-structure-and-heat-balance.md)). Agra and Darjiling lie at about the same latitude, yet their January temperatures are about 16 °C and 4 °C ([NCERT][ncert-ipe]).
3. **Distance from the sea (continentality).** Water warms and cools more slowly than land, so coasts are moderate and interiors extreme ([NCERT][ncert-ipe]). The thermal difference between land and water is the reason the annual temperature range is larger inside continents than on their coasts ([2013 Q25][pyq2013]).
4. **Pressure belts and prevailing winds** ([Chapter 011](011-winds-rainfall-and-weather-systems.md)). Rising air (equatorial low, sub-polar lows) is cloudy and rainy; sinking air (subtropical highs) is dry. The belts shift north in the northern summer and south in winter, so many places sit in one belt in one season and in another belt in the opposite season. That single idea explains most of Part A and all of Part B.
5. **Ocean currents** ([Chapter 013](013-ocean-properties-and-circulation.md)). Warm currents warm and moisten the air above them; cold currents cool and dry it.
6. **Relief.** Mountains force air upward, so the windward side is wet and the lee side dry (a *rain shadow*), and they block cold or moist air from passing. NCERT's example: the windward Western Ghats and Assam receive heavy June–September rain while the leeward southern plateau stays dry ([NCERT][ncert-ipe]).

## Part A — World climates

### A1. Why classify, and the idea behind Köppen's scheme

The Earth has too many local climates to learn one by one, so geographers group places that behave alike. The Köppen scheme, introduced by Köppen in 1900 and revised by Geiger in the 1950s and 1960s ([Kottek et al. 2006][kottek]), is **empirical**: it starts from measurements (monthly temperature and rainfall) and draws its boundaries where natural vegetation changes, because vegetation is a visible record of climate. The NCERT chapter presents it as five groups, four defined by temperature and one (dry climates) by rainfall ([textbook scheme, as summarised in study notes][notes-edzy]).

Two cautions apply throughout. A classification describes **averages**, not any one year's weather. And its boundaries are lines drawn across gradual change, so places near a boundary share traits of both neighbours.

### A2. Reading a Köppen code

A code has up to three letters. Read left to right, from broad to specific.

**First letter: the group.** The definitions below are those of Kottek et al. ([2006][kottek]); the NCERT chapter uses the same thresholds ([study notes][notes-bharat]).

| Letter | Group | Defining test | What it means on the ground |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Tropical (equatorial) humid | Coldest month ≥ 18 °C | Hot all year; no winter |
| B | Dry | Annual rainfall below a threshold that rises with temperature and depends on the season in which the rain falls | Evaporation demand exceeds rainfall |
| C | Warm temperate (mild) | Coldest month between −3 °C and 18 °C | Mild winters, distinct summers |
| D | Cold snow-forest | Coldest month ≤ −3 °C (and warmest month above 10 °C) | Cold, snowy winters; large annual range |
| E | Polar (cold) | Warmest month below 10 °C | No true summer; tundra or ice |

Group B is the only one defined by rainfall. The threshold rises with temperature because warm air and warm ground lose more water to evaporation, so a warm place needs more rain than a cool one before it counts as "not dry".

**Second letter: the rainfall pattern** (for B and E, the kind of dryness or cold), and **third letter: the temperature pattern**.

| Position | Letter | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Second | f | No dry season ("fully humid") |
| | m | Monsoon: heavy rain in the high-Sun season and a short dry season (group A only) |
| | w | Dry winter |
| | s | Dry summer |
| | W / S | In group B: desert / steppe (semi-arid) |
| | T / F | In group E: tundra / permanent frost (ice cap) |
| Third | h / k | In group B: hot / cold dry climate (annual mean ≥ 18 °C / below) |
| | a | Hot summer (warmest month ≥ 22 °C) |
| | b | Warm summer (warmest month below 22 °C, at least four months ≥ 10 °C) |
| | c | Cool summer, cold winter (fewer than four months ≥ 10 °C) |
| | d | Extremely cold winter (coldest month ≤ −38 °C) |

NCERT's tables use shorter forms (for example **Cs** and **Df**) that merge the a/b/c variants. This chapter follows those shorter forms except where the third letter matters.

**Worked examples.** The numbers are invented to show the reasoning.

- *Station 1.* Coldest month 22 °C; rain in every month. Coldest month ≥ 18 °C → **A**. No dry season → **f**. Code **Af**: hot and wet all year.
- *Station 2.* Coldest month 8 °C, warmest 26 °C; nearly all rain falls in the cooler months and summer is dry. Coldest month between −3 and 18 °C → **C**. Dry summer → **s**. Warmest month ≥ 22 °C → **a**. Code **Csa**: the Mediterranean pattern.
- *Station 3.* Coldest month −20 °C; only three months above 10 °C; precipitation in every month. Coldest month ≤ −3 °C → **D**. No dry season → **f**. Fewer than four months ≥ 10 °C → **c**. Code **Dfc**: a subarctic climate.

### A3. The climate types, group by group

The tables follow the textbook scheme. The figures are the textbook's approximate values, cross-checked against two independent sets of study notes ([notes 1][notes-bharat], [notes 2][notes-leverage]); the official NCERT chapter could not be retrieved (see Sources).

**Group A: tropical humid**

| Type | Signature | Where | Why there |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Af** tropical wet | Hot all year (daily highs near 30 °C, lows near 20 °C), tiny annual range, rain in every month, usually as afternoon thunderstorms; evergreen forest | Amazon basin, western equatorial Africa, East Indies islands | The ITCZ's rising air stays near-overhead all year |
| **Am** tropical monsoon | Heavy rain in the high-Sun season, a short dry season; winds reverse with the season | Indian subcontinent, north-eastern South America, northern Australia | The ITCZ migrates over the land in summer and the winds reverse (Part B) |
| **Aw** tropical wet-and-dry (savanna) | A long dry season; less and more variable rain than Af or Am; deciduous forest and tree-studded grassland | Bands on the poleward sides of the Af regions: Brazilian highlands, Sudan, southern Central Africa | The ITCZ is overhead only in the high-Sun season; in the low-Sun season it has moved away and drier air takes over |

The unifying idea: A climates lie where the Sun is high all year (Af) or for most of it (Am, Aw). The farther from the equator, the longer the ITCZ is absent and the longer the dry season.

**Group B: dry**

| Type | Signature | Where | Why there |
|---|---|---|---|
| **BWh, BSh** hot desert, hot steppe | Very little rain, often in short intense showers; high daily and annual ranges | Roughly 15°–35° latitude: the Sahara, Arabia, the Thar; also west coasts beside cold currents | Air sinks under the subtropical highs, warming and drying as it descends; a cold current chills the air above it so that it cannot rise and make rain |
| **BWk, BSk** cold desert, cold steppe | Dry, with a large annual range; steppe is grassland, and in Central Asia the home of nomadic herders | Continental interiors and mountain-ringed basins at roughly 35°–60° | Far from the ocean's moisture, or in a rain shadow |

*BW* is true desert; *BS* (steppe) is the semi-arid fringe that links deserts to wetter climates.

**Groups C, D, E and highland**

| Type | Signature | Where | Why there |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cs** Mediterranean | Dry summer, mild wet winter; about 35–90 cm a year | West sides of continents at roughly 30°–40°: the Mediterranean basin, central California, central Chile, south-western Australia | The subtropical high covers the area in summer (dry); the westerly storm track moves in during winter (rain) |
| **Cfa** humid subtropical | Hot humid summer, mild winter, rain in all seasons; about 75–150 cm | East sides of continents: south-eastern USA, southern and eastern China, southern Japan, north-eastern Argentina, east coast of Australia | Moist air from warm seas reaches east coasts in every season |
| **Cwa** dry-winter humid subtropical | Wet summer and dry winter, with a winter cooler than the tropics (coldest month below 18 °C) | North Indian plains, interior of South China | Monsoon-type rainfall: wet when the winds blow from the sea, dry when they blow from the land |
| **Cfb** marine west coast | Mild winters (about 4–10 °C), cool summers (about 15–20 °C), rain in every season (about 50–250 cm), small annual and daily ranges | North-western Europe, the west coast of North America north of California, southern Chile, south-eastern Australia, New Zealand | The westerlies carry moist ocean air onto west coasts all year |
| **Df, Dw** cold, humid or dry-winter | Snowy winters, large annual range; summers warm to cool. Dw has several months below freezing and little rain (about 12–15 cm), mostly in summer | Between about 40° and 70° N across Eurasia and North America; Dw mainly in north-eastern Asia | Deep inside large northern landmasses, far from the sea's moderating effect |
| **ET** tundra | Warmest month between 0 and 10 °C; permafrost; mosses, lichens and low flowering plants | Beyond about 70° latitude | Low Sun angles; a very short growing season |
| **EF** ice cap | Below freezing in every month; very little precipitation | Interior Greenland and Antarctica | As for ET, with permanent ice |
| **Highland** | Climate changes with altitude over short horizontal distances | High mountains such as the Himalaya | Temperature falls with height ([Section 3.2](#32-six-controls-on-a-places-climate)) |

Group D appears only in the Northern Hemisphere because land exists at these latitudes mainly there. Highland is a separate category in the NCERT scheme rather than part of the A–E temperature tests.

### A4. Why the types sit where they do

Start from the pressure belts of [Chapter 011](011-winds-rainfall-and-weather-systems.md). Air rises (cloud and rain) at the equatorial low and at the sub-polar lows, and sinks (clear and dry) under the subtropical and polar highs.

```
Latitude  Pressure belt (Ch. 011)  Air motion          Tendency for climate
--------  -----------------------  ------------------  --------------------------------------------
90 deg    Polar high               sinking, very cold  E: ice cap and tundra
60 deg    Sub-polar low            rising, stormy      C and D: cloud, rain or snow from storms
30 deg    Subtropical high         SINKING, warming    B: deserts and steppes; Cs on west coasts
 0 deg    Equatorial low (ITCZ)    RISING, moist       Af: rain in every month
```

The belts follow the Sun, moving north in the northern summer and south in winter. Places near the edge of a belt therefore swing between two regimes:

- **Aw and Am:** under the ITCZ in the high-Sun season (rain), out from under it in the low-Sun season (dry).
- **Cs (Mediterranean):** the subtropical high is overhead in summer (dry) and the westerly storm track arrives in winter (rain).

Three contrasts refine the pattern:

1. **West coast versus east coast.** In the mid-latitudes the winds blow from the west, so a west coast receives ocean air first: mild, wet, small ranges (Cfb). An east coast at the same latitude receives air that has crossed the continent for part of the year, giving colder winters and larger ranges (Df). At about 30° the contrast reverses: west coasts are dry (sinking air and cold currents) and east coasts humid (moist air from warm seas, as in Cfa).
2. **Coast versus interior.** Water heats and cools slowly, so coasts have small annual ranges and interiors large ones ([2013 Q25][pyq2013]). This is why group D, with the largest ranges, lies deep inside the northern continents.
3. **Low versus high ground.** Highland climates cut across the latitude pattern altogether.

### A5. From a description to a type

Questions rarely quote a code; they paraphrase a textbook signature. Learn the signature as three things together: the temperature pattern, the timing of rainfall, and the location.

| A description says… | It points to… | Reason |

| Very large annual range, long freezing winter, light rain mostly in summer | Dw (north-eastern Asia) | Deep continental interior |

### A6. What a climate region is, and is not

- A climate region is a statistical summary. Kottek et al.'s world map, for example, rests on temperature and rainfall data for 1951–2000 ([2006][kottek]). As the climate changes, boundaries drift; [Chapter 108](108-climate-science-impacts-and-responses.md) treats that.
- The scheme describes the typical year, not extremes: a Cfb station can still have a heat wave.
- Different books use different levels of detail (Kottek's 31 classes against NCERT's shorter list). Know which scheme your source follows.

**Part A in brief**

- A Köppen code reads from broad to specific: group (temperature or dryness), then rainfall pattern, then temperature detail.
- Climate types follow the pressure belts: rising air wets the equator and the mid-latitude storm belts; sinking air dries the subtropics.
- Where a place sits relative to the shifting belts through the year explains its seasons.
- West coast versus east coast, coast versus interior, and low versus high ground explain most departures from the idealised pattern.

## Part B — The Indian monsoon

### B1. What "monsoon" means, and why it matters

IMD defines the monsoon as "the seasonal reversal of winds and the associated rainfall" ([IMD glossary][imd-gloss]). Both halves matter. An equatorial rainforest has heavy rain but no regular reversal of winds, and the Mediterranean has a wet and a dry season but no reversal either; neither is a monsoon climate ([2014 Q54][pyq2014]). Monsoon climates exist outside India too: the textbook scheme gives Am examples in north-eastern South America and northern Australia ([study notes][notes-bharat]).

For India the stakes are large:

- About 75% of the year's rainfall arrives in the south-west monsoon season, June–September ([IMD, 2025][imd-2025]).
- IMD's long period average (LPA) for that season, for the country as a whole, is 87 cm for 1971–2020 ([PIB][pib-lrf]); an earlier base period, 1961–2010, gave 880.6 mm ([IMD monsoon FAQ][imd-mfaq]). `[DYNAMIC: LPA — the figure depends on the base period and changes whenever IMD moves to a new one; revisit when IMD announces new normals]`
- The year-to-year variation is modest on average, with a standard deviation of only about 10% of the mean ([Gadgil 2003][gadgil]); yet a few points of rainfall decide whether a season counts as normal or deficient (Section B10).
- NCERT's summary: despite regional differences, India's climate is "monsoonal in rhythm and character", and the monsoon ties India's climate to that of the rest of south-east Asia ([NCERT][ncert-ipe]).

The reversal is easiest to see as two snapshots:

```
WINTER (Dec-Feb)                              SUMMER (Jun-Sep)
Land: cold, higher pressure                   Land: hot, low pressure (monsoon trough)
Sea: relatively warm, lower pressure          Sea: cooler, higher pressure
Surface wind: land to sea, from the           Surface wind: sea to land, from the
  north-east; dry                               south-west; moist and rain-bearing
```

The pressure pattern in the winter panel is the standard textbook account. The wind shift itself is recorded by IMD: after the south-west monsoon withdraws, the winds turn north-easterly ([IMD glossary][imd-gloss]).

### B2. How the monsoon arises: four steps

The textbook explanation builds in four steps. Each step is a fact about pressure or wind, and the order matters.

**Step 1: land and sea heat differently, creating a pressure contrast.** Water needs more energy to warm than land and gives heat up slowly. Through April and May the Sun's overhead position moves north through the tropics; it reaches the Tropic of Cancer at the June solstice. The large landmass north of the Indian Ocean heats intensely, air over it rises, and a low-pressure region forms over north and north-west India, while the cooler ocean to the south has higher pressure ([NCERT][ncert-ipe]). Air flows from higher to lower pressure.

**Step 2: the ITCZ follows the Sun and pulls the trade winds across the equator.** As the Sun moves north, so does the ITCZ. NCERT puts it at about 20°–25° N over the Gangetic plain in July, where it is called the monsoon trough and encourages a thermal low over north and north-west India ([NCERT][ncert-ipe]). The low pressure draws in the south-east trade winds of the Southern Hemisphere. After they cross the equator (NCERT places the crossing between about 40° E and 60° E), the Coriolis effect deflects them to the right, so south-easterly winds become south-westerly: the south-west monsoon.

Much of this cross-equatorial flow is concentrated in a narrow, fast, low-level stream, the **Findlater (Somali) jet**. Its axis, at about 850 hPa (roughly 1.5 km above the surface), runs from the Horn of Africa to the Gujarat coast. It builds by late May, strengthens in July and fades by late September, and stronger jets are associated with more active monsoon spells and weaker ones with breaks ([Kushwaha et al. 2022][findlater]).

**Step 3: mountains decide where the moisture falls.** The Himalaya acts as a climatic divide: it shields the subcontinent from cold northern winds and holds the monsoon winds so that they release their moisture within India. The windward slopes of the Western Ghats and Assam get heavy rain, while the leeward southern plateau stays dry ([NCERT][ncert-ipe]).

**Step 4: the upper air changes.** In summer the westerly jet stream that sits over north India in winter withdraws, and an easterly jet stream sets in along about 15° N. NCERT holds this easterly jet responsible for the "burst" of the monsoon ([NCERT][ncert-ipe]). Jet streams are taught in [Chapter 011](011-winds-rainfall-and-weather-systems.md).

The ocean adds a further influence that changes from year to year: the Southern Oscillation, El Niño and the Indian Ocean Dipole (Part C). NCERT notes that the strength of the south-west monsoon winds can be tracked through the pressure difference between Tahiti and Port Darwin ([NCERT][ncert-ipe]).

> **Fact, interpretation, debate.**
> *Fact:* the winds reverse seasonally, a trough of low pressure lies across north India in summer, and the rain follows it.
> *Textbook picture:* a giant sea breeze. Land heats, the sea stays cooler, and air flows in.
> *Research view:* for over 300 years the monsoon was seen as a gigantic land–sea breeze, but Gadgil (2003) argued that observations fit better with seeing it as the seasonal migration of the ITCZ ([Gadgil][gadgil]). A second debate concerns the Tibetan Plateau. Many books credit its summer heating with driving the monsoon; Boos and Kuang (2010) found in model experiments that the narrow mountain ranges at the plateau's edge matter most, by insulating warm moist air over India from cold dry air to the north, while plateau heating mainly adds rain locally along its southern edge ([Boos and Kuang][boos-kuang]). Some reference books also add a high-pressure cell over the southern Indian Ocean east of Madagascar; that is a commonly taught element which this chapter has not independently sourced.
> For the examination, learn Steps 1–4 as the standard account, and hold the explanation loosely: it simplifies an active research question.

### B3. The two branches

After crossing the equator the south-west monsoon reaches India in two currents:

```
south-east trades --(cross the equator, turn right)--> SOUTH-WEST MONSOON --+--> Arabian Sea branch
                                                                            |
                                                                            +--> Bay of Bengal branch
```

| | Arabian Sea branch | Bay of Bengal branch |
|---|---|---|
| Where it strikes | The west coast | Deflected by the Arakan Hills toward West Bengal and Bangladesh |
| Heaviest rain | Windward slopes of the Western Ghats (about 250–400 cm) | The Garo and Khasi hills of Meghalaya; Mawsynram has the highest average annual rainfall in the world |
| Dry zones | The rain shadow east of the Ghats; Saurashtra, Kachchh and Rajasthan get scanty rain | None named |
| Onward path | Rain over the Narmada and Tapi valleys and central India; meets the Bay branch over Punjab and Haryana and rains on the western Himalaya | Splits: west along the Ganga plains toward Punjab, and up the Brahmaputra valley |

Source for the table: [NCERT][ncert-ipe].

### B4. The monsoon calendar: onset, advance, burst

IMD defines **onset** as the "commencement of rainy season with the establishment of monsoon flow pattern", and **withdrawal** as the "cessation of southwest monsoon rainfall" ([IMD glossary][imd-gloss]). Onset is declared first over Kerala, normally about 1 June. The rains then advance north and the monsoon covers the country by early July. NCERT describes the arrival as abrupt, with thunder and lightning (the "burst"), and day temperatures fall by about 5–8 °C between mid-June and mid-July ([NCERT][ncert-ipe]).

| Event | Normal date (IMD, revised 2020) | 2025 | 2026 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Onset over Kerala | 1 June | 24 May (8 days early) | 4 June (3 days late) |
| Monsoon covers the whole country | 8 July | 27 June (11 days early) | 9 July (near normal) |
| Withdrawal begins, from west Rajasthan | 17 September | 14 September (3 days early) | 19 September (2 days late) |

Sources: normals — [IMD press release, 2020][imd-dates] and the dates quoted in the [2025][imd-2025] and [2026][imd-2026] reports; 2025 and 2026 actuals — those two reports. `[DYNAMIC: monsoon dates — the normals are IMD's 2020 revision and can be revised again, and each year brings new actuals; revisit after every season]`

Other normals from the same revision: onset over Mumbai and Kolkata on 11 June and over Delhi on 27 June; withdrawal from Delhi on 25 September and from Mumbai on 8 October; withdrawal ending around 15 October ([IMD, 2020][imd-dates]). IMD used 1961–2019 data to revise the onset dates and 1971–2019 data for withdrawal; the older dates rested on 1901–1940 records from only 149 stations. Older books still quote the earlier figures (NCERT's text, for example, has the monsoon covering the whole subcontinent by mid-July), so know both.

The monsoon reaches the Andaman Sea before Kerala. In 2025 it arrived there on 13 May, which IMD described as nine days earlier than its climatological date ([IMD, 2025][imd-2025]).

### B5. Active and break spells

Within the season the rain is not steady. IMD's glossary describes an **active** spell as one with rainfall 1½ to 4 times the normal (one of its criteria), and a **break** as a spell in which the monsoon trough shifts north and runs close to the foothills of the Himalaya, sharply reducing rainfall over the plains ([IMD glossary][imd-gloss]). NCERT describes breaks as one or more rainless weeks in the middle of the monsoon: in north India they occur when rain-bearing storms are infrequent along the trough, and on the west coast when the winds blow parallel to the coast ([NCERT][ncert-ipe]).

Northward-moving bands of cloud and rain recur every two to six weeks, so a season is a sequence of wet and dry spells, not a steady downpour ([Gadgil 2003][gadgil]). This is why a season's total can hide long dry gaps, and why a "normal" season is not the same as a uniform one.

### B6. Retreat, "October heat" and the north-east monsoon

From mid-September the monsoon withdraws, beginning from west Rajasthan (normally about 17 September) and continuing southwards; in IMD's normal calendar the withdrawal ends around 15 October ([IMD, 2020][imd-dates]). Skies clear, humidity stays high and temperatures rise, which NCERT calls the "October heat", before north India turns drier and cooler ([NCERT][ncert-ipe]).

As the south-west flow gives way to north-easterly winds, the **north-east monsoon** takes over in the south. IMD defines the season as October to December; it is the main rainy season of the south peninsula, and in the coastal districts of Tamil Nadu it supplies nearly 60% of annual rainfall ([IMD glossary][imd-gloss]). This explains a pattern that puzzles beginners: the Tamil Nadu coast lies parallel to the Bay of Bengal branch and in the rain shadow of the Arabian Sea branch, so it is comparatively dry from June to September and wet from October to December ([NCERT][ncert-ipe]). Cyclones that form over the Andaman Sea and the Bay of Bengal in October–November strike the deltas of the Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri ([NCERT][ncert-ipe]).

### B7. India's seasons and local weather

IMD divides the year into four meteorological seasons: winter (January–February), pre-monsoon (March–May), south-west monsoon (June–September) and post-monsoon (October–December) ([IMD glossary][imd-gloss]). NCERT uses a similar four-way split with different names and boundaries. The two schemes overlap but do not match, so note which one a source is using.

| IMD season | NCERT's name | What drives the weather | Local features ([NCERT][ncert-ipe]) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Winter (Jan–Feb) | Cold weather season (from about mid-November; December and January are coldest) | North-easterly flow off the land; western disturbances | Cold nights over north India; light winter rain in the north-west; the peninsula has no distinct cold season |
| Pre-monsoon (Mar–May) | Hot weather season (March–June in north India) | Intense heating of the land; low pressure builds in the north-west | *Loo*, a hot dry wind from Punjab to Bihar; May dust storms in Punjab, Haryana, eastern Rajasthan and Uttar Pradesh; *mango showers* in Kerala and coastal Karnataka; *Nor'westers* (Kalbaisakhi in Bengal, Bardoisila in Assam) |
| South-west monsoon (Jun–Sep) | Advancing monsoon (rainy season) | South-westerly moist flow; the monsoon trough | Abrupt onset; heavy rain; break spells |
| Post-monsoon (Oct–Dec) | Retreating monsoon (October–November), then the cold season | Withdrawal; north-easterly flow | October heat; north-east monsoon rain in the south-east peninsula; Bay of Bengal cyclones |

**An island example.** The Andaman and Nicobar Islands have a humid tropical climate and receive rain from both monsoons; the south-west monsoon reaches the Andaman Sea first (Section B4). The Andaman and Nicobar Administration's statistics directorate puts the south-west monsoon's rain from mid-May to the end of September and the north-east monsoon's from November to January, with rainfall generally higher from May to October and moderate to low from November to April ([A&N Directorate][an-ecostat]). An ICAR–CIARI bulletin prepared with IMD's agrometeorology division gives the share of the year's rain by period: about 61% in June–September, 22% in October–December, 12% in May and 5% in January–April ([ICAR–CIARI][ciari]). The two sources place the north-east monsoon's months a little differently, but both show the rain concentrated from about May to December and the driest months at the start of the calendar year, not a December-to-May rainy season. The humid-tropical and two-monsoon statements agree with the [2026 PYQ key][pyq2026].

### B8. Western disturbances

In winter, north India lies under the westerlies rather than the monsoon winds. A **western disturbance** (WD) is a system within that westerly flow. IMD's glossary describes it as a cyclonic circulation or trough in the mid and lower troposphere (or a surface low) that originates over the Mediterranean, Caspian or Black Sea and moves east across north India ([IMD glossary][imd-gloss]). IMD Srinagar adds that WDs travel across the Middle East (Iran, Afghanistan, Pakistan) into the subcontinent and describes their precipitation as non-monsoonal ([IMD Srinagar][imd-wd]).

What they do in India:

- They bring light to moderate rain to the north-west plains (Punjab, Haryana, Delhi, western Uttar Pradesh) and snow to the Himalaya, mainly in winter. NCERT stresses the benefit to rabi crops, and IMD Srinagar agrees ([NCERT][ncert-ipe]; [IMD Srinagar][imd-wd]).
- They occasionally bring cold-wave conditions and dense fog to the Indo-Gangetic plains, and when precipitation is excessive, crop damage, landslides, floods and avalanches ([IMD Srinagar][imd-wd]).
- They can occur in any season, not only winter ([IMD Srinagar][imd-wd]), but winter is when they matter most for north-west India.

Three systems are easily confused. They differ in origin, season and place:

| | Western disturbance | Tropical cyclone | South-west monsoon |
|---|---|---|---|
| Home | Mid-latitude westerlies; Mediterranean, Caspian, Black Sea | Warm tropical seas ([Chapter 011](011-winds-rainfall-and-weather-systems.md)) | Seasonal reversal of winds over the Indian Ocean and South Asia |
| When it affects India | Mainly winter (any season is possible) | Mostly outside the monsoon months; October–November on the east coast | June–September |
| Where | North-west India, the north Indian plains, the Himalaya | Coasts, especially the east coast | Most of the country |
| Typical weather | Light to moderate rain, mountain snow; sometimes cold wave and dense fog | Strong wind, storm surge, heavy rain | Heavy, widespread rain with active and break spells |
| Who benefits or suffers | Rabi crops benefit; excess brings landslides and floods | Coastal communities and deltas | Kharif agriculture and water supply ([Chapter 018](018-agricultural-geography-and-land-use.md)) |

### B9. Where it rains: the regional pattern

NCERT groups India's annual rainfall into four zones (approximate ranges; the country's average is about 125 cm):

| Zone | Annual rainfall | Where ([NCERT][ncert-ipe]) |
|---|---|---|
| High | Above 200 cm | The west coast, the Western Ghats, north-eastern sub-Himalayan areas and the Meghalaya hills (parts of the Khasi and Jaintia hills exceed 1,000 cm) |
| Medium | 100–200 cm | Southern Gujarat, east Tamil Nadu, Odisha, Jharkhand, Bihar, eastern Madhya Pradesh, the northern Ganga plain, the Cachar valley and Manipur |
| Low | 50–100 cm | Western Uttar Pradesh, Delhi, Haryana, Punjab, Jammu and Kashmir, eastern Rajasthan, Gujarat and the Deccan plateau |
| Scanty | Below 50 cm | Parts of Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka and Maharashtra; Ladakh; most of western Rajasthan (Jaisalmer station averaged 20.16 cm in IMD's 1981–2010 climatology ([IMD station table][imd-jaisalmer])) |

To explain any regional pattern, ask five questions in order:

1. **Which wind brings the moisture in this season?** (a monsoon branch, the north-east monsoon, a western disturbance, local convection)
2. **What lifts the air?** (a mountain, the monsoon trough, a front or storm)
3. **Is the place windward or leeward of that lifting?**
4. **Where is the place relative to the monsoon trough and the shifting belts through the year?**
5. **Is the question about the average, or about this year's anomaly?** (Part C)

| Place or region | Rain season and source | Why |
|---|---|---|
| West coast and windward Western Ghats | June–September; Arabian Sea branch | The slopes force the moist south-westerly air up (about 250–400 cm); the plateau behind is in the rain shadow ([NCERT][ncert-ipe]) |
| Mawsynram and the Garo–Khasi hills | June–September; Bay of Bengal branch | The Arakan Hills deflect the branch toward Bengal, and the Meghalaya hills force the air up; Mawsynram has the highest average annual rainfall in the world ([NCERT][ncert-ipe]) |
| Tamil Nadu coast | Mainly October–December; north-east monsoon | Parallel to the Bay branch and in the rain shadow of the Arabian Sea branch in June–September; north-easterly flow brings the rain afterwards ([NCERT][ncert-ipe]; [IMD glossary][imd-gloss]) |

### B10. Variability, droughts and forecasting

**Variability.** Interannual variation is modest on average (a standard deviation of about 10% of the mean), yet it decides harvests and reservoir levels ([Gadgil 2003][gadgil]). IMD classes the all-India season by percentage of LPA; the categories in its 2026 forecast were deficient (below 90%), below normal (90–95%), normal (96–104%), above normal (105–110%) and excess (above 110%) ([PIB][pib-lrf]). In IMD's glossary an all-India drought year is one with rainfall deficiency above 10% and 20–40% of the country under drought conditions ([IMD glossary][imd-gloss]). `[DYNAMIC: IMD category thresholds — taken from the 2026 forecast release; IMD may revise its categories; revisit at each April forecast]`

**Forecasting.** IMD issues a first-stage long-range forecast in mid-April and an updated one at the end of May, plus monthly outlooks ([IMD, 2026][imd-2026]; [PIB][pib-lrf]). Its statements track the expected ENSO and IOD conditions explicitly, cite forecasts from its Monsoon Mission Coupled Forecast System (MMCFS) for them, and note a general inverse relationship between northern-hemisphere snow cover and the following monsoon's rainfall ([IMD, 2026][imd-2026]). Ocean temperatures in the months before the monsoon are therefore among the inputs forecasters examine; the [2020 question][pyq2020] was framed around one such measure, Ocean Mean Temperature in January–March.

**A forecast against its outcome (2026).** The first-stage forecast was 92% of LPA with an error of ±5%; the update on 29 May was 90% ±4%; the season delivered 87%, which IMD called "accurate and within the forecast error limit". One monthly outlook missed: July was forecast below normal but came in at 101% of LPA ([IMD, 2026][imd-2026]). The lesson is not that forecasts fail but that they are probabilistic and carry error, more so for one month or one place than for a whole season. `[DYNAMIC: 2026 forecast record — the figures come from IMD's season summary; revisit after IMD's final report and when IMD issues its first forecast for 2027]`

**Part B in brief**

- The monsoon is a seasonal reversal of winds with rain; the south-west monsoon brings about three-quarters of India's rainfall.
- Four steps: a land–sea pressure contrast; the ITCZ's northward shift drawing the trades across the equator; the Himalaya and the Ghats deciding where the rain falls; a change in the upper-air jets.
- A season is onset, then wet and dry spells, then withdrawal; the north-east monsoon takes over in the south-east peninsula.
- Western disturbances bring winter rain to the north-west; they are neither tropical cyclones nor part of the monsoon.
- Regional rainfall follows from the wind that carries the moisture, what lifts it, and whether the place is windward or leeward.

## Part C — Ocean–atmosphere links: ENSO and the Indian Ocean Dipole

### C1. Why the monsoon needs the ocean

Part B explained the average monsoon. Part C explains why one year differs from another. The monsoon is a circulation of moist air across the Indian Ocean and South Asia, and that circulation is connected to circulations elsewhere in the tropics. Warm and cool patches of ocean surface, in the Pacific and in the Indian Ocean, change where air rises and rain falls, and the effect can reach India. A link of this kind between distant places is a **teleconnection**.

### C2. ENSO: the Pacific mechanism

**The neutral state.** Along the equator the surface winds (the trade winds) normally blow from east to west ([climate.gov][cg-nutshell]). Warm surface water collects in the western Pacific around Indonesia, where moist air rises and heavy rain falls; cooler water lies at the surface in the east, where deeper water wells up (upwelling, [Chapter 013](013-ocean-properties-and-circulation.md)). The air that rose in the west flows east at height and sinks in the east, closing a loop called the **Walker circulation**: "rising air in the west and sinking air in the east connect the flow in one big, continuous loop" ([climate.gov][cg-walker]).

```
              rising air                               sinking air
                 ^    ---- flow aloft, west to east ---->    |
                 |                                           v
 WEST PACIFIC    warm water, heavy rain       EAST PACIFIC   cooler water, dry
 (Indonesia)     <---- surface trade winds, east to west ----   (South America)

 El Nino: the trades weaken, warm water spreads east, the rising branch shifts east.
```

| | Neutral | El Niño | La Niña |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trade winds | Blow east to west | Weaken, and may reverse in places | Strengthen |
| Surface of the central and eastern tropical Pacific | Near average | Warmer than average | Cooler than average |
| Rainfall | Heavy over Indonesia | Less over Indonesia, more over the tropical Pacific | More over Indonesia, less over the central Pacific |
| Walker circulation | Air rises in the west, sinks in the east | The rising branch shifts east; rising motion over Indonesia weakens | Intensifies |
| Niño-3.4 anomaly | Within ±0.5 °C | At or above +0.5 °C | At or below −0.5 °C |

Sources: [climate.gov][cg-nutshell]; [climate.gov, Walker circulation][cg-walker]; [IMD FAQ][imd-faq].

**The Southern Oscillation** is the atmospheric half of the story: a see-saw of sea-level pressure between the eastern and western tropical Pacific. NCERT identifies the pressure difference between Tahiti and Port Darwin as a measure of it ([NCERT][ncert-ipe]). The index turns negative in El Niño, and the two halves together give the name ENSO (El Niño–Southern Oscillation). Australia's Bureau of Meteorology (BoM) reported a 30-day Southern Oscillation Index of −14.4 on 8 November 2026, consistent with El Niño ([BoM][bom-enso]). `[DYNAMIC: Southern Oscillation Index — changes daily; revisit with the latest BoM update]`

### C3. Measuring ENSO, and what it does to the monsoon

**Measurement.**

- ENSO is tracked in the **Niño-3.4 region**, 5° N–5° S and 170° W–120° W in the central Pacific ([IMD FAQ][imd-faq]).
- The Oceanic Niño Index (ONI) is the 3-month running mean of sea-surface temperature anomalies there. Warm and cold periods are flagged at ±0.5 °C, and a historical episode needs at least five consecutive overlapping seasons beyond the threshold ([CPC][cpc-oni]). IMD adds that declaring an event also needs a coupled atmospheric response ([IMD FAQ][imd-faq]).
- Strength classes: weak 0.5 to below 1.0 °C; moderate 1.0 to below 1.5; strong 1.5 to below 2.0; very strong 2.0 or more ([IMD FAQ][imd-faq]).
- NOAA's Climate Prediction Center now uses the **Relative Oceanic Niño Index (RONI)** as its official index. RONI compares Niño-3.4 with the wider tropics rather than relying only on a 30-year base period ([CPC][cpc-roni]; [CPC][cpc-oni]); IMD notes that the familiar ±0.5 °C thresholds still apply ([IMD FAQ][imd-faq]). `[DYNAMIC: ENSO index — NOAA's switch to RONI is recent (2026) and other agencies may still quote the older ONI; revisit when a source quotes an index]`
- Events develop every 2 to 7 years and usually peak between November and February. El Niño generally lasts 9–18 months; La Niña can persist for two or three years ([IMD FAQ][imd-faq]).

**What El Niño does to the monsoon.** IMD states that El Niño "generally suppresses the Indian summer monsoon by weakening the large-scale atmospheric circulation" ([IMD FAQ][imd-faq]). In terms of Section C2: the main rising branch of the Pacific circulation moves east, rain shifts away from the Indonesian side, and the circulation across the tropics that feeds the monsoon is disturbed. Gadgil describes the strong ENSO–monsoon link as a consequence of the tropical convergence zone over the Pacific influencing the monsoon's year-to-year variation ([Gadgil 2003][gadgil]).

It is a tendency, not a rule:

- El Niño raises the probability of below-normal rainfall, but the relationship is "not deterministic". Many major drought years (IMD lists 1982, 1987, 2002, 2009 and 2015) fell in El Niño events, but not every El Niño ends in drought ([IMD FAQ][imd-faq]).
- 1997 saw a strong El Niño and yet a normal monsoon ([IMD FAQ][imd-faq]); Section C4 shows why.
- Long break spells have also occurred without El Niño, as in 1979 ([Gadgil 2003][gadgil]).
- **La Niña** generally strengthens the monsoon circulation and raises the likelihood of above-normal rainfall ([IMD FAQ][imd-faq]).

### C4. The Indian Ocean Dipole

The **Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD)** is a difference in sea-surface temperature between the western and eastern tropical Indian Ocean ([IMD FAQ][imd-faq]; [BoM][bom-iod]). It is measured by the **Dipole Mode Index (DMI)**: the SST anomaly in a western box (50° E–70° E, 10° S–10° N) minus the anomaly in an eastern box (90° E–110° E, 10° S–equator) ([IMD FAQ][imd-faq]).

| | Positive IOD | Negative IOD |
|---|---|---|
| Western tropical Indian Ocean | Warmer than normal | Cooler than normal |
| Eastern tropical Indian Ocean | Cooler than normal | Warmer than normal |
| DMI | At or above +0.4 °C, sustained for several weeks | At or below −0.4 °C, sustained for several weeks |
| Effect on El Niño's monsoon impact | Can partly, or in some cases substantially, offset the weakening | Can further raise the likelihood of below-normal rainfall |

Sources: [IMD FAQ][imd-faq] for the thresholds and effects. IMD says the IOD typically develops in the boreal summer, peaks in September–November and weakens rapidly; BoM describes events as usually starting around May or June and peaking between August and October ([BoM][bom-iod]). Sources differ by about a month on the peak, so treat "late summer to autumn" as the safe statement.

The 1997 case shows the offset: a strong El Niño, yet normal monsoon rainfall ([IMD FAQ][imd-faq]). A commonly given intuition is that a warm western Indian Ocean supplies extra heat and moisture to the winds that feed the monsoon; this chapter has not independently sourced that explanation.

Do not confuse the two. ENSO is a Pacific phenomenon; the IOD is a west-minus-east difference *within the Indian Ocean*, not a difference between the Indian and Pacific oceans (the point tested in [2017 Q81][pyq2017]). The IOD can modify the effect of El Niño on the monsoon; it does not replace it.

| | ENSO | IOD |
|---|---|---|
| Ocean | Tropical Pacific | Tropical Indian Ocean |
| Measured by | Niño-3.4 anomaly (ONI or RONI) | DMI: west minus east SST anomaly |
| Event threshold | ±0.5 °C | ±0.4 °C |
| Timing | Peaks November–February; El Niño lasts 9–18 months | Develops in boreal summer; peaks late summer to autumn; decays quickly |
| Effect on the monsoon | El Niño tends to suppress it; La Niña tends to strengthen it | Positive IOD can offset El Niño; negative IOD can reinforce its drying |

What the case teaches:

1. **A tendency, not a verdict.** El Niño strengthened through the season while the IOD stayed neutral, so no positive IOD was present to offset it, and the season ended 13% below the LPA. 2. **One figure hides geography.** North-west and central India came close to normal (94% and 97%) while the south peninsula and the east and north-east fell far short (76% and 74%).
3. **One figure hides time.** July reached 101% of LPA between a June at 65% and an August at 84% (active and break spells, Section B5).
4. **Forecasts carry error.** The seasonal total fell inside the forecast range, and a monthly outlook missed (Section B10).
5. **Climate versus weather.** One deficient monsoon is a departure from the monsoon climate, not a new climate (Section 3.1).

**Part C in brief**

- ENSO is a Pacific cycle in which trade winds, warm water and the Walker circulation shift; the IOD is a west–east temperature difference within the Indian Ocean.
- Each is measured by an index with a threshold (±0.5 °C for ENSO, ±0.4 °C for the IOD).
- El Niño tends to suppress and La Niña tends to strengthen the monsoon; a positive IOD can offset El Niño. All three are tendencies that shift the odds.
- A season's outcome should be read regionally and month by month, not only as one national percentage.

| Tempting idea | What is actually true | Section |
|---|---|---|

| Only India has a monsoon | India is the standard case, but monsoon climates occur elsewhere; the textbook scheme gives Am examples in north-eastern South America and northern Australia. | B1, A3 |

Gadgil (2003) argued that the seasonal migration of the ITCZ fits observations better, and Boos and Kuang (2010) found that the mountain ranges at the plateau's edge matter more than plateau heating. | IMD and the textbooks agree on seasons and dates | IMD's four seasons and its 2020-revised normals (whole country covered by 8 July; withdrawal from west Rajasthan about 17 September) differ from NCERT's season names and from the mid-July coverage date in older books. Note which scheme a source uses. | B4, B7 |
| A western disturbance, a tropical cyclone and the monsoon are one kind of rain-bringer | A western disturbance is a mid-latitude westerly system that gives winter rain and snow in the north-west; a tropical cyclone forms over warm tropical seas; the monsoon is a seasonal wind reversal. They differ in origin, season and place. | B8 |
| A normal season is an evenly wet season | Rain comes in active spells separated by breaks, so a normal total can hide long dry gaps. | B5 |
| "90% of LPA" is a forecast for every place and every month | It is a national, seasonal figure with a model error. In 2026 the four regions ranged from 74% to 97% of their own LPAs, and July came in at 101%. | B10, C5 |
| 87 cm and 868.6 mm are two different LPAs | They are the same all-India LPA for 1971–2020 quoted at different precision (868.6 mm is 86.86 cm). The LPA itself changes when the base period changes. | B1, C5 |
| ENSO and the IOD are the same, or the IOD is an Indian–Pacific difference | ENSO is a Pacific cycle read from the Niño-3.4 region; the IOD is a west-minus-east temperature difference within the Indian Ocean, with a ±0.4 °C threshold against ENSO's ±0.5 °C. | C4 |
| El Niño guarantees a drought | El Niño raises the odds of a deficient monsoon but is "not deterministic": 1997 had a strong El Niño and a normal monsoon, a positive IOD can offset the weakening, and a long break spell in 1979 came without El Niño. | C3, C4 |
| A Köppen code tells you the weather | It summarises long-term averages. Cs and Cfb, for example, differ in whether summer is dry, and even a Cfb station can have a heat wave. | A2, A6 |

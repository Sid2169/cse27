## 1. Orientation

Oceans cover about 71% of Earth's surface and hold 97% of all water. They are not passive reservoirs. They store and transport enormous amounts of heat, exchange gases and moisture with the atmosphere, support a large share of Earth's primary production, and shape rainfall patterns thousands of kilometres inland. This chapter builds the vocabulary and the mechanisms that make those connections legible.

The chapter moves from the shape of the ocean floor upward through the water column, then into the three kinds of movement — waves, tides and currents — and finally outward into how currents alter climate and fisheries.

## 3. Foundations and key terms

| Term | Definition used in this chapter |
|---|---|
| **Salinity** | The mass of dissolved salts in grams per 1,000 g (1 kg) of seawater, expressed in parts per thousand (‰ or ppt). Average ocean salinity is about 35‰. |
| **Thermocline** | A layer in the ocean — typically 100–1,000 m deep in low and middle latitudes — where temperature falls rapidly with depth, separating warm surface water from cold deep water ([NCERT][ncert-oceans]). |
| **Current** | A large body of water moving steadily along a defined path, like a river within the ocean. Surface currents affect the upper ~400 m; deep currents circulate the other 90% of ocean water ([NCERT][ncert-movements]). |
| **Tide** | The regular, predictable rise and fall of sea level caused by the gravitational pull of the Moon and the Sun, combined with the centrifugal force of the Earth–Moon–Sun system ([NCERT][ncert-movements]). |
| **Upwelling** | The vertical rise of cold, nutrient-rich water from depth to the surface, often driven by alongshore winds whose net Ekman transport moves surface water offshore (see Section 12.1) ([NCERT][ncert-movements]). |
| **Gyre** | A large, roughly circular ocean-current system driven by prevailing winds and deflected by Coriolis force. Each major ocean basin has at least one gyre in each hemisphere. |
| **Thermohaline circulation** | Density-driven deep-water circulation governed by temperature (thermo-) and salinity (-haline). Cold, salty, dense water sinks at high latitudes and slowly spreads through the deep ocean. |

## 4. Ocean relief

The ocean floor is as varied as the continents. Moving from the shore into deep water, five broad zones are crossed.

### 4.1 Continental shelf

The continental shelf is the gently sloping, submerged extension of the continent — the same rock, merely flooded by the sea. Its gradient is 1° or less; its average width is about 80 km; and depths range from a few metres to about 200 m. The shelf ends abruptly at the **shelf break**, where the floor steepens. The Siberian shelf is the world's widest, up to 1,500 km; shelves are almost absent along the coasts of Chile and western Sumatra ([NCERT][ncert-oceans]).

The shelf matters enormously for fisheries: it receives river sediment, sunlight reaches the floor, and nutrients from the land accumulate. Most of the world's commercially important fish live here.

### 4.2 Continental slope

Beyond the shelf break, the **continental slope** falls steeply at 2–5°, from the shelf edge down to about 3,000 m. This is the true boundary between the continent and the ocean basin. **Submarine canyons** cut across the shelf and slope, often opposite large river mouths; they channel sediment down into the deep ocean.

### 4.3 Continental rise

At the foot of the slope, the gradient eases and sediment fans out in a broad, gentle apron called the **continental rise**. It merges gradually into the deep ocean floor.

### 4.4 Abyssal plain (deep sea plain)

The abyssal plain is the flattest, smoothest terrain on Earth — the result of thousands of years of fine clay and silt settling from the water column above. Depths are typically 3,000–6,000 m. Most of the ocean floor lies in this zone.

### 4.5 Trenches

**Ocean trenches** are the deepest parts of the ocean: narrow, steep-sided basins that cut below the surrounding abyssal floor, typically an additional 3–5 km. They form where one tectonic plate subducts under another, and so lie adjacent to island arcs and are associated with strong earthquakes and volcanoes. The Mariana Trench in the Pacific holds the deepest known point, Challenger Deep, at about 10,924 m.

NCERT notes that 57 ocean deeps have been explored: 32 in the Pacific, 19 in the Atlantic, and 6 in the Indian Ocean ([NCERT][ncert-oceans]).

### 4.6 Mid-oceanic ridges and other features

**Mid-oceanic ridges** are continuous underwater mountain chains where new seafloor is created as plates pull apart. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge runs the full length of the Atlantic; Iceland is one of its peaks above sea level. Ridges consist of two parallel chains separated by a central rift valley; individual peaks can reach 2,500 m above the surrounding floor.

Other relief features:

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| **Seamount** | A pointed, extinct volcanic mountain that does not reach the surface; typically 3,000–4,500 m tall. |
| **Guyot** | A seamount with a flat top, produced by wave erosion as it once stood at the surface, then subsided. |
| **Atoll** | A low, ring-shaped coral island surrounding a central lagoon, formed as a coral reef persists while the volcanic island beneath it subsides. The Lakshadweep Islands are atolls. |

## 5. Temperature and the thermocline

### 5.1 Surface temperature

The ocean's surface temperature follows insolation, so it is highest near the equator and decreases toward the poles. Near the equator, average surface temperature is about 27°C, falling to about 22°C at 20° latitude, 14°C at 40°, and near 0°C in polar waters ([NCERT][ncert-oceans]). The warmest patch is not at the equator but slightly north of it, because the northern hemisphere has more land, which warms faster and heats the overlying air.

Enclosed seas depart from the open-ocean pattern: in the tropics they are warmer (less exchange, more heating) while in high latitudes they are colder. The Red Sea reaches salinities and temperatures higher than the open Indian Ocean.

Currents displace the temperature pattern sideways relative to latitude lines (**isotherms**). In January, in the Northern Hemisphere, land cools faster than the ocean, so isotherms bend poleward over the ocean (warmer) and equatorward over the continent (colder) ([2024 Q18][pyq2024]). Statement 2 of that question — which called the Gulf Stream and North Atlantic Drift *cold* currents — is incorrect; they are warm currents that push isotherms northward.

### 5.2 Temperature with depth: the three-layer model

In low and middle latitudes, temperature changes dramatically with depth:

| Layer | Depth | Temperature | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm surface layer | 0–500 m | 20–25°C | Heated by insolation; mixed by wind. Exists year-round in tropics, only in summer in mid-latitudes. |
| **Thermocline** | ~100–400 m to ~1,000–1,500 m | Falls rapidly | The transition zone. Separates warm, light surface water from cold, dense deep water. |
| Cold deep layer | Below thermocline to floor | Near 0°C | Contains about 90% of the ocean's volume. |

In polar waters (above roughly 60°N or 60°S) the thermocline is absent; the water column is uniformly cold from surface to floor, because solar heating is too weak to maintain a warm surface layer.

### 5.3 Why the thermocline matters

The thermocline acts as a lid. Because cold, dense water lies under warm, light water, the two layers do not mix easily. This prevents nutrients — which accumulate at depth from sinking organic matter — from reaching the sunlit surface. Where the thermocline is broken or absent (at high latitudes, in upwelling zones), nutrients mix upward and biological productivity is high.

## 6. Salinity and density

### 6.1 Salinity: definition and factors

Salinity is the grams of dissolved salt per kilogram (1,000 g) of seawater, written as ‰ (parts per thousand). The world ocean averages about 35‰. Brackish water is less saline than ordinary seawater. The often-quoted 24.7‰ value concerns the relationship between freezing and maximum density; do not use it as a universal salinity observed at the equator or as a necessary boundary for every classification of brackish water ([Scripps][scripps-density]).

The main factors controlling surface salinity:

- **Evaporation raises salinity** by removing fresh water and leaving salts behind.
- **Precipitation, river inflow and melting ice lower salinity** by adding fresh water.
- **Freezing raises salinity** in the remaining liquid (brine rejection).

Consequently, the subtropical oceans (~20–30° latitude) — where subtropical high pressure brings clear skies and strong evaporation but little rain — have the highest surface salinities (up to 37‰ in the Atlantic). Near the equator, heavy rainfall lowers salinity despite high evaporation. Near the poles, melting sea ice dilutes the surface. Enclosed seas with high evaporation are very salty: the Red Sea reaches ~41‰ ([NCERT][ncert-oceans]).

Near river mouths, freshwater discharge, tides and mixing also strongly affect salinity; a local change should be explained from evidence for that estuary rather than inferred from a single activity alone.

### 6.2 Salinity with depth: the halocline

A **halocline** is a layer where salinity changes rapidly with depth, analogous to the thermocline for temperature. Below the halocline, salinity is nearly constant. The halocline typically lies at the same depths as the thermocline.

### 6.3 Density

Seawater density depends on both temperature and salinity. Colder water is denser; saltier water is denser. These two factors combine in the **thermohaline circulation** (Section 10.5). At normal surface pressure, fresh water reaches maximum density near 4°C, **above** its freezing point. As salinity rises, that temperature decreases; near 24.7‰ it meets the freezing point. Ordinary seawater near 35‰ therefore becomes denser as it cools toward freezing, unlike fresh water cooling from 4°C to 0°C ([Scripps][scripps-density]). Pressure also affects seawater density.

## 7. Waves

### 7.1 What a wave is

A wave carries energy across the surface; the water itself moves in roughly circular orbits and returns nearly to where it started. Wind is the energy source. When wind blows across the water surface, friction creates ripples; with sufficient fetch (the unobstructed distance over which wind acts), duration and strength, ripples grow into full waves.

**Parts of a wave:**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Crest | Highest point |
| Trough | Lowest point |
| Wave height | Vertical distance from trough bottom to crest top |
| Amplitude | Half the wave height |
| Wavelength | Horizontal distance between successive crests |
| Wave period | Time between successive crests passing a fixed point |
| Fetch | The unobstructed distance of water over which wind blows |

### 7.2 Breakers and swell

As waves approach a shoreline, the sea floor shallows. At depths of roughly half a wavelength or less, bottom influence becomes relevant: this is the **wave-base/deep-water criterion**, not an automatic breaking threshold. As water shallows further, wave speed and wavelength decrease, the wave can grow steeper, and it eventually becomes unstable and breaks. Breaking depends on wave height, depth and steepness ([Scripps CDIP][scripps-waves]). Waves generated locally by wind are steep; waves that have travelled from a distant source lose their steepness and arrive as long, smooth **swell**.

**Tsunamis** are not wind waves. They are generated by sudden displacement of the sea floor — in a large submarine earthquake, a landslide or a volcanic eruption — and affect the full depth of the ocean. In the open ocean they are low and fast (up to 800 km/h); near a coast they slow, grow dramatically in height, and can travel kilometres inland.

## 8. Tides

### 8.1 Causes

Tides are the most predictable of all ocean movements. Three forces act together ([2015 Q67][pyq2015]):

1. **Gravitational pull of the Moon** — the dominant force; the Moon is close enough that its gravity noticeably distorts the ocean surface.
2. **Tide-generating effect of the Sun** — roughly 46% of the Moon's. This compares the difference in gravitational attraction across Earth, not total gravitational pull: the Sun's total pull on Earth is much stronger than the Moon's ([NOAA tides][noaa-tides]).
3. **Centrifugal force** of the Earth–Moon system rotating around their shared centre of mass.

The result is two bulges: one on the side of Earth facing the Moon (gravitational pull exceeds centrifugal force there) and one on the opposite side (centrifugal force exceeds gravitational pull there). As Earth rotates under these bulges, most coasts experience two high tides and two low tides each day.

### 8.2 Spring and neap tides

| Condition | Sun–Moon–Earth geometry | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Spring tide** | Sun, Moon and Earth roughly in line (new moon or full moon) | Gravitational forces reinforce; unusually high high-tides and low low-tides |
| **Neap tide** | Sun and Moon at right angles to Earth (first or last quarter moon) | Gravitational forces partly cancel; smaller tidal range |

Spring tides occur twice a month. Neap tides follow about a week later. Additionally, when the Moon is at **perigee** (closest point to Earth, monthly) the range is greater; at **apogee** it is smaller. Earth's closest approach to the Sun (**perihelion**, about 3 January) makes the Sun's pull stronger and tidal ranges larger.

### 8.3 Types by daily frequency

| Type | Description | Where common |
|---|---|---|
| **Semi-diurnal** | Two roughly equal highs and two lows per day | Most coasts |
| **Diurnal** | One high and one low per day | Parts of the Gulf of Mexico, Pacific |
| **Mixed** | Unequal highs and lows; two of each | West coast of North America, many Pacific islands |

A **tidal current** forms when tides are funnelled between islands or into a bay; a **surge** is an irregular, storm-driven rise in sea level, not a tide.

### 8.4 Tidal range and uses

Tidal range varies greatly with coastal geometry. Funnel-shaped bays amplify the range: the Bay of Fundy in Nova Scotia, Canada has the world's largest tidal range, up to 15–16 m ([NCERT][ncert-movements]). India's tidal energy potential is highest in the Gulf of Khambhat and the Gulf of Kutch; NCERT mentions a 3 MW tidal project at Durgaduani in the Sundarbans ([NCERT][ncert-movements]).

Tides are used for navigation (deep-draft vessels cross harbour bars at high tide), for flushing pollutants out of estuaries, and for tidal power generation.

## 9. Ocean currents: causes and types

### 9.1 What drives currents

Several forces combine to set water in horizontal and vertical motion ([NCERT][ncert-movements]):

- **Solar heating and gravity.** Equatorial water is heated and expands, raising sea level about 8 cm above mid-latitudes. Gravity pulls this water downslope, creating a pressure gradient.
- **Wind.** Friction between the atmosphere and the ocean surface drags surface water in the direction of the prevailing wind. Trade winds drive water westward near the equator; westerlies drive it eastward in the temperate zone.
- **Coriolis force** ([Chapter 011](011-winds-rainfall-and-weather-systems.md)). Earth's rotation deflects moving water to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern. This turns straight wind-driven flows into large circular systems — **gyres** — that rotate clockwise in the NH and anti-clockwise in the SH.
- **Density differences.** Saltier, colder water is denser and sinks; lighter, warmer water rises to fill the space. This drives the deep thermohaline circulation.
- **Monsoon winds.** In the Indian Ocean, the reversal of monsoon winds seasonally reverses some surface currents (see Section 10.3).

### 9.2 Warm and cold currents

A **warm current** flows from lower latitudes toward higher latitudes, carrying warm water into cooler regions. A **cold current** flows from higher latitudes toward lower latitudes, carrying cold water into warmer regions.

The pattern of warm and cold currents follows a consistent rule:

| Latitude zone | East coast of continent | West coast of continent |
|---|---|---|
| Tropics and subtropics | **Warm** currents (trade winds push water westward; it piles up and flows poleward along east coasts) | **Cold** currents (upwelling and equatorward drift) |
| Temperate and sub-polar | **Cold** currents (polar water flows equatorward) | **Warm** currents (westerlies push water eastward; warm water reaches west coasts) |

This explains [2021 Q51][pyq2021]: trade winds push warm surface water westward in tropical oceans, so the western sections are warmer than the eastern sections at tropical latitudes. In the temperate zone, westerlies push warm water eastward, so eastern sections are warmer. Both statements are correct.

| **Equatorial Counter Current** | Warm | Flows **east** between the two equatorial currents |

### 10.4 The equatorial counter-current

This current is a PYQ favourite ([2015 Q89][pyq2015]). The North and South Equatorial Currents carry water westward, where it piles up. The pile-up creates a slight pressure gradient that drives a compensating eastward flow in between — the **equatorial counter-current**. The answer is therefore (b): the convergence of the two equatorial currents. Earth's rotation and salinity differences are not the primary cause; the belt of calm near the equator (the doldrums) has weak winds and may help sustain it, but the counter-current is fundamentally a pressure-gradient response to the westward-moving equatorial currents.

### 10.5 Thermohaline circulation (global conveyor belt)

Surface wind-driven currents and deep thermohaline currents together form a single connected system sometimes called the **ocean conveyor belt**. The chain begins in the North Atlantic, where the Gulf Stream carries warm, salty water to high latitudes. As it cools, the water becomes very dense, sinks to the ocean floor, and flows slowly south. This deep water spreads into the Indian and Pacific Oceans, where it gradually warms and rises, then returns as surface water to the North Atlantic, completing a loop that takes roughly 1,000 years for a water parcel. The Atlantic branch of this system is called the **AMOC** (Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation). Its weakening is one of the major climate concerns for north-western Europe.

## 11. Effects on climate

### 11.1 Desert-forming cold currents on tropical west coasts

Cold currents on the western margins of continents in the tropics and subtropics cool and stabilise the air above them (a temperature inversion). The cool air carries little moisture and inhibits convection, so rainfall is very low. This explains why hot deserts sit on tropical west coasts:

- **Atacama Desert** (Chile, Peru) — Humboldt Current
- **Namib Desert** (Namibia, South Africa) — Benguela Current
- **Saharan Atlantic coast** (Mauritania, Morocco) — Canaries Current
- **Baja California coast** (Mexico) — California Current

Fog is common along these coasts because moist ocean air moves over the cold current and is chilled below its dew point.

### 11.2 Warm currents and marine climates at high-latitude west coasts

Warm currents on west coasts at higher latitudes bring mild, moist conditions. The Gulf Stream and North Atlantic Drift keep north-western Europe (the British Isles, Scandinavia, western France) much milder in winter than their latitude would otherwise predict. Bergen in Norway (61°N) rarely freezes; comparable latitudes in eastern Canada are severely cold.

### 11.3 Warm currents and rainfall on tropical east coasts

The warm currents (Gulf Stream, Kuroshio, Agulhas) on the eastern coasts of continents in the tropics carry warm, moist air onshore and generate heavy rainfall. The south-east coast of the USA, southern Japan, and the east coast of southern Africa all show this pattern.

### 11.4 January isotherms

In January, in the Northern Hemisphere, landmasses have cooled more than the oceans (because of water's high specific heat). Isotherms therefore **deflect toward the equator over continents** (colder) and **toward the poles over oceans** (warmer) ([2024 Q18][pyq2024]). The inverse holds in July, when land heats faster. This land–sea contrast is the same mechanism that drives sea and land breezes at the daily scale.

## 12. Upwelling and fisheries

### 12.1 Upwelling mechanism

**Upwelling** is the rise of deeper water toward the surface. In major coastal systems, wind commonly blows **along the coast**. Wind stress and Earth's rotation produce **Ekman transport**: the net movement of the wind-driven surface layer is to the right of the wind in the Northern Hemisphere and to its left in the Southern Hemisphere. If this transport is offshore, deeper water rises near the coast to replace it ([INCOIS][incois-upwelling]). Offshore **water transport** does not require an offshore **wind**.

**Plan view and cross-section: a Northern Hemisphere west coast** — original schematic, not to scale. North is up in the plan view.

```text
PLAN VIEW                         CROSS-SECTION (looking north)
          N ↑                     offshore ←              coast
    OCEAN     | LAND               surface water ← ← ←       | LAND
wind ↓        |                   ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~|
wind ↓        |                                    ↑       /|
transport ←   |                              deeper water ↗ /
              |                   ________________________/
```

Here a southward alongshore wind gives westward transport (its right), away from the coast. Reverse hemisphere or wind direction and reconsider the geometry. Rising water supplies nutrients to the sunlit layer, supporting phytoplankton and fisheries.

The main wind-driven upwelling zones are:

- **Peru (Humboldt) Current coast** — equatorward alongshore winds favour offshore Ekman transport to their left in the Southern Hemisphere, supporting productive fisheries, notably anchoveta.
- **California Current coast** — north-west winds produce upwelling along the US west coast.
- **Benguela Current coast** — south-east trade winds; supports large sardine and anchovy fisheries off Namibia and South Africa.
- **Somali Current coast** — during the south-west monsoon, strong south-west winds create upwelling along the Somali and Arabian coasts.

### 12.2 Warm–cold current meeting zones

Where a warm current and a cold current converge, the temperature contrast creates turbulence that mixes nutrients upward from depth. The meeting zone (a **convergence zone**) supports huge concentrations of plankton and, in turn, fish. This is why the most productive fisheries in the world are at warm–cold convergences ([2013 Q59][pyq2013]):

- **Grand Banks** (off Newfoundland) — Gulf Stream meets Labrador Current; one of the richest cod fisheries historically.
- **North-west Pacific, off Japan** — Kuroshio meets Oyashio; the basis of Japan's large fishing industry.
- **South Atlantic** — Falkland meets Brazilian current; productive fishing off Argentina.

### 12.3 El Niño and fishery collapse

Under normal conditions, strong trade winds push warm water west across the Pacific, allowing upwelling along South America's coast. During **El Niño**, the trade winds weaken, warm water spreads east, and the thermocline deepens along the South American coast. Cold, nutrient-rich water can no longer reach the surface; phytoplankton crash; and the anchoveta fishery of Peru collapses. El Niño and its effects on the Indian monsoon are treated in detail in [Chapter 012](012-world-climates-and-the-indian-monsoon.md).

### 12.4 Ocean Mean Temperature

**Ocean Mean Temperature (OMT)** is the average temperature of the ocean down to the depth of the 26°C isotherm, which separates warm surface water from cooler sub-surface water. This isotherm lies at different depths in different parts of the ocean. OMT measured in the south-western Indian Ocean during January–March can be used to assess whether the June–September monsoon rainfall will be above or below the long-term mean: a warmer-than-normal upper ocean supplies more moisture and energy to the overlying atmosphere ([2020 Q45][pyq2020]). Statement 1 of that question (which said the 26°C isotherm lies at 129 m in the south-western Indian Ocean in January–March) is stated as an observation-dependent figure; NCERT does not fix it at a specific depth. Only Statement 2 — that OMT from January–March is useful for monsoon forecasting — is confirmed correct.

## 13. Marine productivity and ocean–atmosphere interaction

### 13.1 Primary producers in ocean food chains

The base of ocean food chains consists of microscopic photosynthetic organisms in the sunlit surface layer:

- **Phytoplankton** — the collective term for microscopic marine plants and plant-like organisms; they perform most marine photosynthesis.
- **Diatoms** — a group of phytoplankton with silica cell walls; among the most productive marine primary producers.
- **Cyanobacteria** — photosynthetic bacteria found throughout the sunlit ocean; some groups also fix atmospheric nitrogen; this is not a property of every cyanobacterium.

Diatoms and cyanobacteria are therefore **primary producers** in ocean food chains ([2021 Q90][pyq2021]). **Copepods** are tiny crustaceans — primary consumers (grazers on phytoplankton) — not producers. **Foraminifera** are single-celled heterotrophic protists — consumers — not producers.

Marine phytoplankton and photosynthetic bacteria together produce approximately **50% of the world's oxygen** ([2025 Q20][pyq2025]). The idea that rain forests produce more oxygen than oceans (Statement I of [2025 Q20][pyq2025]) is incorrect; a roughly half-global contribution does not imply that oceans necessarily exceed all terrestrial ecosystems combined. Statement III — that well-oxygenated surface water contains several times more oxygen than atmospheric air — is also incorrect; atmospheric air (~21% oxygen) contains far more oxygen per unit volume than even well-oxygenated seawater.

### 13.2 Indian Ocean Dipole

The **Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD)** is an ocean–atmosphere interaction in the Indian Ocean characterised by a difference in sea surface temperature (SST) between the **tropical western Indian Ocean** and the **tropical eastern Indian Ocean** ([2017 Q81][pyq2017]). A positive IOD means the western Indian Ocean is warmer than normal and the eastern cooler; a negative IOD is the reverse. This is covered in depth in [Chapter 012](012-world-climates-and-the-indian-monsoon.md).

The key correction for the PYQ ([2017 Q81][pyq2017]): Statement 1 is incorrect because IOD is defined in terms of the western versus eastern **Indian** Ocean — not the tropical Eastern **Pacific** Ocean (the Pacific is where ENSO operates). Statement 2 is correct: a positive IOD tends to produce above-average monsoon rainfall in India, partially offsetting a weak El Niño; a negative IOD can amplify El Niño's suppressive effect on the monsoon.

<a id="common-confusions"></a>

## 14. Common confusions

| Confusion | Clarification |
|---|---|
| **Gulf Stream is a cold current** | Wrong. Gulf Stream is warm. It is called "cold" in a PYQ as a false statement to test this ([2024 Q18][pyq2024]). |
| **Tides are caused by the Moon alone** | Wrong. All three — Moon's gravity, Sun's gravity, and centrifugal force — contribute ([2015 Q67][pyq2015]). |
| **Equatorial counter-current flows east because of Earth's rotation** | Wrong. It flows east because water piles up in the west (pushed by trade winds) and flows back east down the pressure gradient — the convergence of two equatorial currents ([2015 Q89][pyq2015]). |
| **IOD is defined relative to the Pacific Ocean** | Wrong. IOD is western Indian Ocean vs eastern Indian Ocean SST ([2017 Q81][pyq2017]). |
| **Copepods are primary producers** | Wrong. Copepods are primary consumers (they eat phytoplankton) ([2021 Q90][pyq2021]). |
| **Coastal upwelling requires wind blowing straight offshore** | Alongshore winds can cause offshore Ekman transport; deeper water rises to replace it. Check hemisphere and wind direction. |
| **Ocean salinity is uniform** | Evaporation, precipitation, runoff, ice formation/melt and circulation alter salinity. 24.7‰ is a density/freezing threshold, not a universal equatorial or polar value. |
| **Thermocline exists everywhere** | Wrong. In polar seas, the water column is uniformly cold from surface to floor; there is no thermocline. |

<a id="summary-of-key-concepts-and-takeaways"></a>

## 15. Summary of key concepts and takeaways

- Ocean relief provides the setting, while temperature, salinity and pressure influence density and vertical structure. A thermocline or halocline is a zone of rapid change, not a fixed universal depth.
- Waves mainly transmit energy; tides respond to astronomical forcing and local coastal geometry; currents transport water. Storm surge is a separate meteorological rise in sea level.
- Winds drive surface circulation, while density differences help drive deep overturning. These connected movements redistribute heat and influence coastal climate.
- Productivity requires light and nutrients. Offshore Ekman transport can bring nutrient-rich deep water upward; a warm surface alone does not establish productive fishing conditions.

## 16. UPSC-specific notes

<a id="static-notes"></a>

### 16.1 Static notes

| Topic | Prelims GS-I revision notes |
|---|---|
| Ocean-floor sequence | Distinguish continental shelf, slope, rise and abyssal plain; trenches are deep depressions and mid-oceanic ridges are submarine mountain systems. A geological shelf and a legal continental shelf are different concepts. |
| Temperature, salinity, density | Evaporation raises salinity; rain, runoff and ice melt lower it; freezing leaves the remaining water saltier. Colder or saltier seawater is generally denser, with pressure also relevant. Fresh water's maximum density near 4°C cannot simply be transferred to ordinary seawater. |
| Layering | Thermocline concerns temperature; halocline concerns salinity. Their depths and strength vary. Stratification can restrict nutrient exchange between deep water and the sunlit layer. |
| Waves and tides | Wave energy can travel without the same water parcel crossing an ocean. Spring tides occur near new/full moon, neaps near quarter phases; “spring” is not a season here. Tidal range depends on geometry as well as lunar/solar forcing; tide and storm surge are distinct. |
| Current map | Warm: Gulf Stream/North Atlantic Drift, Kuroshio, Brazil, Agulhas. Cold: Labrador, Oyashio, California, Humboldt, Benguela and West Australian. Link each to its ocean margin and flow direction, rather than memorising the adjective alone. The Somali circulation changes with monsoon winds. |
| Counter-current and overturning | Equatorial counter-current flows east against westward equatorial currents, responding to accumulated water/pressure gradients. Deep thermohaline circulation depends on density; it is not merely the surface gyre drawn at a greater depth. |
| Upwelling and ecology | Apply the hemisphere first: net Ekman transport is right of wind in the north, left in the south. Alongshore wind can produce offshore water transport and upwelling. Diatoms and cyanobacteria are producers; copepods and foraminifera are consumers. Nutrient supply and light matter together. |
| Atmosphere linkage | ENSO belongs to the Pacific; IOD compares two regions within the Indian Ocean. OMT incorporates subsurface warmth; do not turn a dated isotherm-depth figure into a constant. |

These are condensed from the chapter's [ocean properties][ncert-oceans], [ocean movements][ncert-movements], [NOAA tides][noaa-tides], [Scripps density][scripps-density] and [INCOIS upwelling][incois-upwelling] sources.

<a id="current-connections"></a>

### 16.2 Current connections

- **ENSO and fisheries:** the chapter's El Niño example links weakened trades and a deeper eastern-Pacific thermocline to reduced nutrient supply off Peru. Use that mechanism to interpret a dated fishery report; it does not establish the current catch or ocean state. The dated monsoon/ocean-state case is in [Chapter 012](012-world-climates-and-the-indian-monsoon.md).
- **Marine productivity:** [2025 Q20][pyq2025] uses the approximately half-global oxygen contribution of marine photosynthesis. Distinguish oxygen production from dissolved oxygen concentration; a producer's global contribution does not imply that seawater contains more oxygen per unit volume than air.
- **Ocean warming and circulation news:** separate a measured SST anomaly, a subsurface heat measure and an overturning-circulation assessment. Each needs its own location, observation period and source. [Update watch](#update-watch) applies before using these mechanisms as evidence for present conditions; this section adds no new AMOC trend or forecast.

## 1. Orientation

Of all the water on Earth, only about 2.7% is fresh — and most of that is locked in glaciers and ice caps. The fraction actually available in rivers, lakes and underground stores is a thin margin that all terrestrial life depends on. This chapter explains where that water comes from, how it moves through a landscape, and what happens when it accumulates underground or in lakes.

The chapter also introduces the applied ideas the paper tests: what a watershed development programme does, what sand mining does to underground water, how wetlands purify water, and how badly India's groundwater is overdrawn.

## 3. Foundations and key terms

| Term | Definition used in this chapter |
|---|---|
| **Watershed** | The boundary (a ridge line) separating two adjacent drainage basins; precipitation on one side drains to one river, precipitation on the other side drains to a different river. Also called a **drainage divide**. |
| **Aquifer** | A permeable underground rock or sediment layer that holds and transmits usable quantities of groundwater. |
| **Water table** | The upper surface of the saturated zone — the depth below which all pores in the rock or soil are filled with water. It rises after heavy rain and falls during drought. |
| **Recharge** | The addition of water to an aquifer from above: by percolation of rain and surface water through the unsaturated zone (natural recharge) or by deliberate human interventions (artificial recharge). |
| **River regime** | The pattern of a river's discharge (volume of flow) through the year; it depends on the mix of rainfall, snowmelt and groundwater feeding the river. |
| **Permeability** | A measure of how easily water moves through a material. Gravel is highly permeable, clay very low. |
| **Evapotranspiration** | The combined loss of water from a surface by evaporation (from open water and bare soil) and transpiration (from plant leaves). |

## 4. The hydrological cycle

The hydrological cycle is the continuous movement of water between the ocean, atmosphere, land surface and subsurface ([NCERT][ncert-water-atmos]). It has no beginning or end. The Sun provides the energy and gravity drives the return flow.

### 4.1 Stages

**Evaporation and transpiration.** The Sun heats the ocean (the main source), lakes, rivers, and moist soil, turning liquid water into vapour that rises into the atmosphere ([Testbook][testbook-hydro]). Plants also release water vapour through their leaves — this is transpiration. Together, these two processes are called **evapotranspiration**. Maximum evaporation occurs over the subtropical western Atlantic and Pacific, where warm ocean currents (Gulf Stream, Kuroshio) underlie dry, moving air.

**Condensation.** As moist air rises and cools, water vapour condenses around tiny particles of dust or salt (hygroscopic nuclei), forming cloud droplets or ice crystals. This releases latent heat back into the atmosphere — it is one of the main ways the tropics export energy poleward.

**Precipitation.** Cloud droplets coalesce until they are heavy enough to fall as rain, snow, sleet or hail depending on temperature. The three types of precipitation (convectional, orographic, cyclonic/frontal) were covered in [Chapter 011](011-winds-rainfall-and-weather-systems.md).

**Surface runoff.** Precipitation that does not soak into the ground flows overland into streams, rivers and eventually the ocean. Runoff is highest on steep, impermeable, saturated or frozen ground and lowest on flat, permeable, dry ground. Urbanisation replaces permeable soil with impermeable concrete, sharply increasing runoff and flood peaks.

**Infiltration and percolation.** Some precipitation soaks into the soil (infiltration). Water that moves deeper through soil and rock (percolation) eventually reaches the saturated zone and becomes groundwater. Infiltration is controlled by soil texture, slope, vegetation cover, and antecedent moisture.

**Groundwater flow.** Groundwater moves slowly through aquifers and discharges into rivers, lakes, springs and the ocean, completing the cycle. This baseflow sustains rivers during dry seasons.

### 4.2 Why the cycle matters

The hydrological cycle distributes fresh water over the land surface, moderates temperature (through latent heat), erodes and deposits sediment, and supports every terrestrial ecosystem. Human interventions — deforestation (reducing infiltration and increasing runoff), damming rivers, and over-extracting groundwater — alter each of these functions.

## 5. Global freshwater distribution

Water covers about 71% of Earth's surface, but the distribution by type is very uneven:

| Store | Approximate share of all water | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Oceans and seas | ~97.3% | Saline; not directly usable |
| Ice caps and glaciers | ~2.1% | Largest store of **fresh** water (~68.7% of all freshwater) |
| **Groundwater** | ~0.6% | Largest share of **liquid** fresh water |
| Soil moisture | ~0.001% | Directly supports plant growth |
| Freshwater lakes and rivers | ~0.009% | Most visible; smallest liquid store |
| Atmosphere | ~0.001% | Water vapour; short residence time |

Two PYQs directly test these proportions:

- [2013 Q21][pyq2013]: "On the planet Earth, most freshwater exists as ice caps and glaciers. Of the remaining freshwater, the largest proportion exists as groundwater." — correct; rivers and lakes hold far less ([2013 Q21][pyq2013], Answer: c).
- [2021 Q73][pyq2021]: Statement 1 — rivers and lakes hold more than groundwater — is **wrong**; groundwater greatly exceeds surface water. Statement 2 — polar ice caps and glaciers hold more than groundwater — is **correct** (Answer: b — only 2).

The practical implication: rivers and lakes are easy to access but small in volume; groundwater is large in volume but difficult to replenish once depleted; glaciers are vast but effectively inaccessible and are retreating with climate change ([Chapter 108](108-climate-science-impacts-and-responses.md)).

## 6. Drainage basins, watersheds and patterns

### 6.1 Drainage basin

A **drainage basin** (or catchment) is the total area of land that drains into a single river system. Every drop of rain that falls within the basin eventually flows into that river (unless it evaporates or percolates underground). The basin boundary is the **watershed** or **drainage divide** — high ground where rain falling on either side flows in different directions.

India classifies river basins by size: major (catchment above 20,000 km²; Ganga, Brahmaputra, Krishna), medium (2,000–20,000 km²), and minor ([NCERT][ncert-drainage]). About 77% of India's total drainage area flows to the Bay of Bengal; the remaining ~23% flows to the Arabian Sea via west-flowing rivers. The Western Ghats form the main drainage divide between the two systems.

### 6.2 Drainage patterns

The shape of a drainage network depends on rock type, structure and slope:

| Pattern | Appearance | Condition | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dendritic** | Tree with branches | Uniform, horizontal rock; most common | Northern Plains |
| **Trellis** | Rectangular grid with right-angle junctions | Alternating hard and soft rock bands (fold mountains) | Himalayan foothill rivers |
| **Rectangular** | Right-angle bends; less regular than trellis | Jointed or faulted rock | — |
| **Radial** | Rivers spread outward from a central high point | Dome or volcanic cone | Rivers from Amarkantak Plateau |
| **Centripetal** | Rivers converge into a central basin or lake | Basin or depression | Rivers draining into Loktak Lake |

### 6.3 Watersheds and development

Because a watershed is a self-contained hydrological unit, it is the natural unit for managing water, soil and vegetation together. The **Integrated Watershed Development Programme (IWDP)** works at the micro-watershed level to: (1) prevent soil runoff by building check dams and bunds; (3) harvest rainwater and recharge groundwater; and (4) regenerate natural vegetation through afforestation ([2014 Q53][pyq2014], Answer: c — options 1, 3 and 4). Option 2 — linking perennial rivers with seasonal rivers — is **not** an objective of watershed development; it describes a separate river-linking proposal.

## 7. River regimes

A river's **regime** is its characteristic pattern of discharge through the year, governed by its sources of water:

| Regime | Description | Indian examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Perennial** | Flows year-round; fed by snowmelt or glacier melt as well as rain; discharge varies but never drops to zero | Ganga, Brahmaputra, Indus (Himalayan rivers) |
| **Seasonal (non-perennial)** | Flows only during and shortly after the monsoon; dry or near-dry in summer | Most Peninsular rivers (Krishna, Kaveri, Godavari at their upper reaches) |
| **Ephemeral** | Flows only briefly during and after rain events; dry for most of the year | Luni (western Rajasthan); many streams in Thar Desert |

The contrast between Himalayan and Peninsular regimes explains India's uneven water availability: northern rivers are perennial and can supply water through the dry season; most southern rivers are seasonal and flow strongly only from June to October.

## 8. Lakes

Lakes form by several processes. Each type leaves a characteristic shape and location:

| Type | Origin | Indian example |
|---|---|---|
| **Oxbow** | A meander loop cut off when a river straightens | Lakes along Himalayan floodplains (terai) |
| **Glacial** | Carved by a glacier and filled by meltwater or rain | Dal Lake, Nainital |
| **Tectonic** | A block of crust subsides or tilts, forming a depression | Wular Lake (Kashmir), Kolleru Lake |
| **Crater / volcanic** | The vent of an extinct volcano fills with water | Lonar Lake (Maharashtra) |
| **Artificial / reservoir** | Constructed by damming a river | **Kodaikanal** (Tamil Nadu) |
| **Lagoon** | A coastal water body cut off from the sea by a sandbar or spit | Chilika Lake, Vembanad Lake |

[2018 Q100][pyq2018] asks which of Kodaikanal, Kolleru, Nainital and Renuka is artificial. The answer is **Kodaikanal** — it was created in 1863 by damming a stream on the Palani Hills. Kolleru is a tectonic lake, Nainital is glacial, and Renuka (Himachal Pradesh) is natural. The common confusion is assuming a hill-station lake must be natural; Kodaikanal's lake is a rare exception.

## 9. Groundwater: zones, aquifers and springs

### 9.1 Zones below the surface

Water moving downward through the soil and rock passes through two zones:

**Unsaturated zone (vadose zone).** The pores contain both air and water. Soil moisture in this zone supports plant roots; excess moisture percolates downward.

**Saturated zone (phreatic zone).** All pores are filled with water. The upper boundary of the saturated zone is the **water table**. The water table is not flat — it broadly mirrors the land surface above it, rising under hills and falling under valleys. Springs form wherever the water table intersects the ground surface.

### 9.2 Aquifer types

An **aquifer** is a saturated, permeable geological formation — gravel, sand, fractured rock or limestone — that yields significant quantities of water to wells or springs. The overlying and underlying layers (aquicludes or aquitards) may be impermeable rock or clay that restricts water movement.

| Aquifer type | Description | Pressure | Well behaviour |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Unconfined** | Water table is the upper boundary; directly recharged by rain percolating from above | At atmospheric pressure | Water in well stands at water-table depth |
| **Confined (artesian)** | Sandwiched between two impermeable layers; not directly recharged from directly above | Above atmospheric pressure (from water entering the aquifer at a higher elevation) | Water rises in well above the aquifer top; may flow at the surface without pumping (artesian well) |
| **Perched** | A localised unconfined aquifer sitting on an impermeable lens above the main water table | Atmospheric | Often small and unreliable; dries in drought |

An **artesian well** flows naturally when the pressure head in a confined aquifer is high enough to push water above the land surface. The name comes from the Artois region of France, where natural flowing wells were long known.

### 9.3 Springs

A spring forms where groundwater reaches the surface naturally. Types include:

- **Depression spring:** where the land surface dips below the water table.
- **Contact spring:** where permeable rock overlies impermeable rock and water is forced to the surface at the boundary.
- **Artesian spring:** where a confined aquifer reaches the surface naturally and pressure drives water out.

### 9.4 Effects of human activity on groundwater

**Sand mining from riverbeds** causes multiple problems ([2018 Q77][pyq2018], Answer: b — options 2 and 3):

- Mining removes the sand and gravel that act as natural filters and recharge conduits. The water table beneath the riverbed drops.
- Chemicals, fuel and silt from mining operations enter the groundwater, polluting it.
- Option 1 — "decreased salinity in the river" — is not a consequence of sand mining; it describes reduced salt content, which has no clear causal link to mining.

**Over-extraction** of groundwater for irrigation lowers the water table over time, drying shallow wells, reducing spring flows, increasing pumping costs and, in coastal areas, drawing in saline water (seawater intrusion).

## 10. India's groundwater: extraction and stress

### 10.1 Scale of extraction

India is the world's largest user of groundwater. It draws on groundwater for more than 60% of its irrigation water and for a large part of its domestic water supply, particularly in rural areas. This is partly because India has extensive alluvial plains with accessible aquifers, partly because surface water is seasonal, and partly because individual farmers can drill wells without coordination.

### 10.2 Key facts tested in PYQs

[2020 Q44][pyq2020] tests three statements about CGWA and Indian groundwater:

- **Statement 2 (correct): CGWA was formed under the Environment (Protection) Act.** The Central Ground Water Authority was constituted in 1997 under Section 3(3) of the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986.
- **Statement 3 (correct): India has the largest area under groundwater irrigation in the world.** India uses more groundwater for irrigation than any other country, overtaking China and the USA.
- **Statement 1: 36% of India's districts are classified as "overexploited" or "critical" by CGWA.** This figure is not confirmed as the stated answer; the answer (b — 2 and 3) confirms only statements 2 and 3.

[2023 Q32][pyq2023] uses the UN World Water Development Report 2022, which states India extracts more than a quarter (>25%) of the world's annual groundwater withdrawal (Statement I — **correct**). Statement II claims this is because India needs to satisfy the drinking and sanitation needs of its ~18% share of world population — this is **incorrect**: the driver is agriculture (mostly irrigation), not drinking and sanitation ([2023 Q32][pyq2023], Answer: c — Statement I correct, Statement II incorrect).

### 10.3 Artificial recharge

Where natural recharge is insufficient to match extraction, **artificial recharge** restores some water to the aquifer:

- **Recharge basins and check dams:** shallow impoundments that slow runoff and allow it to percolate into the ground. The IWDP extensively uses this method.
- **Rainwater harvesting and percolation wells:** directing rooftop or surface runoff into shafts that reach permeable layers.
- **Treated wastewater injection:** pumping reclaimed water back underground, common in water-scarce urban areas.

The Delhi Ridge, the Aravallis and the Sahyadri (Western Ghats) function as natural watershed boundaries and recharge zones; protecting their vegetation maintains the recharge needed by the rivers and aquifers they feed.

## 11. Wetlands

A **wetland** is land that is saturated with water seasonally or permanently — it may be a marsh, swamp, floodplain, mangrove, peatland or shallow lake. Wetlands function as:

- **Lungs?** No — that metaphor is used for forests, which fix carbon and produce oxygen. Wetlands produce some oxygen, but the kidneys metaphor is more apt.
- **Kidneys** ([2022 Q45][pyq2022], Answer: d): Aquatic plants, algae and microbial mats in wetlands **absorb heavy metals and excess nutrients** (nitrogen and phosphorus from fertilizer runoff), filtering polluted water before it reaches rivers or the ocean. This purification function mirrors what kidneys do in the body.

The other options in that question describe functions wetlands perform but that are not analogous to what a kidney does: (a) describes the water cycle (not filtration); (b) describes biodiversity/food chain support (ecological, not filtration); (c) describes sedimentation balance (geomorphic, not filtration).

Wetlands also reduce flooding by absorbing peak flows, sustain dry-season flows by releasing stored water, and support exceptionally high biodiversity. The Ramsar Convention lists wetlands of international importance; India has over 80 Ramsar sites, including Chilika Lake (Odisha), Keoladeo National Park (Rajasthan), and the Loktak Lake (Manipur). Wetlands are covered further in [Chapter 102](102-habitats-biomes-and-biodiversity-services.md).

## 12. Water stress and scarcity

**Water stress** is a condition in which water demand approaches or exceeds available supply, forcing trade-offs between competing uses. It is typically measured per capita per year:

| Threshold | Condition |
|---|---|
| Below 1,000 m³/person/year | Water scarcity (chronic shortage) |
| 1,000–1,700 m³/person/year | Water stress (periodic shortage) |
| Above 1,700 m³/person/year | Adequate supply under normal conditions |

India's per capita freshwater availability has fallen sharply as population has grown and pollution has reduced usable supply. Many river basins in peninsular India — the Krishna, Kaveri and Sabarmati — are already in water scarcity. The Ganga-Brahmaputra basin retains higher availability; the western and peninsular regions face chronic stress.

Groundwater depletion is accelerating this stress because groundwater sustains dry-season flows in many rivers (baseflow). When aquifers are overdrawn, rivers that depended on groundwater baseflow become more seasonal. This is a feedback: surface water scarcity pushes more extraction from groundwater, which in turn reduces surface water flows.

## 13. Common confusions

| Confusion | Clarification |
|---|---|
| **Most freshwater is in rivers and lakes** | Wrong. The largest store of liquid freshwater is groundwater. The largest store of all freshwater is glaciers and ice caps ([2013 Q21][pyq2013], [2021 Q73][pyq2021]). |
| **Linking perennial and seasonal rivers is a watershed-development benefit** | Wrong. Watershed development works within a single catchment on soil conservation, rainwater harvesting and vegetation; it does not link rivers ([2014 Q53][pyq2014]). |
| **Sand mining decreases river salinity** | Incorrect causal claim. Sand mining lowers the water table and pollutes groundwater; it has no meaningful effect on river salinity ([2018 Q77][pyq2018]). |
| **Kodaikanal lake is natural** | Wrong — it is artificial, created by damming in 1863. Nainital and Kolleru are natural ([2018 Q100][pyq2018]). |
| **India's large groundwater extraction is for drinking water** | Wrong. The driver is agricultural irrigation. Drinking and sanitation account for a small share ([2023 Q32][pyq2023]). |
| **A watershed and a drainage basin are the same thing** | A drainage basin is the area; a watershed is its boundary — the ridge or divide between two basins. |
| **A confined aquifer has no water pressure** | The opposite: confined aquifers are under pressure (from water entering at a higher elevation), which is why artesian wells flow without pumping. |
| **Wetlands are lungs** | Forests are conventionally called lungs; wetlands are kidneys — they filter and purify water ([2022 Q45][pyq2022]). |

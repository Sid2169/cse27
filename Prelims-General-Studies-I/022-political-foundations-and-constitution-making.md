## 1. Begin with the problem of public power

Imagine a town needs a road, a school and a way to resolve disputes. Someone must collect funds, choose priorities, implement decisions and settle disagreements. Giving an authority these powers creates another problem: what prevents it from collecting money arbitrarily, silencing criticism or favouring its supporters?

Political institutions address both sides: they enable common decisions and constrain how decisions are made. Keep three questions separate throughout this chapter:

- **Authority:** Who may decide?
- **Limits:** What may that authority do, and what must it respect?
- **Accountability:** How can people question, replace or obtain a remedy against it?

An institution's name does not answer all three. An elected body may misuse power; a written rule may authorise oppressive conduct. Understanding the arrangement requires examining powers, limits and remedies together. NCERT introduces constitutions through precisely these problems of coordination, allocation of authority, restraint and social aspirations. [S1]

### State, government and society

| Term | Meaning in this chapter | Concrete distinction |
|---|---|---|
| **State** | The enduring political organisation exercising public authority over a population within a territory; sovereignty is a defining element in the conventional account | Elections can change the government while the State continues |
| **Government** | The institutions and office-holders through which public authority is exercised | In ordinary political speech, “the government” often means the ministry currently in office |
| **Society** | The wider network of people, communities and relationships | Families, associations and religious communities are not all government institutions |
| **Nation** | A community understood as sharing a political identity or belonging | A national identity is a different concept from an administrative institution |
| **Constitution** | Fundamental rules establishing public institutions, distributing power, limiting its exercise and expressing governing commitments | It determines the framework within which governments act |

Usage matters. In “the State of Maharashtra”, *State* means a constituent unit of the Indian Union. In political theory, *the State* can mean the whole political organisation. In Part III of India's Constitution, Article 12 gives “the State” a specific inclusive definition covering Union and State governments and legislatures, and local or other authorities within its stated scope. Do not substitute one meaning for another. [S2, Article 12]

**Worked distinction:** A ministry loses an election. Its ministers leave office, but courts, public offices and legal obligations do not disappear. This is a change of government, not the creation of a new State.

## 2. Sovereignty and constitutional government

### Sovereignty: independence of public authority

**Sovereignty** means supreme authority in the relevant political order. Its external aspect concerns independence from another State's governing authority; its internal aspect concerns ultimate public authority within the political community. The Preamble describes India as sovereign. [S2, Preamble]

This does not mean every government official has unlimited power. Separate the sovereignty of the political community from the competence of a particular institution. A minister cannot justify an unauthorised action by saying that India is sovereign.

Nor is international cooperation automatically a loss of sovereignty. As a conceptual example, a country can undertake a treaty obligation through its own authorised institutions. The existence of an obligation and the existence of an external ruler are different facts. Whether a particular treaty creates a particular domestic power must be answered from the applicable legal provisions.

### A constitution and constitutionalism are different ideas

A **constitution** supplies the fundamental framework. **Constitutionalism** is the principle that public power must operate under effective limits, rather than the unrestricted will of the rulers. A country can possess an impressive constitutional document while its rulers evade its limits. [S1]

A constitutional government is therefore a **limited government**. “Limited” refers to the scope and manner of its authority, not the number of ministries or the size of its welfare budget. A large administration can be constitutionally limited; a small administration can act arbitrarily.

For example, compare two hypothetical orders:

- “The officer may close any publication merely because the officer dislikes it.”
- “Any restriction must have a valid legal basis, satisfy applicable constitutional conditions and remain open to legal challenge.”

The second arrangement constrains power. It does not guarantee that every decision will be correct, but it provides standards against which a decision can be examined.

**Rule of law** means that public power is governed by law rather than personal whim, with equality before the law as a central commitment. Merely passing a law does not settle whether it is constitutional: an ordinary law must itself satisfy the higher constitutional framework. Article 13 addresses laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights; Articles 14 and 32 provide important parts of this framework. Their operation is taught in [Chapter 024](024-fundamental-rights-and-constitutional-remedies.md). [S2]

For a conceptual question, distinguish four different properties: having a legislature, being popular, allowing multiple parties, and being limited by constitutional rules. None of the first three, by itself, establishes the fourth.

## 3. Democracy, republic and parliamentary government

These terms answer different questions. Learn their relationship rather than treating them as synonyms.

| Principle or arrangement | Question it answers | What it does not establish by itself |
|---|---|---|
| **Democracy** | How do people participate in, authorise and hold public power accountable? | That every majority decision respects rights |
| **Republic** | Is the head of State a hereditary monarch? | That elections and opposition are free |
| **Parliamentary government** | How does the political executive depend on the legislature? | That the head of State is necessarily elected |
| **Constitutionalism** | Is the exercise of power effectively limited? | That limits are respected merely because they are written |

### Democracy: more than counting votes

In a **direct democracy**, people themselves decide public questions. In a **representative democracy**, people choose representatives to make decisions and remain answerable to them. India's national parliamentary institutions operate through representation. Article 326 establishes adult suffrage for elections to the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies, subject to its conditions and disqualifications. *Adult suffrage* means voting eligibility based on adulthood, rather than a general property or educational qualification; the present constitutional voting age is eighteen. [S2]

Elections help authorise and replace rulers. Meaningful political choice also requires space to criticise, organise and hear alternatives. A **constitutional democracy** combines democratic authorisation with constitutional restraints. The majority chooses within the constitutional framework; it does not acquire a licence to disregard every minority interest or right. [S1]

**Worked distinction:** Suppose a majority supports excluding a religious community from a public service solely because of its religion. The number supporting the policy answers the question of popularity. It does not answer the separate question of constitutional validity.

### Republic: non-hereditary head of State

A republic rejects hereditary monarchy as the basis of the head of State's office. In India, the President is elected indirectly through the electoral college specified in Article 54. A direct popular election for President is not necessary for India to be a republic. Detailed composition and election procedure belong to [Chapter 026](026-president-and-vice-president.md). [S2]

The categories are logically independent. A hypothetical country with an elected President but no meaningful political competition may be republican in institutional form without being democratic in practice. Conversely, a hereditary monarch can coexist with an elected, accountable ministry: parliamentary government does not inherently require a republic.

### Parliamentary government: the executive depends on legislative confidence

Three functions help explain the arrangement:

- The **legislature** makes laws and scrutinises the executive.
- The **executive** administers public affairs and implements laws and policies.
- The **judiciary** adjudicates disputes and interprets applicable law.

These are principal functions, not claims that each institution performs only one kind of act. In a parliamentary system there is an institutional connection between the political executive and legislature. The ministry must retain the confidence of the relevant elected house. NCERT explains this through the distinction between the formal head and the politically responsible executive. [S3]

At the Union level, distinguish the **President**, the constitutional head of State, from the **Prime Minister**, who heads the Council of Ministers. Article 74 provides for ministerial aid and advice to the President; Article 75(3) makes the Council collectively responsible to the **Lok Sabha**, the House of the People. The President can require reconsideration of advice but must act on the advice tendered after reconsideration. This is a constitutional office with specified powers, not a personally governing monarch. [S2]

**Collective responsibility** means that the ministry is answerable as a body. Losing the Lok Sabha's confidence is different from losing a vote on any arbitrary proposal. Do not infer that every legislative defeat automatically amounts to a loss of confidence. The detailed procedures belong to Chapters [027](027-prime-minister-ministers-and-administration.md) and [029](029-houses-membership-and-parliamentary-procedure.md). [S3]

In a presidential arrangement, the executive's ordinary tenure does not rest on the same continuing legislative-confidence relationship. Thus, “elected President” identifies a method of selection; it does not by itself identify a presidential system.

## 4. Secularism and the other constitutional commitments

### Secularism: religious freedom and equal citizenship

**Secularism** concerns the relationship between public power, religious communities and individual freedom. In the Indian constitutional setting, religious identity must not become a basis for unequal civic standing, and the State must protect freedom of conscience. This is different from demanding that citizens abandon religion. [S4]

Indian secularism also cannot be reduced to “the State never interacts with religion”. Article 25 protects religious freedom subject to its stated limits and preserves State powers concerning associated secular activities and social reform. The distinction is between constitutionally justified action and religious domination, rather than between all contact and no contact. Detailed rights and exceptions belong to Chapter 024. [S2]

**Illustration:** A public authority denying an otherwise eligible person a benefit solely because of their faith raises a question of equal treatment. A general public-health measure affecting gatherings raises a different question: what lawful restrictions can apply, and are they applied constitutionally? Calling both actions “interference with religion” conceals the distinction.

In **Balram Singh v. Union of India, 2024 INSC 893**, the Supreme Court rejected the challenge to the insertion of “socialist” and “secular” in the Preamble. The Court explains secularism through equality and religious freedom, rather than hostility towards religious practice (paragraphs 3–4). It also explains that Indian constitutional socialism does not impose one exclusive economic model or prohibit private enterprise (paragraphs 5–6). [S5]

### Socialism, justice, liberty, equality and fraternity

In this constitutional setting, **socialism** expresses a commitment to social and economic justice. It does not allow you to infer that every productive asset must be owned by government. The constitutional goal and the detailed policy instruments chosen to pursue it are separate questions. [S5]

Read the remaining commitments as connected ideas:

- **Justice:** Address unfair social relations, economic deprivation and unequal political standing.
- **Liberty:** Protect the space to think, express, believe and worship, within the applicable constitutional framework.
- **Equality:** Reject inferior civic status and enable fair opportunity; equality does not mean identical treatment in every circumstance.
- **Fraternity:** Sustain a sense of common membership that respects each person's dignity across social differences.

These are explanatory meanings; the Preamble's exact allocation of words is set out in Section 6. Their relationship matters. Formal permission to apply for a job is not the same as a realistic opportunity to qualify for it. Conversely, a policy claiming to promote collective welfare still needs constitutional authority and must respect applicable rights. Do not use one ideal as an automatic answer to every conflict involving another. [S1, S4]

## 5. How the Constitution came to be made

### A short colonial bridge

Under colonial government, institutions operated under authority ultimately controlled from Britain. Legislative councils and administrative reforms developed within this unequal relationship. They were not equivalent to a sovereign democratic constitution made by India's people.

| Stage | Change to remember | Why it matters here |
|---|---|---|
| **1773: Regulating Act** | British parliamentary supervision over Company government increased | Public institutions were being reorganised under imperial authority |
| **1858: Crown rule** | Rule passed from the East India Company to the British Crown | Company rule and Crown rule are distinct stages |
| **1909: Indian Councils Act** | Councils expanded and separate Muslim electorates were introduced | Representation expanded through restricted and communal arrangements |
| **1919: Government of India Act** | Provincial dyarchy and a bicameral central legislature were introduced | Representation did not mean full responsible government |
| **1935: Government of India Act** | Provincial autonomy and a proposed all-India federal structure | Provincial reforms operated; the proposed all-India federation did not come into being |
| **1946: Cabinet Mission Plan** | Provided the scheme under which the Constituent Assembly was formed | Constitution-making began before independence |
| **1947: Independence and Partition** | British rule ended and the Assembly's composition changed | Independence preceded the Constitution's general commencement |

For the colonial sequence, see the President's 2017 lecture on parliamentary history and Parliament's historical account. The lecture contains an imprecise description of provincial reserved subjects; use only its broad chronology here, not that description. [S6, S7]

**Dyarchy** means a division of provincial subjects between two branches of executive authority: reserved subjects under the Governor's control and transferred subjects administered by ministers responsible to the provincial legislature. **Provincial autonomy** under the 1935 framework replaced provincial dyarchy, while retaining important gubernatorial safeguards and powers. The proposed federation and provincial autonomy were separate components: implementation of one does not prove implementation of the other. [S7]

This bridge explains institutional inheritance without equating colonial authority with popular sovereignty. Detailed nationalist movements and negotiations are studied in Chapters [086](086-european-arrival-and-british-expansion.md)–[097](097-negotiations-partition-and-independence.md).

### Who made the Constitution?

The **Constituent Assembly** was the body that deliberated on and adopted the Constitution. *Constituent power* concerns establishing or altering the fundamental constitutional framework; it differs from an ordinary legislature's work of making laws within that framework.

Under the Cabinet Mission scheme, the intended membership was **389**: 292 provincial representatives, four representatives of Chief Commissioners' Provinces and 93 from princely states. Provincial representatives were chosen indirectly through the Provincial Legislative Assemblies. Princely-state representation followed a different selection arrangement; it was not a nationwide direct election. After Partition, the Assembly's strength became **299**. These figures refer to different stages, not rival counts of the same event. [S8, S9]

The Assembly was **not elected by universal adult franchise**. That fact should not be replaced with the claim that its provincial members were simply nominated by political parties. Indirect election and nomination are different procedures. Its authority also drew on the national movement, deliberation and a wide range of represented views; this is an account of its political legitimacy, not a claim that its selection met today's universal-franchise standard. [S1, S8]

### Keep the offices and dates distinct

| Date or person | Role or event | Frequent confusion |
|---|---|---|
| **9 December 1946** | First sitting; **Sachchidananda Sinha** served as Provisional President | Confusing the provisional presiding officer with the later President |
| **11 December 1946** | **Rajendra Prasad** elected permanent President of the Assembly | Confusing this office with chairmanship of the Drafting Committee |
| **13 December 1946** | **Jawaharlal Nehru** moved the Objectives Resolution | Confusing moving a resolution with its adoption |
| **22 January 1947** | Objectives Resolution adopted | Confusing this with adoption of the Constitution |
| **29 August 1947** | Drafting Committee appointed, chaired by **B. R. Ambedkar** | Assigning the committee's creation to November 1949 |
| **B. N. Rau** | Constitutional Adviser | Confusing the advisory role with committee chairmanship |
| **26 November 1949** | Constitution adopted | Treating adoption and general commencement as the same event |
| **24 January 1950** | Members signed the Constitution | Confusing signing with adoption |
| **26 January 1950** | General commencement; India became a republic | Saying independence began only on this date |

Historical sources: Parliament's Assembly account, the official Constitution Day backgrounder and NCERT for the Assembly's work; Parliament's reproduced Objectives Resolution for its dates. [S1, S8–S11]; Rau’s advisory role is also recorded in the [Assembly debates of 14 July 1947](https://eparlib.sansad.in/bitstream/123456789/762957/1/cad_14-07-1947.pdf), published by the Lok Sabha Secretariat (accessed 23 November 2026).

The **Objectives Resolution** stated foundational commitments, including authority derived from the people, justice, freedoms, equality and safeguards. It helped shape the Preamble; it was neither the complete Constitution nor an ordinary statute governing day-to-day administration. [S10]

The Constitution was not the unaided work of one author. Committees considered subjects; advisers and the Drafting Committee contributed to formulation; the Assembly debated and adopted provisions. Ambedkar's drafting leadership and Prasad's presidency were different contributions to a collective process. [S1, S8]

### Adoption is not the same as commencement

**Adoption** means formal acceptance of the constitutional text. **Commencement** means that provisions become legally operative. Article 394 brought specified provisions into force immediately, while the remaining provisions commenced on 26 January 1950. Therefore, “every provision first came into force on 26 January 1950” is inaccurate. [S2, Article 394]

Remember the sequence: constitution-making started in 1946; independence came in 1947; adoption occurred in 1949; general commencement followed in 1950. Independence and republican government answer different historical questions.

## 6. Read the Preamble as a structured statement

The **Preamble** is the introductory statement of constitutional authority, the character of the polity, its commitments and adoption. Read it in four groups rather than as an undifferentiated slogan. [S2, Preamble]

| Group | What the text identifies | Reading discipline |
|---|---|---|
| Source of authority | The people of India | Distinguish the claimed ultimate source from the Assembly's actual selection procedure |
| Character of the polity | Sovereign, socialist, secular, democratic, republic | Each term expresses a different attribute |
| Commitments | Justice, liberty, equality and fraternity, with their specified dimensions | Do not exchange the wording attached to different commitments |
| Act and date | Adoption, enactment and giving the Constitution to ourselves on 26 November 1949 | The date remains the adoption date despite later amendments |

The important word associations are:

| Commitment | Specified dimensions or relationship |
|---|---|
| Justice | Social, economic and political |
| Liberty | Thought, expression, belief, faith and worship |
| Equality | Status and opportunity |
| Fraternity | Dignity of the individual, and unity and integrity of the Nation |

**Worked reading:** “Economic” is attached to justice; “economic liberty” is not an expressly listed Preamble phrase. That textual distinction does not establish that economic freedom is absent from the rest of the Constitution. Always separate “not listed here” from “not recognised anywhere”. [S2]

### Original wording and later amendment

The original description was **sovereign democratic republic**. Section 2 of the **Constitution (Forty-second Amendment) Act, 1976** added **socialist** and **secular**, and added **integrity** to the phrase concerning national unity. The Preamble amendment took effect on **3 January 1977**, as recorded in the official constitutional footnotes. Calling it the 1976 amendment and giving its 1977 commencement date are compatible statements. [S2]

Do not infer that religious freedom and equal citizenship first appeared only when the word “secular” was inserted. Their substantive constitutional foundations existed independently of that later wording. Similarly, the amendment changed the Preamble without replacing its adoption date. [S5]

### Legal role: part of the Constitution, not an independent power

The Preamble is part of the Constitution and helps explain its purposes. It is not independently enforceable as if each aspiration were a separate operative right, nor is it an independent grant of power to Parliament or the executive. The Government of India explanatory note on the Preamble distinguishes its interpretive role from the grant of enforceable substantive rights. [S12]

For example, a ministry cannot introduce a tax merely by citing economic justice in the Preamble. The question remains: where does the Constitution authorise the action, and what procedure applies? Article 265 requires authority of law for taxation. Likewise, a person seeking a judicial remedy must identify the relevant legal right and jurisdiction rather than rely only on a general aspiration. [S2]

The Preamble can be amended, subject to constitutional limits; it is not immune from amendment simply because it records a 1949 adoption date. The Supreme Court reaffirmed Parliament's power to amend it in the 2024 decision discussed above. The detailed basic-structure limitation belongs to Chapter 023. [S5]

## 8. What to retain before proceeding

A political community can be sovereign while its institutions are constitutionally limited. A republic has a non-hereditary head of State; a democracy requires public authorisation and accountability; parliamentary government makes the ministry dependent on legislative confidence. None of these labels is a substitute for the others.

For constitution-making, retain the difference between indirect provincial elections and universal adult franchise; between the Assembly's President, Drafting Committee chair and Constitutional Adviser; and between adoption and commencement. For the Preamble, retain its word associations and its interpretive role without inventing an independent power or remedy.

## 1. Use the Constitution as a document you can navigate

Suppose a report says Parliament has changed a State's name. Three questions arise: who had authority to make the change, what procedure was required, and whether the Constitution itself had to be amended through Article 368. A political principle such as democracy does not answer those procedural questions. You must locate and read the relevant provisions.

Use the [Legislative Department's official constitutional text][constitution]. The edition used here is dated **10 September 2026**. A search result or an isolated quotation is only a starting point: read the heading, the whole provision, its exceptions and the footnotes.

<a id="the-units-of-the-document"></a>

### 1.1 The units of the document

| Unit | Function | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Preamble** | Introduces the source of authority, character and commitments of the polity | Its legal role was explained in Chapter 022 |
| **Part** | Groups related provisions under a broad subject | Part II concerns citizenship |
| **Chapter within a Part** | Subdivides some large Parts | Part V separates the Union executive, Parliament and other subjects |
| **Article** | A numbered constitutional provision | Article 3 concerns changes to States |
| **Clause or sub-clause** | Subdivides a provision into more precise rules | Article 19(1)(a): Article 19, clause (1), sub-clause (a) |
| **Schedule** | Contains constitutional lists, forms or detailed arrangements linked to articles | The First Schedule identifies States and Union territories |
| **Proviso** | Qualifies the rule to which it is attached | The proviso to Article 3 imposes procedural requirements |
| **Explanation** | Clarifies how a provision is to be understood | Article 3 gives a special meaning to “State” for specified purposes |

A schedule is part of the Constitution, not merely an informal appendix. Conversely, a publisher's footnote explains the text's history or legal status; it is not itself an enacted constitutional provision. Both can matter to accurate reading. [Constitution, contents and cited articles][constitution]

**Worked navigation:** To identify the speech provision, open Part III, locate Article 19 and then clause (1)(a). Do not stop there: clause (2) identifies the framework for permitted restrictions. Whether a particular restriction is valid belongs to Chapter 024. The lesson here is that reading the entitlement without its qualification produces an incomplete rule.

<a id="do-not-confuse-numbering-with-a-count"></a>

### 1.2 Do not confuse numbering with a count

The original Constitution had 395 articles, 22 Parts and eight schedules. Later changes inserted lettered provisions and Parts, omitted others and added schedules. For example, Article 21A sits between Articles 21 and 22; Part IVA follows Part IV; Part VII is marked omitted. The current edition contains twelve schedules. Do not count current provisions merely by reading the last article number, or infer that every number has remained operative. [Official contents and amendment footnotes][constitution]

Some important locations are:

| Subject | Location |
|---|---|
| Union and territory | Part I, Articles 1–4 |
| Citizenship | Part II, Articles 5–11 |
| Fundamental Rights | Part III |
| Directive Principles | Part IV |
| Fundamental Duties | Part IVA |
| Union institutions / State institutions | Parts V / VI |
| Union territories | Part VIII |
| Panchayats / municipalities | Parts IX / IXA |
| Union–State relations | Part XI |
| Elections | Part XV |
| Official language | Part XVII |
| Emergencies | Part XVIII |
| Amendment | Part XX, Article 368 |

This is a navigation aid, not a claim that each subject appears nowhere else. Language rights, for example, also appear in Part III.

## 2. Know what each schedule does

Start with subject–schedule associations. Detailed rules are taught in later institutional chapters.

| Schedule | Main subject | How to avoid a common confusion |
|---|---|---|
| **First** | States and Union territories and their territories | Territory, not legislative subject lists |
| **Second** | Provisions concerning emoluments, allowances and privileges for specified constitutional offices | Not the oath forms |
| **Third** | Forms of oaths or affirmations for specified offices | Not every constitutional oath is here; the President's oath is in Article 60 |
| **Fourth** | Allocation of Rajya Sabha seats | Representation, not territorial boundaries |
| **Fifth** | Administration and control of Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes | It is distinct from the Sixth Schedule's specified tribal-area arrangements |
| **Sixth** | Administration of tribal areas in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram | Not a framework applying to every northeastern State |
| **Seventh** | Union, State and Concurrent Lists | Distribution of legislative subjects |
| **Eighth** | Listed languages | Inclusion does not make a language an official language of every government |
| **Ninth** | Specified Acts and Regulations associated with Article 31B | Inclusion does not create absolute immunity from judicial review |
| **Tenth** | Disqualification on the ground of defection | Not a list of recognised parties |
| **Eleventh** | Matters associated with Panchayats | Read with Article 243G; listing alone does not automatically transfer every power |
| **Twelfth** | Matters associated with municipalities | Read with Article 243W; distinct from the Panchayat list |

Source: [official schedules and Articles 60, 243G and 243W][constitution]. The Ninth Schedule qualification is explained in Part 3. The Fifth and Sixth Schedules' institutions belong to [Chapter 038](038-scheduled-areas-and-community-rights.md); local-government powers belong to [Chapter 037](037-panchayats-and-municipalities.md). Those are planned follow-on chapters.

The First and Fourth Schedules often change together when States are reorganised: one records territory, the other parliamentary representation. That is a relationship between two different constitutional functions, not interchangeable content.

## 3. India, its States and its territory

Article 1 describes India, that is Bharat, as a **Union of States**. Its territorial description includes the territories of States, specified Union territories and other territories that may be acquired. A **Union territory** is a constitutional category distinct from a State; the detailed arrangements differ, including whether a legislature exists. Those differences are covered in [Chapter 034](034-state-executives-legislatures-and-union-territories.md). [Articles 1 and 239 onwards][constitution]

“Union” does not mean State governments are merely municipal offices. The Constitution distributes powers between levels of government. It does mean you must look to the constitutional framework, rather than assume that each State can decide independently whether it remains in India. Federal relations are developed in [Chapter 035](035-federal-relations-and-special-arrangements.md).

<a id="articles-2-3-and-4-perform-different-jobs"></a>

### 3.1 Articles 2, 3 and 4 perform different jobs

| Provision | Function |
|---|---|
| **Article 2** | Parliament may admit into the Union, or establish, new States by law on terms and conditions it considers appropriate |
| **Article 3** | Parliament may form new States and alter the areas, boundaries or names of existing States through the specified process |
| **Article 4** | Laws under Articles 2 and 3 may make necessary First/Fourth Schedule changes and supplemental, incidental or consequential provisions; they are not amendments for Article 368 purposes |

Read Article 3's clauses rather than reducing it to “Parliament may rename a State”: it also allows separation and combination of territory, increases and decreases in area, and boundary changes. [Articles 2–4][constitution]

<a id="consultation-is-not-a-veto"></a>

### 3.2 Consultation is not a veto

For a bill under Article 3:

1. Introduction may be in **either House of Parliament**, but requires the **President's recommendation**.
2. Where a proposal affects the area, boundaries or name of a State, the President must refer the bill to that State's legislature for its **views**, within the specified period or an extended period the President allows.
3. Parliament acts through legislation. The Constitution does not require that State legislature's **consent** as a condition of passage.

The distinction is substantive: a body can have a right to express its views without having the power to block the decision. Parliament need not accept an adverse State view. The expiry-of-period wording also means that the State cannot turn non-response into an indefinite veto. [Article 3 proviso][constitution]

**Important qualification:** Article 3's Explanation I includes Union territories within “State” for clauses (a)–(e), but excludes them from “State” in the proviso. Do not apply the State-legislature consultation requirement to a Union territory solely because “State” was used more broadly earlier in that article.

**Worked hypothetical:** Parliament proposes separating a district from State A to form State B. Locate Article 3, not the Eighth Schedule. Presidential recommendation and reference for State A's views are required. State A's objection does not itself defeat Parliament's power. Necessary changes to the First and Fourth Schedules can accompany the law under Article 4.

Article 4 makes this a useful counterexample to an overgeneralisation: **a law can change constitutional schedule text without being an Article 368 amendment**. The Constitution itself supplies the exception. Ordinary parliamentary voting rules apply to this route; no Article 368 special majority is needed merely because those schedules change. Part 3 explains the different majority requirements.

<a id="internal-reorganisation-and-international-territorial-change"></a>

### 3.3 Internal reorganisation and international territorial change

Do not treat every change on a map as an Article 3 exercise. Internal State boundaries and an agreement concerning territory with another country pose different constitutional questions. The official text's Appendix I records the **Constitution (One Hundredth Amendment) Act, 2015**, concerning acquired and transferred territories under the India–Bangladesh arrangements. This is an actual constitutional-amendment example, not a rule that every technical demarcation requires amendment. [Constitution, Appendix I][constitution]

<a id="summary-of-key-concepts-and-takeaways"></a>

## 5. Summary of key concepts and takeaways

- Read a constitutional claim through its article, clause, proviso, Explanation, schedule and legal-status footnotes. A label or article number alone cannot establish the complete operative rule.
- Schedules serve different constitutional functions; they are part of the Constitution, not interchangeable lists or an informal appendix.
- Articles 2–4 provide particular territorial powers and procedures. State consultation under Article 3 is mandatory in its stated setting, but State consent is not a veto; Article 4 permits specified schedule changes outside Article 368.

## 6. UPSC-specific notes

<a id="static-notes"></a>

### 6.1 Static notes

- **Document map:** Part I—Union/territory; II—citizenship; III—rights; IV—Directive Principles; IVA—duties; V/VI—Union/State institutions; IX/IXA—Panchayats/municipalities; XI—Union–State relations; XV—elections; XVII—official language; XVIII—emergencies; XX—amendment.
- **Schedule map:** First—States/UTs/territory; Second—specified office emoluments; Third—specified oaths; Fourth—Rajya Sabha seats; Fifth—Scheduled Areas/Tribes; Sixth—tribal-area arrangements in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram; Seventh—legislative lists; Eighth—languages; Ninth—specified laws/Article 31B; Tenth—defection; Eleventh—Panchayat matters; Twelfth—municipal matters.
- **Exceptions to shortcuts:** the President's oath is in Article 60, not the Third Schedule; Sixth Schedule arrangements do not cover every northeastern State; Ninth Schedule listing is not complete judicial immunity; Eleventh/Twelfth listings do not automatically transfer every power without the relevant legal framework.
- **Territorial powers:** Article 1 describes India as a Union of States; Article 2 concerns admission/establishment of new States; Article 3 concerns formation and internal area/boundary/name changes; Article 4 provides necessary First/Fourth Schedule and consequential changes outside Article 368.
- **Article 3 procedure:** bill in either House, President's recommendation, affected State legislature's views within the permitted period. Parliament need not accept those views. “State” includes UTs for clauses (a)–(e), but not for the proviso's State-legislature reference.
- **Counting trap:** original 395 articles/22 Parts/eight schedules are historical facts. Lettered insertions, omissions and changed schedules mean the last article number is not a count of current provisions. Read the edition, not a memorised total.

<a id="current-connections"></a>

### 6.2 Current connections

The document used here is the **10 September 2026 edition**. It contains twelve schedules; that is an edition-specific observation, not a reason to assume every printed provision is operative.

The **One Hundredth Amendment Act, 2015**, concerning the India–Bangladesh territorial arrangements, is the chapter's dated example of international territorial change. Compare it with internal reorganisation under Articles 3–4: a changed map does not by itself identify the legal route. Neither example establishes that every technical demarcation requires amendment. [Official text, Appendix I][constitution]

When a new State/UT or boundary proposal appears in the news, identify the legal instrument, recommendation/reference requirements, commencement and affected schedules before updating a map or administrative count.

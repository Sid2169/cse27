## 1. Membership of a political community

**Citizenship** is legal membership of a State. **Residence** is living in a place. **Domicile** is a legal concept connecting a person to a permanent home; its application depends on the relevant legal context. A person can live in India without being an Indian citizen, and an Indian citizen can live abroad. An address, ancestry and citizenship therefore answer different questions.

India has a single Indian citizenship, rather than separate citizenships conferred by each constituent State. Moving from one State to another does not require acquiring a new State citizenship. State residence or domicile requirements used for a particular benefit are a separate question; the label on a certificate does not create another sovereign citizenship. [Constitution, Part II; Citizenship Act][constitution] [Act][citizenship]

Citizenship also does not mean that every legal protection is available only to citizens. Article 14 speaks of **any person**; Article 19 confers its listed freedoms on **citizens**. Conversely, citizenship does not eliminate age, electoral-registration or other conditions for a political right. Article 58 requires the President to be an Indian citizen but does not restrict that office to citizens by birth. Detailed rights and election eligibility belong to Chapters 024, 026 and 039. [Articles 14, 19, 58 and 326][constitution]

### The Constitution's starting point and Parliament's continuing law

Part II contains Articles 5–11. Its historical citizenship provisions must not be misread as a complete present-day application code.

| Provision | Main function | Qualification to remember |
|---|---|---|
| **Article 5** | Citizenship at constitutional commencement | Requires domicile in India plus one of its specified birth, parentage or prior-residence connections |
| **Article 6** | Certain migrants to India from the territory then included in Pakistan | Conditions differ around **19 July 1948**; the later-migration category requires registration and the specified residence period |
| **Article 7** | Certain persons migrating to Pakistan after **1 March 1947** | Includes a proviso for return under a permit for resettlement or permanent return |
| **Article 8** | Certain persons of Indian origin residing outside India | Requires the specified connection and consular/diplomatic registration; not ancestry alone |
| **Article 9** | Effect of voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship on the specified constitutional claims | Read its reference to Articles 5, 6 and 8 |
| **Article 10** | Continuance of citizenship recognised under the preceding provisions | Subject to parliamentary law |
| **Article 11** | Parliament's power over acquisition, termination and other citizenship matters | Enables continuing legislation such as the Citizenship Act, 1955 |

Under Article 5, the alternatives are birth in India, a parent born in India, **or** ordinary residence for at least five years immediately before commencement. Domicile is a separate shared requirement. Thus, “five years' residence alone automatically establishes Article 5 citizenship” drops part of the rule. For Article 6's category involving migration on or after 19 July 1948, registration required residence for at least six months immediately before application. [Part II][constitution]

The present statutory framework is principally the **Citizenship Act, 1955**, as amended, together with rules and relevant notifications. Parliament can change that Act through ordinary legislation; changing the statutory conditions does not necessarily amend Article 11 itself. This is the same distinction between a grant of power and a law made under that power that appeared in Part 1.

## 2. How citizenship is acquired

The Act distinguishes five principal routes. A route is a legal category, not a promise that every applicant in that category succeeds.

| Route | Basic idea | Provision |
|---|---|---|
| **Birth** | Birth in India, subject to the applicable date and conditions | Section 3 |
| **Descent** | Birth outside India with the required parental connection and applicable formalities | Section 4 |
| **Registration** | Registration of persons in specified eligible categories, with statutory conditions | Section 5 |
| **Naturalisation** | Grant after meeting the statutory qualifications and procedure, subject to permitted relaxations | Section 6 and Third Schedule |
| **Incorporation of territory** | Citizenship arrangements when territory becomes part of India | Section 7 |

Source: [Citizenship Act, acquisition provisions][citizenship]. There are also special provisions, including Section 6A for persons covered by the Assam Accord and Section 6B for the category specified by the 2019 amendment. Do not assume the general route table exhausts every special case.

### Birth: the date changes the rule

For persons born **in India**, Section 3 divides the ordinary rule into three periods:

| Date of birth | Parental condition under the ordinary rule |
|---|---|
| **26 January 1950 to 30 June 1987** | No parental-citizenship condition under Section 3(1)(a) |
| **1 July 1987 to 2 December 2004** | At least one parent was an Indian citizen at the time of birth |
| **On or after 3 December 2004** | Both parents were Indian citizens, **or** one parent was an Indian citizen and the other was not an illegal migrant at the time of birth |

These are the Section 3(1) rules, **subject to Section 3(2)**: the specified diplomatic-immunity case and the specified enemy-alien birth in enemy-occupied territory are exceptions. The statutory definition of **illegal migrant** broadly addresses unauthorised entry and overstay, with statutory exemptions that must be read in the applicable text. It does not mean every foreign national. [Sections 2 and 3, including commencement footnotes][citizenship]

**Worked examples:** Assume birth in India, neither Section 3(2) exception, and no later event affecting citizenship.

- A person born in 1980 does not need to satisfy the parental condition introduced for births from July 1987.
- For a child born in 1995, one Indian-citizen parent at birth meets the parental requirement in the applicable period.
- For a child born in 2010 to one Indian citizen and one foreign national lawfully present, the foreign parent's nationality alone does not defeat the route. The relevant question is whether that parent was an illegal migrant at birth.

Always identify the period **before** applying the condition. Do not apply the newest birth rule backwards to every person alive today.

### Descent, registration and naturalisation

For birth **outside India**, consult Section 4 rather than the domestic-birth table. Its requirements depend on date, parental citizenship and, in applicable cases, consular registration. For births on or after 3 December 2004, consular registration within the statutory period, or registration later with Central Government permission, is important; the prescribed parental declaration concerning another country's passport must also be satisfied. A parental connection does not remove the statutory formalities. [Section 4][citizenship]

Registration and naturalisation require applications and satisfaction of the relevant conditions. Marriage to an Indian citizen is not itself an automatic grant of citizenship: spouses are a specified registration category with conditions. Registration as an OCI Cardholder is also not the same act as registration **as an Indian citizen**. Similar words in two procedures do not make their legal consequences identical. [Sections 5–7B][citizenship]

### A dated example of statutory change: CAA 2019

The **Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019** created a conditional framework concerning specified Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist, Jain, Parsi and Christian persons from Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Pakistan who entered India on or before **31 December 2014**, with the required statutory exemption. Section 6B concerns the route to registration/naturalisation for that category and provides territorial exclusions for specified Sixth Schedule tribal areas and Inner Line areas. The **Citizenship (Amendment) Rules, 2024** were notified on **11 March 2024** to provide the application mechanism. [Sections 2 and 6B][citizenship]; [MHA/PIB, 2024 implementation account][caa]

The lesson is the difference between **eligibility, application procedure and grant**. Neither belonging to one listed community nor holding an identity document by itself proves that all conditions have been met. This is a dated explanation of the statutory change and its 2024 implementation, not a current checklist of application forms. The MHA's register also lists later 2026 rule changes; consult the operative rules and orders for an actual application. [MHA register][mha]

## 3. Loss of citizenship and OCI

The Act distinguishes different mechanisms of loss:

| Mechanism | Meaning | Qualification |
|---|---|---|
| **Renunciation — Section 8** | A qualifying citizen voluntarily makes the prescribed declaration giving up citizenship | Registration of the declaration and statutory consequences matter |
| **Termination — Section 9** | Consequence of voluntary acquisition of another country's citizenship under the statutory rule | Do not confuse voluntary acquisition with every involuntary or disputed nationality situation |
| **Deprivation — Section 10** | Central Government order affecting specified categories of citizenship on statutory grounds | It is not an unrestricted power to remove any person's citizenship for any reason |

Fraud in obtaining the relevant citizenship is one specified deprivation ground. The section also prescribes procedural safeguards. Therefore, the absolute claim “citizenship once granted can never be taken away” is wrong, but so is “the executive can remove everyone's citizenship at will”. [Sections 8–10][citizenship]

**Overseas Citizen of India (OCI) Cardholder** is the name of a statutory status for eligible foreign nationals. Despite the name, an OCI Cardholder is **not an Indian citizen** and does not hold Indian dual citizenship. OCI provides specified facilities, including immigration-related benefits, subject to law and notifications; it does not confer the citizen's right to vote or eligibility for Parliament. [Sections 7A–7B][citizenship]; [official OCI overview][oci]

Likewise, **Aadhaar is not proof of citizenship or domicile**. Section 9 of the Aadhaar Act expressly separates the identity number and its authentication from those statuses. This is a direct example of why document purpose matters: an identification mechanism does not answer every legal-membership question. [UIDAI, Aadhaar Act, Section 9][aadhaar]

## 4. Official language is not the same as national or scheduled language

Different language provisions govern different functions. Ask: language for **which institution, which activity and under which provision**?

### Union and State official business

Article 343(1) identifies **Hindi in Devanagari script** as the official language of the Union and specifies the international form of Indian numerals. It does not declare Hindi India's “national language”. The Constitution does not designate a national language. [Article 343 and Part XVII][constitution]

Article 343 initially provided for continued English use for fifteen years and expressly allowed Parliament to legislate for its use afterwards. Section 3 of the **Official Languages Act, 1963** provides for continued English use in addition to Hindi for the specified Union purposes and parliamentary business. Thus, “English automatically ceased to have official use in 1965” is wrong. Nor does Section 3 mean every government communication is freely interchangeable: it contains specific communication and bilingual-document requirements. [Official Languages Act, Section 3][languages]

Under Article 345, a State legislature can adopt one or more languages in use in the State, or Hindi, for all or specified State official purposes, subject to Articles 346 and 347. The provision does not require that every adopted State language first appear in the Eighth Schedule. Article 346 and the Act regulate relevant intergovernmental communication. [Articles 345–347][constitution]

### Scheduled languages: a constitutional list with a different function

The **Eighth Schedule** presently lists **22 languages** in the constitutional edition used here:

Assamese, Bengali, Bodo, Dogri, Gujarati, Hindi, Kannada, Kashmiri, Konkani, Malayalam, Manipuri, Marathi, Nepali, Odia, Punjabi, Sanskrit, Santhali, Sindhi, Tamil, Telugu, Urdu and Maithili. The sequence here groups names for reading rather than reproducing the schedule's numbered order. [Eighth Schedule][constitution]

English is not listed. Listing does not make all 22 languages official languages of the Union. Scheduled status, Union official-language status, State official-language status and **classical-language recognition** are distinct categories; a claim about one cannot be established merely by evidence about another. The Eighth Schedule is referred to in Articles 344 and 351, including the official-language commission and development of Hindi. [Articles 344 and 351][constitution]

### Courts, grievances and linguistic minorities

Article 348 provides the English-language baseline for Supreme Court and High Court proceedings and specified authoritative legal texts until Parliament otherwise provides. Article 348(2) allows the Governor, with the President's previous consent, to authorise Hindi or another relevant State official language for High Court proceedings; that clause's proviso excludes judgments, decrees and orders. **Section 7 of the Official Languages Act separately addresses their language**, requiring an English translation under the High Court's authority when the authorised alternative is used. Do not turn the general baseline into the assertion that no qualified exception exists. [Article 348][constitution]; [Act, Section 7][languages]

| Safeguard | Provision | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Grievance representations | Article 350 | Every person may submit a representation in a language used in the Union or the State, as applicable |
| Primary-stage mother-tongue facilities | Article 350A | States and local authorities should endeavour to provide adequate facilities for children belonging to linguistic minorities |
| Special Officer for linguistic minorities | Article 350B | President-appointed officer investigates constitutional safeguards and reports through the specified process |

These provisions show why language policy is not solely a choice of administrative convenience. It also concerns access to public institutions and minority safeguards. Detailed cultural and educational rights are taught in Chapter 024.

## 5. Follow the exact PYQ wording

**[2024 Q9](Prelims-General-Studies-I-PYQS/2024-Prelims-General-Studies-I.md)** asks which languages were added by the **71st Amendment, 1992**. Konkani, Manipuri and Nepali were the three, making option **(a)** correct. Maithili is also in the Eighth Schedule today, but it was added by the **92nd Amendment, 2003**, together with Bodo, Dogri and Santhali. Today's membership cannot establish which historical amendment inserted a name. The [Eighth Schedule's official footnotes][constitution] resolve the distinction.

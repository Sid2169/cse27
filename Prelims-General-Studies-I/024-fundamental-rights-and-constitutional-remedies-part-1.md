## 1. What makes a right fundamental?

Suppose an authority excludes an eligible person from a public service because of caste. The affected person needs more than an appeal to the authority's goodwill. A **right** identifies a legally protected claim; a **remedy** is a means of obtaining enforcement or redress. Fundamental Rights are constitutional guarantees in **Part III**, with constitutional mechanisms for their enforcement. **Justiciable** means capable of being enforced through judicial proceedings. Article 32 guarantees the right to move the Supreme Court for enforcement of Part III rights. [Constitution, Part III and Article 32][constitution]

“Fundamental” does not mean every right is unlimited, that every right belongs only to citizens, or that every dispute can go directly to the Supreme Court. The article's wording determines its beneficiaries and content; the applicable restriction or exception determines its limits; the remedy provision determines the court's role.

Distinguish three sources:

| Type | Where its authority comes from | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Fundamental Right | Part III of the Constitution | Article 19 freedoms |
| Other constitutional right | A constitutional provision outside Part III | Property protection under Article 300A |
| Statutory right | An ordinary Act | A claim created by a particular welfare or employment statute |

The distinction concerns legal source and enforcement route, not whether the interest matters in ordinary life. A statutory entitlement can be legally enforceable. Article 226 enables High Courts to enforce legal rights beyond Part III; Article 32 is specifically for Fundamental Rights. Part 3 explains this difference. [Articles 32, 226 and 300A][constitution]

### A map of Part III

| Article range | Subject |
|---|---|
| 12–13 | Meaning of the State and the constitutional control of inconsistent laws |
| 14–18 | Equality |
| 19–22, including 21A | Freedoms, criminal safeguards, life/liberty, education and detention |
| 23–24 | Protection against exploitation |
| 25–28 | Freedom of religion |
| 29–30 | Cultural and educational rights |
| 31A–31C | Qualified protections for specified laws; read judicial-status notes |
| 32–35 | Enforcement and specified special legislative powers |

Article 31 is omitted. Its number does not prove that the former property right remains a Fundamental Right. The six familiar groups are a useful learning arrangement, not six identical rights with identical beneficiaries.

## 2. Ask who is acting and who is protected

### Article 12: “the State” is wider than the ministry

For Part III, Article 12 includes the Union government and Parliament, State governments and legislatures, and local or other authorities within India's territory or under the Government of India's control. A municipality can therefore be relevant even though it is not the Union ministry. “Other authorities” requires legal interpretation: a familiar organisational label alone does not decide a body's constitutional status. [Article 12][constitution]

Many rights regulate State action. But **do not memorise “Fundamental Rights operate only against government”** as a universal rule. Article 15(2) addresses specified discrimination in access to shops and public facilities; Articles 17, 23 and 24 contain prohibitions that can apply to private conduct. A private employer does not gain permission to use forced labour merely because it is private. [Articles 15(2), 17, 23–24][constitution]

The scope of a particular right and the availability of a particular writ are separate questions. Under Article 226, a body outside Article 12 may still be answerable for an applicable public duty. That does not turn every private dispute into constitutional litigation; the relevant duty and public-law element matter. Part 3 develops this distinction.

### “Person”, “citizen” and “minority” are not interchangeable

| Provision | Beneficiary wording or focus | Consequence for reading a question |
|---|---|---|
| Article 14 | Any person within India's territory | Not restricted to Indian citizens |
| Articles 15, 16 and 19 | Citizens, within the respective guarantees | Residence alone does not establish citizenship |
| Articles 20 and 21 | No person may be deprived of their specified protections | Non-citizens are not excluded merely because of nationality |
| Article 21A | All children aged six to fourteen | Do not substitute all ages or higher education |
| Article 25 | All persons, subject to its qualifications | Religious freedom is not exclusively a citizen's entitlement |
| Article 29(1) | Any section of citizens with a distinct language, script or culture | Not confined by its text to numerically small communities |
| Article 30 | Minorities based on religion or language | A specifically protected institutional interest |

This table teaches distinctions, not a mechanical list resolving every question about corporate claimants, enemy aliens or institutional rights. Always return to the actual article and applicable exception. [Part III][constitution]

## 3. Article 13: law is also subject to rights

Article 13(1) addresses pre-Constitution laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights, to the extent of inconsistency. Article 13(2) prohibits the State from making laws taking away or abridging the rights, again making contravening law void to the extent of the conflict. **Abridge** means reduce or curtail. [Article 13][constitution]

“Law” here is broad: it includes ordinances, orders, rules, regulations, by-laws and customs or usages having the force of law. An authority cannot escape the issue simply by calling its instrument a “rule” instead of an “Act”. Equally, an unpopular decision is not automatically unconstitutional; identify a particular rule and a particular conflict.

**Worked illustration:** A municipal by-law bars citizens of a specified caste from using a public bathing facility. The instrument's local status does not exempt it from Part III. Locate the equality guarantee, the relevant actor and Article 13's treatment of inconsistent laws. The legal inquiry is more precise than “the policy feels unfair”.

Constitutional amendments follow the separate framework explained in Chapter 023. Article 13(4) does not make Parliament's amending power unlimited: the basic-structure limitation remains. Do not transfer the ordinary-law analysis to an amendment without considering that distinction.

## 4. Article 14: equality does not require identical treatment

Article 14 combines **equality before the law** with **equal protection of the laws**. Public power cannot treat people as above the law or distribute legal protection on arbitrary grounds. But equality is not a command to ignore every relevant difference.

A law may classify people or situations if the distinction is intelligible and rationally connected to its objective. **Intelligible differentia** means an understandable basis distinguishing the included group from those left out; **rational nexus** means a reasonable connection between that basis and the law's aim. The Supreme Court restates this two-part classification test in **Dev Gupta v. PEC University of Technology (2023), paragraphs 14–16**. It is one important method of equality analysis, not the entirety of Article 14 jurisprudence. [Official judgment][equality]

**Worked comparison:** Assume a scholarship aims to overcome financial barriers to schooling. Family income can be relevant to that objective. The initial letter of a student's name, by itself, has no apparent connection to the objective. Merely dividing applicants into two clearly identifiable groups is insufficient: the basis must relate rationally to the purpose.

Likewise, a rule treating unlike situations identically can create disadvantage. A provision that responds to a relevant disadvantage is not disproved merely by pointing out that recipients receive different treatment. Ask what difference matters, what the measure does and which constitutional provision authorises or constrains it.

**Rule of law**, introduced in Chapter 022, connects limits on power, equality before law and protection of liberties. It does not mean that people exist only to obey unconstrained rulers. Nor does the number of enacted laws measure liberty: lawful safeguards against detention or discrimination can increase practical freedom.

## 5. Articles 15 and 16: distinguish discrimination from safeguards

### Article 15: discrimination and access

Article 15(1) prohibits State discrimination against citizens on grounds **only** of religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth, or any of them. Article 15(2) covers specified discriminatory disabilities in access to shops, public restaurants, hotels and places of public entertainment, and the listed public-use facilities. Read the grounds and setting precisely. For example, **place of birth** and **residence** are not the same concept. [Article 15][constitution]

Article 15 also permits specified special provisions:

| Clause | Enabling subject |
|---|---|
| 15(3) | Women and children |
| 15(4) | Advancement of socially and educationally backward classes, Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes |
| 15(5) | Specified educational-admission measures, including in private aided/unaided institutions, excluding Article 30(1) minority institutions |
| 15(6) | Economically weaker sections other than the classes specified in 15(4)/(5); qualifying admission reservations up to ten per cent in addition to existing reservations, with the stated minority-institution exclusion |

**Scheduled Castes (SCs)** and **Scheduled Tribes (STs)** refer to constitutionally recognised categories under Articles 341 and 342; the names do not mean everyone with a low income. The backward-class and economically weaker-section categories also have their own legal criteria. This chapter identifies the enabling constitutional provisions rather than current certificate rules or every reservation judgment. [Articles 15, 341 and 342][constitution]

### Article 16: public employment

Article 16 addresses equality of opportunity in **employment or appointment under the State**, not every job in the economy. Clause (2)'s prohibited grounds include **descent and residence**, in addition to the listed religion/race/caste/sex/place-of-birth grounds. Under clause (3), **Parliament** may prescribe the specified residence requirements by law. It is not a general permission for any official to invent a local-residence rule. [Article 16][constitution]

Clause (4) enables reservations for a backward class inadequately represented in State services in the State's opinion. Clause (4A) specifically concerns qualifying promotion reservations for SCs/STs, including consequential seniority. Clause (4B) addresses the treatment of carried-forward reserved vacancies. Clause (5) preserves specified religious-office laws; clause (6) provides for EWS reservations subject to its category and ten-per-cent limits. Do not assume that a promotion clause naming SCs/STs automatically covers every reservation category.

These are **enabling provisions with conditions**, not a declaration that every group gets the same quota in every institution. An example of a policy falling within an allowed category still needs to satisfy the relevant constitutional and legal requirements.

## 6. Articles 17 and 18: civic status

**Article 17 abolishes untouchability**, forbids its practice in any form and makes enforcement of a disability arising from it punishable according to law. It addresses the caste-based social practice; it is not a label for every refusal to touch something, such as a general infection-control precaution. A caste-based exclusion from a public facility can engage this prohibition as well as equality provisions. [Article 17][constitution]

**Article 18 concerns titles.** The State cannot confer titles other than military or academic distinctions. Citizens cannot accept a title from a foreign State; the article gives additional consent rules concerning non-citizens in offices of profit or trust and foreign presents, emoluments or offices. Therefore “all distinctions, including academic degrees, are constitutionally banned” is false. Read the express exceptions rather than enlarging the prohibition. [Article 18][constitution]

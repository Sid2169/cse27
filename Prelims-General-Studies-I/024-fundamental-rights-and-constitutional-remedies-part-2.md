## 1. Article 19: six freedoms, with different restriction clauses

Article 19(1) protects six freedoms of **citizens**. Each must be read with the applicable restriction clause. A **reasonable restriction** is a constitutionally permissible limit, not simply a limit an official personally regards as sensible. There must be the relevant legal authority, a permitted basis and a restriction satisfying constitutional scrutiny. [Article 19][constitution]

| Freedom | Article | Applicable restriction framework |
|---|---|---|
| Speech and expression | 19(1)(a) | 19(2): specified grounds, listed below |
| Peaceful assembly without arms | 19(1)(b) | 19(3): sovereignty/integrity of India or public order |
| Associations, unions or cooperative societies | 19(1)(c) | 19(4): sovereignty/integrity, public order or morality |
| Movement throughout India's territory | 19(1)(d) | 19(5): interests of the general public or protection of a Scheduled Tribe's interests |
| Residence and settlement in any part of India | 19(1)(e) | 19(5): the same stated grounds |
| Profession, occupation, trade or business | 19(1)(g) | 19(6): general-public-interest restrictions, and specified provisions concerning qualifications and State enterprise |

The lettering jumps from (e) to (g) because the former property provision, Article 19(1)(f), was omitted. Do not silently invent a seventh present freedom. [Official text and Forty-fourth Amendment footnote][constitution]

### The speech grounds are a defined list

Article 19(2) names:

- Sovereignty and integrity of India.
- Security of the State.
- Friendly relations with foreign States.
- Public order.
- Decency or morality.
- Contempt of court.
- Defamation.
- Incitement to an offence.

These are not interchangeable descriptions. For example, criticism of government is not automatically a threat to the security of the State. Equally, freedom of expression does not make defamation or incitement constitutionally irrelevant. One must examine the particular law, its ground and its application. “The general public would benefit” is not an additional free-standing speech ground simply because a similar phrase appears in Article 19(5) or (6). [Article 19(2)–(6)][constitution]

**Worked comparison:** A licensing rule requiring doctors to hold appropriate professional qualifications directly relates to Article 19(6)'s qualification provision. That does not establish that the same reasoning authorises censorship of a newspaper. Start with the affected freedom; then locate its restriction clause.

A peaceful assembly without arms is expressly protected. That does not establish an unconditional entitlement to occupy any road at any time. A valid restriction requires its own constitutional basis; the existence of a right and the validity of a specific restriction are two questions, not mutually exclusive assertions.

### Law can protect liberty as well as restrict it

Suppose no enforceable rule prevents an employer from confining workers. Mere absence of regulation does not give those workers practical freedom. Laws prohibiting confinement can protect liberty. Conversely, laws allowing arbitrary confinement can undermine it. The relevant inquiry is the law's content and constitutional limits, not its mere existence or frequency of amendment.

This is the conceptual point behind **[2018 Q60](Prelims-General-Studies-I-PYQS/2018-Prelims-General-Studies-I.md)**. Among its alternatives, option **(b)** captures law's role in securing liberty. More laws do not invariably mean less liberty; democratic participation alone does not capture the entire law–liberty relationship; frequent change alone does not establish an assault on freedom. The aphoristic answer must not be misread as “every enacted law necessarily protects freedom”.

## 2. Article 20: three distinct criminal-law safeguards

Article 20 protects **persons**, not citizens alone. It addresses conviction, punishment and compelled self-incrimination. [Article 20][constitution]

| Protection | Constitutional rule | Simple application |
|---|---|---|
| No retrospective criminalisation or heavier retrospective penalty | A person cannot be convicted for an act that did not violate the law in force when committed, or receive a penalty greater than the applicable penalty at that time | A new law cannot make last year's lawful act retrospectively criminal |
| Protection against double jeopardy | A person cannot be prosecuted **and punished** for the same offence more than once | Identify the same offence and the relevant prior prosecution/punishment, rather than treating every second proceeding as automatically barred |
| Protection against compelled self-incrimination | An accused person cannot be compelled to be a witness against themselves | The protection concerns compelled self-incriminating testimony, not blanket immunity from investigation |

Do not extend the first rule into a claim that **all retrospective legislation**, including every tax or civil enactment, is forbidden by Article 20(1). Nor should a learner assume that two proceedings concern the same offence simply because they arise from related events. Those questions require the relevant law and facts.

The text's “prosecuted and punished” formulation and the status of an accused person matter. A generic reference to a penalty, inquiry or demand for information does not by itself prove that every element of Article 20 is engaged.

## 3. Article 21: life, liberty and fair procedure

Article 21 protects every **person** against deprivation of life or personal liberty except according to **procedure established by law**. This wording does not authorise any procedure merely because the legislature has enacted it. Constitutional adjudication reads the guarantee with fairness and the protections in Articles 14 and 19 where relevant. [Article 21][constitution]; [Puttaswamy, discussion of Maneka Gandhi][privacy]

**Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978)** is central to the requirement that a liberty-depriving procedure be fair, just and reasonable rather than arbitrary. The Supreme Court's **Puttaswamy judgment of 2017** explains that development and the interconnected reading of Fundamental Rights. A beginner should distinguish the Constitution's actual wording from the judicial interpretation of what a valid law and procedure require. [Official judgment, discussion of procedure and Article 21][privacy]

**Worked illustration:** A statute says an official may detain anyone the official dislikes without explaining why or providing meaningful safeguards. Pointing to the statute's existence does not settle the constitutional question. The source of power, fair procedure and the substantive rights involved still require examination.

### Privacy is constitutionally protected

In **Justice K. S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India**, decided **24 August 2017**, the Supreme Court recognised privacy as intrinsic to life and personal liberty under Article 21 and to the freedoms guaranteed in Part III. Privacy was recognised through interpretation; a new article explicitly titled “Right to Privacy” was not inserted. [Judgment's final order][privacy]

Privacy includes interests concerning personal autonomy and personal information, not just secrecy behind a closed door. It is **not absolute**. Justice Chandrachud's opinion explains the requirements of legality, a legitimate State aim and proportionality for invasion of life or liberty; the separate opinions elaborate related protections. **Proportionality** concerns whether the interference is justified in relation to its purpose, rather than accepting any burden merely because the aim is legitimate. [Puttaswamy, concluding synthesis and opinions][privacy]

**Worked comparison:** Assume a government system collects health data for a defined public programme. A legitimate programme purpose does not by itself establish that collecting unlimited unrelated data or publishing individuals' files is justified. Ask for the legal basis, purpose and relationship between collection/use and that purpose. This is an invented constitutional reasoning example, not a ruling on any particular programme or data-protection statute.

The **2017 privacy decision** is distinct from the **2018 Aadhaar merits decision**. Do not identify every conclusion about privacy with a conclusion approving or invalidating every particular use of Aadhaar.

### Existing privacy questions

For **[2018 Q42](Prelims-General-Studies-I-PYQS/2018-Prelims-General-Studies-I.md)**, option **(c)** correctly links Article 21 with the freedoms in Part III. The other alternatives pair it with unrelated combinations: Article 14 and the Forty-second Amendment; Article 17 and Directive Principles; or Article 24 and the Forty-fourth Amendment. For **[2021 Q23](Prelims-General-Studies-I-PYQS/2021-Prelims-General-Studies-I.md)**, Article **21**, option **(c)**, is the answer among Articles 15, 19, 21 and 29. Article 19 may interact with privacy without replacing the Article 21 answer to that stem.

## 4. Article 21A: education with a specified age range

Article 21A requires the State to provide **free and compulsory education to all children aged six to fourteen**, in the manner determined by law. The constitutional provision does not say all ages, all courses or every preferred institution. It was inserted by the **Eighty-sixth Amendment Act, 2002**, and brought into operation on **1 April 2010**, as the official footnote records. Enactment year and commencement date are different facts. [Article 21A and footnote][constitution]

The **Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009** supplies the statutory implementation framework. Do not confuse that Act's year with the constitutional amendment's year. Early-childhood care and education under Article 45, and the related parental duty under Article 51A(k), are distinct provisions covered in Chapter 025. [Articles 21A, 45 and 51A(k)][constitution]; [official RTE Act][rte]

## 5. Article 22: arrest and preventive detention are different

For an ordinary arrest, Article 22(1)–(2) provides safeguards including being informed of the grounds as soon as may be, access to a legal practitioner of one's choice, and production before the nearest magistrate within **twenty-four hours**, excluding necessary journey time. Detention beyond that period requires a magistrate's authority. [Article 22][constitution]

Twenty-four hours is not a general promise of automatic release regardless of judicial authorisation. It is a limit on custody without the required judicial intervention. Conversely, the existence of an allegation does not remove the safeguard.

**Preventive detention** is detention intended to prevent anticipated prejudicial conduct, rather than punishment following conviction for an offence. Article 22(3) excludes enemy aliens and persons detained under preventive-detention law from the particular safeguards in clauses (1) and (2). That exception must not be expanded into “preventive detainees have no constitutional protection”.

Clauses (4)–(7) provide a separate framework. Important features include communication of grounds as soon as may be, the earliest opportunity to make a representation, specified public-interest limits on disclosure, and the advisory-board and parliamentary-law provisions concerning detention periods. [Article 22(3)–(7)][constitution]

**Read the operative text:** In the official edition dated 10 September 2026, clause (4) uses **three months** in its advisory-board framework, with its stated exceptions. A footnote prints a proposed **two-month** replacement under Section 3 of the Forty-fourth Amendment but marks its commencement date **yet to be notified**. Do not treat an uncommenced amendment as operative merely because it appears in a footnote. Equally, three months is not an unconditional maximum for every preventive detention: the remaining clauses and the applicable law matter. Recheck commencement notifications if this point changes. [Article 22 and commencement footnotes][constitution]

## 6. Articles 23 and 24: protection against exploitation

### Article 23: trafficking and forced labour

Article 23 prohibits traffic in human beings, **begar** and similar forms of forced labour. Begar refers to compulsory labour without payment. The constitutional prohibition can apply to private exploitation as well as State action. [Article 23][constitution]

Article 23(2) preserves compulsory service for public purposes, but the State cannot impose it with discrimination on grounds only of religion, race, caste or class. Therefore, the correct rule is not “every compulsory public service is prohibited”. The exception is also not permission to use compulsory service as caste-based exploitation.

**Worked illustration:** An employer keeps workers confined and makes them work against their will. A wage payment does not, by itself, establish genuine freedom to leave or remove the forced-labour issue. Identify coercion and the applicable protection, rather than assuming that the employer's private status settles the matter.

### Article 24: a precise constitutional prohibition

Article 24 prohibits employment of a child **below fourteen** in **any factory or mine**, or engagement in **other hazardous employment**. The factories/mines prohibition is not limited to jobs an employer labels hazardous. [Article 24][constitution]

The article does not itself say “all work by all persons below eighteen”. But failure to fall within Article 24's exact wording does not establish that an activity is lawful: ordinary child-labour, education and other laws may impose additional restrictions. Do not convert a constitutional minimum into an exhaustive permission.

**[2017 Q62](Prelims-General-Studies-I-PYQS/2017-Prelims-General-Studies-I.md)** illustrates classification by constitutional heading. Trafficking/forced labour and the specified child-employment prohibition fall under protection against exploitation. Abolition of untouchability belongs to equality; minority protections belong to cultural/educational rights. Statements **1 and 4**, option **(c)**, are correct. Each other option includes a protection from a different group.

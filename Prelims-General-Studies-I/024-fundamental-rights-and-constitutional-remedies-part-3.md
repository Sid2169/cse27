## 1. Articles 25–28: individual freedom and religious institutions

### Article 25: conscience and religious practice

**Freedom of conscience** concerns a person's beliefs and convictions. To **profess** a religion is to affirm it, to **practise** it is to observe it, and to **propagate** it is to communicate its teachings. Article 25 protects all persons in freely professing, practising and propagating religion, subject to **public order, morality, health and the other provisions of Part III**. The freedom belongs to persons, not only Indian citizens. [Article 25][constitution]

The article also preserves legislative power to regulate economic, financial, political or other secular activities associated with religious practice, and to provide for social welfare and reform and the specified opening of public Hindu religious institutions. Its explanations include the wearing and carrying of kirpans within profession of the Sikh religion, and a particular extension of the reference to Hindus for Article 25(2)(b). That extension is a rule for the specified provision, not a declaration that every religion has become legally identical for all purposes.

**Worked distinction:** A belief and the financial administration of an institution associated with that belief are different subjects. Article 25 does not establish that any activity becomes immune from regulation once someone calls it religious. Conversely, the existence of a regulatory power does not remove the need to respect religious freedom and the other constitutional limits.

### Article 26: denominational affairs

Subject to public order, morality and health, every religious denomination or section of one has rights to establish and maintain religious/charitable institutions, manage its religious affairs, own/acquire property and administer that property according to law. **Denomination** here concerns an identifiable religious group, not simply any association that adopts a name. [Article 26][constitution]

Separate the individual's Article 25 guarantee from the institutional interests in Article 26. Also distinguish managing religious affairs from administering property: the latter expressly operates according to law. A specific dispute requires the relevant constitutional interpretation; the article's title alone does not decide it.

### Article 27: a specified tax prohibition

Article 27 protects a person from compulsion to pay taxes whose proceeds are **specifically appropriated** for promoting or maintaining a particular religion or religious denomination. It does not say that every tax paid by a religious person is prohibited. The purpose and appropriation of the proceeds matter. [Article 27][constitution]

### Article 28: the institution's funding and status matter

| Setting | Constitutional rule |
|---|---|
| Educational institution wholly maintained from State funds | Article 28(1) prohibits religious instruction |
| State-administered institution established under an endowment or trust requiring religious instruction | Article 28(2) provides an exception to clause (1) |
| Institution recognised by the State or receiving State aid | Article 28(3) protects against compulsory participation in religious instruction or worship without the person's consent, or the guardian's consent for a minor |

Source: [Article 28][constitution]. “Wholly State-maintained”, “State-administered”, “State-aided” and “State-recognised” are not interchangeable descriptions.

**Worked illustration:** A school receives some public aid and makes attendance at worship compulsory for every child. Article 28(3)'s consent protection is relevant even though partial aid does not make the school wholly maintained by the State. A rule inferred from the wrong funding category can miss the actual violation.

## 2. Articles 29–30: culture and minority education

Article 29(1) gives **any section of citizens** with a distinct language, script or culture the right to conserve it. Despite the article's heading about minority interests, the operative wording is not confined to a community proving that it is a numerical minority. [Article 29][constitution]

Article 29(2) addresses a different issue: a citizen cannot be denied admission to a State-maintained or State-aided educational institution on grounds **only** of religion, race, caste, language, or any of them. Do not copy Article 15's grounds into this clause without reading it, or treat “conserving culture” as its only function. Read admission safeguards together with the applicable enabling provisions in Article 15 explained in Part 1.

Article 30(1) protects **religious and linguistic minorities** in establishing and administering educational institutions of their choice. Article 30(2) forbids discrimination in granting State aid merely because an institution is under minority management. Article 30(1A) protects the institutional right in the stated compulsory-acquisition setting. [Article 30][constitution]

| Distinction | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Article 29(1) versus Article 30(1) | A broad cultural-conservation guarantee is different from the minority educational-institution guarantee |
| Culture versus admission | Article 29 contains both; do not answer every Article 29 question with language conservation alone |
| Establishing/administering versus State aid | Minority management is not, by itself, a permitted ground for discriminatory denial of aid; it also does not create an unconditional claim to any requested grant |

These protections explain one way constitutional equality accommodates diversity. They are not a licence to disregard the entire Constitution, and their precise regulatory boundaries depend on case law. Detailed minority-institution litigation is beyond this introductory chapter.

## 3. Articles 32 and 226: choose the correct constitutional route

A right's practical value depends partly on being able to challenge its violation. A **writ** is a formal judicial remedy of the relevant kind. Constitutional courts can also issue suitable directions or orders; enforcement is not limited to reciting the Latin name of a writ. [Articles 32 and 226][constitution]

| Feature | Article 32 | Article 226 |
|---|---|---|
| Court | Supreme Court | High Court |
| Protected purpose | Enforcement of Part III Fundamental Rights | Enforcement of Fundamental Rights **and other legal rights** within its scope |
| Constitutional status | The right to move the Supreme Court for Part III enforcement is itself guaranteed in Part III | Constitutional writ power outside Part III |
| Territorial framework | Not restricted to a single State High Court's territory | Read the High Court's territorial jurisdiction and Article 226(2)'s whole/part cause-of-action rule |
| Does every grievance qualify? | No: identify the Fundamental Right and appropriate proceedings | No: identify an enforceable legal/public-law claim and the applicable conditions |

“Cause of action” means the material facts giving rise to the legal claim. Article 226(2) permits jurisdiction where the cause of action arises wholly or partly within the High Court's territory, even if the relevant authority's seat lies elsewhere. Do not reduce this to “only the authority's office address matters”. [Article 226][constitution]

A person need not treat Article 32 as an appeal route available only after losing in the High Court; it is an original constitutional remedy for Fundamental Rights. That does not imply that every filing automatically receives every requested order: maintainability, appropriate proceedings and the court's handling of the case remain relevant.

The phrase **“for any other purpose”** in Article 226 does not mean any personal wish. It extends the relevant power beyond Fundamental Rights to other enforceable legal claims. A wholly private contractual dispute does not become a writ matter merely because someone prefers a High Court hearing. The public duty and the nature of the particular dispute matter. [Ramakrishna Mission v. Kago Kunya, paragraphs 26–35][mandamus]

## 4. Five writs: identify the legal problem, not just the name

| Writ | Main function | Limit or distinction |
|---|---|---|
| **Habeas corpus** | Examine the legality of detention and secure release if detention is unlawful | Can address unlawful confinement by a private person as well as public detention; not a general substitute for every appeal against a valid custody order |
| **Mandamus** | Require performance of an enforceable public or statutory duty | A private organisation may be subject to it for the relevant public duty; a purely private obligation is different |
| **Prohibition** | Restrain a lower court or tribunal from continuing proceedings contrary to the applicable jurisdictional limits | A preventive jurisdictional remedy, not a demand that Parliament pass an alcohol-prohibition law |
| **Certiorari** | Quash a legally defective judicial/quasi-judicial determination, such as for jurisdictional or natural-justice error | Not an automatic fresh appeal on every disagreement about evidence |
| **Quo warranto** | Test the legal authority by which a person holds a substantive public office | Concerns lawful entitlement to a public office, not every private appointment or a court's preference between candidates |

The constitutional lists appear in [Articles 32 and 226][constitution]. NIOS explains the basic functions in [Section 7.5 of its law lesson][nios]; public-duty and public-office qualifications are discussed in the [Ramakrishna Mission][mandamus] and [Premachandran Keezhoth][quo] judgments. The certiorari distinction between supervisory review, jurisdictional error and denial of a fair hearing is explained in [Hari Vishnu Kamath v. Ahmad Ishaque][certiorari]. **Natural justice** includes fair-hearing and impartial-decision requirements in the applicable setting; the exact procedural requirements depend on the case.

### Paired examples

**Detention versus public duty:** If a person is secretly confined without lawful authority, habeas corpus addresses the legality of that confinement. If an authority refuses to perform an applicable mandatory public duty, mandamus may be appropriate. Detention does not become a mandamus problem merely because an official is involved.

**Prohibition versus certiorari:** Assume a tribunal proceeds with a matter the law gives it no authority to decide. Prohibition can prevent continuation. If the tribunal has already made a legally defective determination, certiorari may quash it. This is a useful basic contrast, not a rule that certiorari is unavailable until every stage of an entire dispute has ended.

**Qualification versus merit:** Assume a statute requires a public office-holder to have a specified qualification, but the appointee lacks it. Quo warranto can test the legal entitlement. It is not a means to remove a legally qualified appointee simply because the petitioner prefers another candidate. A bona fide public-minded petitioner need not prove personal entitlement to the post. [Premachandran Keezhoth, paragraphs 31–34][quo]

**Private label versus public duty:** A body's private form is not an absolute answer to Article 226. But neither public benefit, regulation nor receipt of some grant automatically makes every act of a private body a public duty. In **Ramakrishna Mission v. Kago Kunya (2019)**, the Court examined the actual organisation and activity and rejected writ amenability on the facts. Do not convert that fact-specific outcome into “all private hospitals are public authorities” or the opposite universal rule. [Official judgment, paragraphs 29–38][mandamus]

## 5. A reusable rights-and-remedy method

For a short situation, establish these points in order:

1. **Conduct:** What act or omission is challenged? Avoid replacing facts with a broad allegation of unfairness.
2. **Beneficiary and actor:** Does the particular guarantee protect this claimant, and does it apply to the relevant authority or conduct?
3. **Right and limit:** Identify the article and its applicable exception or restriction. A legitimate aim alone does not validate every means.
4. **Remedy:** Identify the court's jurisdiction and the requested legal result: release, performance of duty, stopping proceedings, quashing an order, or testing public-office entitlement.

**Integrated illustration:** Assume a State college denies an otherwise eligible citizen admission solely because of language, without any relevant qualifying constitutional provision. The actor is public; the beneficiary is a citizen; Article 29(2) addresses the stated admission ground and institutional setting, with equality also potentially relevant. Enforcement of the relevant legal duty may involve Article 226; an actual Fundamental Right claim also engages Article 32's purpose. The claim is not a habeas corpus matter merely because it concerns a fundamental freedom. Real cases may require further facts about the institution and admission scheme.

The ability to identify a possible right is not a guarantee of success. These invented examples isolate the teaching issue; they do not predict outcomes in disputes with additional facts.

## 6. Qualifications that prevent overgeneralisation

### Property and the saving provisions

The **Forty-fourth Amendment** removed the former property provisions from the Fundamental Rights framework. **Article 300A** now provides that no person may be deprived of property except by authority of law. It is a constitutional right outside Part III, not a Fundamental Right and not merely a right available to citizens. An allegation concerning property may involve Article 300A and potentially other guarantees on its facts; loss of its former Part III classification did not abolish all legal protection. [Article 300A, Article 19(1)(f)/31 omission notes][constitution]

Articles **31A, 31B and 31C** protect specified laws against specified challenges within their operative scope. Do not read these as a blanket exemption for every policy called social reform. Chapter 023 explains the Ninth Schedule and basic-structure qualification; Chapter 025 will explain the rights–Directive Principles relationship. The official text's judicial-invalidity footnotes are especially important when reading Article 31C.

### Articles 33–35: specified parliamentary powers

| Provision | What it addresses | What not to infer |
|---|---|---|
| **33** | Parliament may by law restrict or abrogate the application of Part III rights to specified armed forces, public-order forces, intelligence/counter-intelligence and connected telecommunications personnel, for duty and discipline | Every person in any government job automatically loses all rights |
| **34** | Parliament's power concerning indemnity and validation of specified acts connected with restoration of order/martial law in an area | Every emergency declaration and martial law are the same event |
| **35** | Exclusive parliamentary legislation for its specified Part III matters and punishment for acts declared offences under Part III | Every law touching a right is exclusively Parliament's field |

Source: [Articles 33–35][constitution]. **Abrogate** means remove the application of the relevant right to the specified extent. These powers have constitutional subjects and purposes; they are not a free-standing permission for an administrator to disregard Part III.

### Emergencies: suspension is not one undifferentiated rule

| Provision | Key distinction |
|---|---|
| **Article 358** | Its Article 19 framework concerns an emergency based on **war or external aggression**, not armed rebellion; qualifying-law recital and executive-action conditions matter |
| **Article 359** | Requires a presidential order identifying the rights whose court enforcement is suspended for the specified period; **Articles 20 and 21 are excluded** |

Article 359(1A) also addresses the effect on qualifying legislation and executive action while the order operates; describing the entire provision as only a procedural postponement would be incomplete. Neither article means every official act becomes valid, and unrelated constitutional constraints do not vanish. Detailed proclamation and emergency procedures belong to [Chapter 036](036-emergencies-and-constitutional-safeguards.md), currently planned. [Articles 358–359][constitution]

**[2021 Q10](Prelims-General-Studies-I-PYQS/2021-Prelims-General-Studies-I.md):** The right to property is a legal right available to **any person**, option **(b)**, grounded in Article 300A. “Citizens only” is too narrow; the Fundamental Right label is no longer correct; saying neither legal nor fundamental protection exists ignores Article 300A.

### Explicit discrepancy: local 2022 Q87

**Encountered and checked on 24 November 2026.** The [local 2022 file](Prelims-General-Studies-I-PYQS/2022-Prelims-General-Studies-I.md) records **(d), all three statements**, for its mandamus/quo-warranto question. For the reproduced stem, the legally supported analysis is:

| Statement | Assessment | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Mandamus does not lie against a private organisation unless entrusted with a public duty | **Correct** in the question's public-law context | The public-duty requirement prevents a purely private obligation being treated as a mandamus claim |
| 2. Mandamus does not lie against a company even though it may be a government company | **Incorrect** | There is no blanket company-form immunity: Article 12/public-authority status and the enforceable public duty must be examined |
| 3. A public-minded person can petition for quo warranto | **Correct**, subject to bona fides and the legal public-office requirements | Personal infringement or competing entitlement to the office is not indispensable |

The supported combination is **1 and 3 only: option (c)**. Option (a) incorrectly accepts statement 2 and rejects 3; option (b) accepts 2 and omits 1; option (d) also incorrectly accepts 2. ## Revision and next steps

A right must be read with its beneficiary, relevant actor, restriction and remedy. Equality is not identical treatment in all circumstances; liberty is not absence of all law; religious freedom is not immunity for every associated secular activity. Constitutional enforcement requires identifying the legal problem and the court's power, not only naming a writ.

# 025. Directive Principles, Fundamental Duties and social justice

**Paper:** Prelims General Studies I, UPSC CSE 2027.

**Curriculum entry:** [Chapter 025](Prelims-General-Studies-I-Curriculum.md#chapter-025).

**Authored and sources checked:** 25 November 2026.

**Scope:** Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSPs), Fundamental Duties, welfare and social justice, and their relationship with enforceable rights, legislation and policy.

**Prerequisites:** [022 — Political foundations](022-political-foundations-and-constitution-making.md), [023 — Constitutional structure and change](023-constitutional-structure-citizenship-and-change.md), and [024 — Fundamental Rights and remedies](024-fundamental-rights-and-constitutional-remedies.md). Recall the State/government distinction, judicial review, Articles 14, 19, 21 and 21A, and constitutional amendment.

**Learning outcome:** Classify a constitutional provision by its addressee and legal effect; explain how a directive can guide an enforceable law without itself becoming a Fundamental Right; distinguish the main directives and all eleven duties; apply the rights–directives relationship without assuming absolute priority for either.

## 1. Three different constitutional questions

Imagine three situations: a public authority arbitrarily excludes a child from a school; a government chooses how much to spend on early-childhood care; a citizen damages a public library. Each concerns constitutional values, but each requires a different legal inquiry.

- **Fundamental Rights** ask what protections a person or citizen can claim, against whom, and through which constitutional remedy.
- **Directive Principles** ask what social order the State should work towards and what objectives should guide its laws and policies.
- **Fundamental Duties** identify constitutional responsibilities of citizens.

A **directive** is a constitutional direction, not merely an announcement by a minister. **Justiciability** means whether a claim can be determined and enforced through a court. **Social justice**, in this context, concerns reducing exclusion, exploitation and unequal access to the conditions of a dignified life. It is broader than one benefit scheme or one reservation category.

A **welfare State** takes responsibility for people's welfare, rather than limiting public action to maintaining order. Article 38 supplies a constitutional welfare objective; the subsequent directives identify particular concerns. This does not prescribe one identical policy instrument for every government. The purpose, the authority to act and the legal limits must be examined separately. [Constitution, Articles 37–39][constitution]

| Framework | Location | Addressee and legal effect |
|---|---|---|
| Fundamental Rights | Part III, Articles 12–35 | Enforceable constitutional protections, with beneficiaries and restrictions depending on the article; remedies include Article 32. |
| DPSPs | Part IV, Articles 36–51 | Directions to the State; Article 37 excludes direct court enforcement of the provisions, while making their application in law-making a State duty. |
| Fundamental Duties | Part IVA, Article 51A | Responsibilities of every citizen, with clause (k) specifically concerning a parent or guardian; Article 51A supplies no automatic penalty or separate Article 32-style enforcement machinery. |

Parts III, IV and IVA form different components of one Constitution. A desirable objective does not automatically create an enforceable individual entitlement, and an enforceable law may pursue more than one constitutional objective. [Parts III–IVA][constitution]

## 2. Why non-justiciable directives still matter

### 2.1 Read both halves of Article 37

Article 36 normally gives “the State” in Part IV the same meaning as in Part III. Thus directives concern public authority broadly, not only the Union Cabinet. Article 37 makes three connected points: the provisions are not enforceable by a court; the principles are fundamental to governance; and the State must apply them when making laws. Retaining only the first point turns a constitutional obligation into an optional preference. Retaining only the last two incorrectly turns every policy objective into an immediately claimable right. [Articles 36–37][constitution]

**Example:** Article 41 concerns work, education and public assistance, within the State's economic capacity and development. It does not, by its own force, entitle every unemployed person to any job or payment they choose. If a valid statute creates a defined entitlement, its beneficiary, conditions and remedy must be read from that statute. The legal claim then has a specific basis beyond simply pointing to Article 41.

### 2.2 A goal, a law and a scheme are different things

Follow this sequence when reading policy news:

1. Identify the constitutional objective, such as nutrition under Article 47.
2. Identify the legal instrument: a constitutional provision, an Act, rules under an Act, or an executive scheme.
3. Read who qualifies, which authority must act and what remedy the instrument provides.
4. Distinguish legal eligibility from the money allocated and from actual delivery.

A policy inspired by a directive does not automatically acquire constitutional validity. The legislature needs power to enact it, procedures must be followed, and applicable constitutional limits remain. Conversely, non-justiciability of a directive does not make a statute implementing it unenforceable. The distinction is between **direct enforcement of Part IV** and **enforcement of an independent legal obligation**.

## 3. Read the directives as a connected policy map

The following tables paraphrase the constitutional provisions; the linked official text controls their precise wording. Textbook groups such as “socialist”, “Gandhian” and “liberal-intellectual” can aid recall, but are not classifications written into Part IV and may overlap. Learn the actual subject and qualification before the label. [Articles 38–51][constitution]

### 3.1 Welfare, resources and access to justice

| Article | Direction | Distinction to retain |
|---|---|---|
| 38 | Promote welfare through a social order informed by social, economic and political justice; minimise income inequalities and address inequalities in status, facilities and opportunities. | Concern extends to groups and regions, not only differences between individuals. |
| 39(a) | Secure adequate means of livelihood for citizens, men and women equally. | A policy objective, not an Article 19-type freedom to practise an occupation. |
| 39(b) | Distribute ownership and control of material resources of the community to serve the common good. | Concerns resources and their distribution; not a statement that all private property is automatically community property. |
| 39(c) | Prevent concentration of wealth and means of production to the common detriment. | Distinguish concentration harmful to the community from an unconditional prohibition on all private wealth. |
| 39(d) | Equal pay for equal work for men and women. | The directive is in Part IV; do not present its text as a separately enumerated Fundamental Right. An actual equality claim also requires the relevant law and constitutional reasoning. |
| 39(e)–(f) | Protect workers' health and strength and children against abuse; protect childhood and youth and support children's healthy development with dignity and opportunity. | Wider welfare objectives than Article 24's particular employment prohibition. |
| 39A | Secure equal justice and free legal aid so economic or other disabilities do not deny access to justice. | Legal aid concerns access to the legal system; it is not the same as separating the judiciary from the executive under Article 50. |

“Distribution” is about how ownership or control serves the common good, not merely giving the same physical share to every person. For Article 39(b), the nature of the resource and its community significance matter. Section 5 examines the judicial qualifications.

### 3.2 Work, local organisation and cooperation

| Article | Direction | Qualification or contrast |
|---|---|---|
| 40 | Organise village panchayats and give them powers needed for self-government. | A directive; the detailed constitutional framework of panchayats is studied later. |
| 41 | Provide for work, education and public assistance in unemployment, old age, sickness, disablement and other undeserved want. | Expressly within the limits of economic capacity and development. |
| 42 | Secure just and humane working conditions and maternity relief. | Not the same subject as worker participation in management. |
| 43 | Secure a living wage, decent life and opportunities for leisure and social/cultural life for workers; promote rural cottage industries on an individual or cooperative basis. | A living-wage objective is not simply a statement of a particular statutory minimum-wage rate. |
| 43A | Secure workers' participation in management of industrial undertakings and organisations. | Participation in management is distinct from equal pay in Article 39(d). |
| 43B | Promote voluntary formation, autonomous functioning, democratic control and professional management of cooperative societies. | Four linked organisational qualities; do not reduce it to government control of cooperatives. |

These directives address different aspects of welfare: access to work, conditions at work, remuneration, participation, and local organisation. A maternity measure is naturally connected to Article 42; a rule about worker representation in management points to Article 43A. Similar language about workers does not make the provisions interchangeable.

### 3.3 Children, weaker sections, health and the environment

| Article | Direction | Qualification or contrast |
|---|---|---|
| 45 | Provide early-childhood care and education until children complete six years. | This is the substituted provision, not the original under-fourteen formulation. |
| 46 | Promote educational and economic interests of weaker sections, particularly Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, and protect them from social injustice and exploitation. | Broader than a single quota; does not independently prescribe a reservation percentage. |
| 47 | Raise nutrition and living standards and improve public health; work towards prohibiting consumption of intoxicating drinks and drugs injurious to health, except for medicinal purposes. | Nutrition/public health and the specified prohibition objective belong to one article; preserve its medicinal qualification. |
| 48 | Organise agriculture and animal husbandry on modern and scientific lines; preserve/improve breeds and prohibit slaughter of cows, calves and other milch and draught cattle. | Distinguish animal-husbandry subject matter from environmental protection under Article 48A. |
| 48A | Protect and improve the environment and safeguard forests and wildlife. | A State directive; the citizen's environmental duty is Article 51A(g). |

**A social-justice example:** Suppose a scholarship is announced for a disadvantaged group. Article 46 helps explain the objective. But the exact eligibility, available funding and enforceable obligations depend on the governing instrument. Whether a classification is constitutionally valid also requires the relevant Part III provisions. “Social justice” does not replace that analysis or establish every beneficiary's eligibility by itself.

### 3.4 Civil law, institutions, heritage and international relations

| Article | Direction | Distinction to retain |
|---|---|---|
| 44 | Endeavour to secure a uniform civil code for citizens throughout India. | A directive about civil law; the provision does not itself enact a code. |
| 49 | Protect monuments, places and objects of artistic or historic interest declared by or under Parliamentary law to be of national importance. | Preserve the national-importance qualification rather than treating every old object as covered identically. |
| 50 | Separate judiciary from executive in the public services of the State. | This belongs to Part IV, not Article 51A's list of citizen duties. |
| 51 | Promote international peace/security, just and honourable relations, respect for international law and treaty obligations, and settlement of international disputes by arbitration. | Article 51 is a directive; Article 51A is the separately located duties provision. |

An Article 44 reference in the news is not proof that one national code has been enacted or that every civil-law difference has ended. A measure must be checked for its jurisdiction, coverage, exceptions and commencement.

## 4. Education shows how legal categories can change

The Eighty-sixth Amendment Act, 2002 connected three provisions, but did not merge them. The official constitutional footnotes record the relevant provisions taking effect on **1 April 2010**. Distinguish the amendment's year from its commencement. [Articles 21A, 45 and 51A(k), including footnotes][constitution]

| Provision | Category | What it addresses |
|---|---|---|
| Article 21A | Fundamental Right | Free and compulsory education for children aged six to fourteen, in the manner determined by State law. |
| Article 45 | DPSP | Early-childhood care and education until age six. |
| Article 51A(k) | Fundamental Duty | A parent or guardian's responsibility to provide educational opportunities to a child or ward aged six to fourteen. |

The original Article 45 concerned free and compulsory education for children until fourteen, with a ten-year constitutional endeavour. It was replaced; it is not the current wording to memorise. Article 41 still includes education within its broader, capacity-qualified policy direction.

**Worked classification:** A claim concerning a ten-year-old's elementary education should not be analysed solely as an unenforceable directive. Article 21A and the applicable education law matter. Section 3 of the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 provides the statutory six-to-fourteen entitlement through completion of elementary education. For a four-year-old's early-childhood care, Article 45 is the relevant directive, but eligibility for a specific service requires its own legal or scheme basis. [RTE Act, Section 3][rte]

The parent's duty does not cancel the State's educational obligation, and Article 21A does not turn every educational activity at every age into an identical Fundamental Right.

## 5. Rights and directives: harmony with defined exceptions

### 5.1 Neither blanket superiority nor a power to ignore rights

Part III protects enforceable rights; Part IV directs the use of public power towards welfare. The constitutional approach is to give effect to both where possible. Calling a law “welfare legislation” does not generally exempt it from rights review. Equally, reducing welfare objectives to legally irrelevant aspirations misses their constitutional role.

In **Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980)**, the Supreme Court rejected the Forty-second Amendment's attempt to give Article 31C protection to laws implementing any Part IV principle. The balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles matters to the basic structure. The **2024 Property Owners Association** judgment reviews that history and confirms the surviving, narrower Article 31C. [Official judgment, Part C][property]

### 5.2 What Article 31C actually protects

Read Article 31C with its judicial-status footnotes and the controlling judgment, not just the printed replacement words. In the legal position explained in **Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra, 5 November 2024**:

- The protection retained from Article 31C concerns laws giving effect to **Article 39(b) or 39(c)**, not all directives.
- It protects qualifying laws against inconsistency challenges under **Articles 14 and 19**; it is not an exemption from the entire Constitution.
- A legislature's declaration is not conclusive: courts may examine whether the law really has the required connection to Article 39(b) or (c).
- For a State Legislature's law, Article 31C's proviso requires reservation for the President's consideration and Presidential assent for this protection to apply.

[Article 31C and footnotes][constitution]; [judgment, especially paragraphs 130–134 and conclusions][property].

**Worked distinction:** A law is described as promoting nutrition under Article 47. That description alone cannot obtain Article 31C immunity. A separate law genuinely distributing Article 39(b) resources may qualify, but a slogan or statutory declaration does not prevent the court from examining the connection. Even qualification does not supply immunity against every constitutional objection.

### 5.3 The dated property judgment prevents two opposite errors

The majority in **Property Owners Association (2024 INSC 835)** rejected the position that all privately owned resources fall within Article 39(b) merely because they meet material needs. It also did not exclude private resources as a class. Some can qualify, depending on their nature, community significance, scarcity and the consequences of concentration. [Judgment, paragraphs 211–222 and conclusions][property]

A privately held resource with major ecological or community significance and an ordinary personal possession cannot simply be treated as constitutionally identical. This is a context-dependent inquiry, not an automatic rule that either everything private or nothing private is covered. The judgment supplies a constitutional interpretation; it does not itself announce that every proposed acquisition or redistribution is valid.

This dated decision is a useful current-affairs anchor. Recheck later controlling judgments before treating its application to a particular statute as settled.

## 6. Fundamental Duties: citizens' responsibilities

### 6.1 Origin and complete working list

Part IVA and the first ten duties were inserted by the **Forty-second Amendment Act, 1976**. The **Eighty-sixth Amendment Act, 2002** added clause (k), giving eleven clauses, (a)–(k). The constitutional footnotes distinguish enactment and commencement dates. These duties are addressed to **citizens**, unlike Fundamental Rights whose beneficiaries vary by provision. [Article 51A and footnotes][constitution]

The table is a paraphrased revision map, not a substitute for reading the article once in full.

| Clause | Citizen's responsibility | Recognition cue |
|---|---|---|
| (a) | Abide by the Constitution; respect its ideals and institutions, the National Flag and National Anthem. | Constitutional allegiance and national symbols. |
| (b) | Cherish and follow the noble ideals of the national freedom struggle. | Ideals, not an instruction to memorise every event. |
| (c) | Uphold and protect India's sovereignty, unity and integrity. | The political community's integrity. |
| (d) | Defend the country and render national service when called upon. | Retain the condition about being called upon. |
| (e) | Promote harmony and common brotherhood across diversities; renounce practices derogatory to women's dignity. | Both intergroup harmony and women's dignity. |
| (f) | Value and preserve the rich heritage of composite culture. | Cultural heritage; distinguish Article 49's State obligation. |
| (g) | Protect and improve the natural environment, including forests, lakes, rivers and wildlife; have compassion for living creatures. | Citizen environment duty plus compassion. |
| (h) | Develop scientific temper, humanism, inquiry and reform. | Critical inquiry and reform, not merely technical knowledge. |
| (i) | Safeguard public property and reject violence. | Public property and nonviolence. |
| (j) | Strive for excellence in individual and collective activity. | Individual and national achievement. |
| (k) | As parent or guardian, provide educational opportunities to a child or ward aged six to fourteen. | Particular responsible person and age band. |

Voting and payment of taxes may involve important civic responsibilities or separate laws, but neither is expressly listed as a clause of Article 51A. Do not add plausible civic conduct to a question asking what is **enumerated** there.

### 6.2 A constitutional duty is not automatically a criminal offence

Article 51A does not itself set a punishment for every breach. Enforcement through a valid statute requires the statute's actual ingredients, exceptions and procedures. Parliament or a State Legislature may enact laws within its competence that give legal effect to conduct associated with constitutional duties; such laws remain subject to constitutional review. The absence of an automatic Article 51A penalty does not make all related conduct legally unregulated.

**Worked distinction:** Damaging a public library is contrary to the duty to protect public property. Criminal liability, however, must rest on an applicable penal law and the proven facts, not merely the assertion “Article 51A(i) creates a sentence”. Similarly, an environmental restriction needs its legal authority; invoking Article 51A(g) does not erase the rights and procedural safeguards relevant to it.

The provision can inform constitutional reasoning, but it is not a general licence to demand that a person first demonstrate perfect civic conduct before claiming a Fundamental Right. Identify the right, its lawful restrictions and the actual alleged conduct.

## 7. Constitutional change: distinguish additions from original provisions

| Amendment Act | Relevant change | Frequent confusion |
|---|---|---|
| Forty-second, 1976 | Added Articles 39A, 43A and 48A; substituted Article 39(f); inserted Part IVA's ten duties. | Equal pay under Article 39(d) was not introduced by this amendment. |
| Forty-fourth, 1978 | Added Article 38(2)'s focus on reducing inequalities. | Do not assign every welfare directive to the Forty-second Amendment. |
| Eighty-sixth, 2002 | Inserted Article 21A, substituted Article 45 and added duty 51A(k). | Three different legal categories, not three versions of one provision. |
| Ninety-seventh, 2011 | Inserted Article 43B on cooperative societies. | Do not confuse this Part IV provision with every issue about the separate Part IXB framework. |

Source: relevant amendment footnotes in the [official Constitution][constitution]. The year in an amendment's title is not necessarily the date every provision commenced. ## 9. Common confusions

| Confusion | Correction |
|---|---|
| Non-justiciable means optional or outside the Constitution. | Article 37 makes directives fundamental to governance and obliges the State to apply them in law-making. |
| Every welfare aspiration is already an individual Fundamental Right. | Identify the specific right, statute or other legal obligation supporting the claim. |
| DPSPs automatically override all Fundamental Rights. | Harmonise the provisions; apply the narrow Article 31C conditions where relevant. |
| Every Article 51A breach has a constitutionally specified punishment. | The duty and a particular statute's offence or obligation are different legal sources. |
| Articles 48A and 51A(g) impose the same obligation on the same person. | One directs the State; the other addresses citizens. |
| Article 45 still contains the current six-to-fourteen education right. | Current Article 45 concerns under-six care/education; Article 21A supplies the six-to-fourteen right. |
| Article 51 and Article 51A are adjacent subjects in the same Part. | Article 51 closes Part IV; Article 51A is in Part IVA. |
| Article 44 itself enacts one uniform code nationwide. | A directive and an enacted, commenced law have different legal effects and territorial coverage. |

## 10. Summary of key concepts and takeaways

Rights protect claims, directives guide State policy, and duties express citizen responsibilities. Their shared constitutional purpose does not erase differences in beneficiaries, addressees and enforcement. Article 37 is the bridge: the State must pursue the directives, but courts do not directly enforce Part IV as though every aspiration were a Fundamental Right.

Implementation adds legal detail. A statute can turn a policy objective into defined entitlements, and a duty-related law can impose specific obligations. Education shows this clearly through the separate roles of Articles 21A, 45 and 51A(k). Social justice combines welfare, equal opportunity, protection against exploitation and lawful institutional action; it is not a blanket exception to rights.

The rights–directives balance includes a carefully delimited Article 31C protection for Article 39(b)/(c) laws. Neither an ideological label nor a statutory declaration settles whether that protection applies. Learn the operative provision, its qualifications and its judicial status together.

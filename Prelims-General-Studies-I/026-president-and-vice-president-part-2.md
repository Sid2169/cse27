## 1. Formal authority and responsible government

Article 53 vests the Union's executive power in the President. Article 74 provides for a Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister to aid and advise the President, who acts in accordance with that advice. Article 75 makes the Council collectively responsible to the **Lok Sabha**, not to both Houses collectively. Thus the President is the constitutional head while ministers provide the politically accountable government. [Constitution, Articles 53 and 74–75][constitution]

This is not the claim that the President has no constitutional work. The office appoints, summons, assents, promulgates and exercises other powers through legally specified procedures. It is the claim that **the constitutional attribution of a power and freedom to choose a policy personally are different questions**.

### 1.1 Advice can be reconsidered, not ignored indefinitely

Article 74 allows the President to require reconsideration of ministerial advice, generally or otherwise. Once advice has been reconsidered, the President must act in accordance with it. This is an opportunity to raise constitutional concerns, not a permanent personal veto over the elected government's policy.

Article 78 allows the President to obtain information from the Prime Minister and require a matter decided by an individual minister to be considered by the Council. Article 74(2) bars courts from inquiring into whether, and if so what, ministerial advice was tendered. Do not expand this specific protection of advice into “no presidential action or underlying legal issue can ever be reviewed”. [Articles 74 and 78][constitution]

### 1.2 A majority question is not an unrestricted personal choice

Under Article 75, the President appoints the Prime Minister and appoints other ministers on the Prime Minister's advice. When no party has an obvious majority, identifying a person capable of securing Lok Sabha confidence requires judgment. The objective remains a government accountable to that House, rather than appointment of whichever person the President privately prefers. The ministry, confidence and transitional-government framework is developed in [Chapter 027](027-prime-minister-ministers-and-administration.md), now authored.

**Worked distinction:** “The President appoints ministers” is a statement about formal authority. “The President may freely select all ministers despite the Prime Minister's advice” is not the rule in Article 75. Always retain the condition attached to the power.

## 2. A map of presidential functions

| Area | Constitutional function | Limitation or institutional connection |
|---|---|---|
| Executive | Union executive power; specified appointments including the Prime Minister and other constitutional offices. | Appointment routes differ; advice, qualifications and removal procedures must be checked for each office. |
| Defence | Supreme command of the defence forces is vested in the President. | Article 53 places its exercise under regulation by law; it is not a personal authority to conduct military policy outside responsible government. |
| Parliament | Summon and prorogue either House; dissolve the Lok Sabha; address Parliament and send messages. | Articles 74 and 85–87; Rajya Sabha is not dissolved. |
| Legislation | Assent, withholding or return within Article 111; promulgate an ordinance under Article 123. | Bill type, session conditions, urgency and constitutional limits matter. |
| Finance | Cause the annual financial statement to be laid before both Houses; recommendation required for demands for grants and specified bills. | Articles 112–117; the government and Parliament retain their distinct preparation, authorisation and scrutiny roles. |
| Clemency | Article 72 powers over specified convictions/sentences. | Exercised on advice, within the constitutional categories and subject to limited review. |
| Advice from Supreme Court | Refer specified questions under Article 143. | An advisory reference is distinct from an ordinary appeal; not required for every governmental decision. |
| Emergencies | Functions under Articles 352, 356 and 360. | Different grounds, approval requirements and consequences; not one undifferentiated personal power. |

Sources: the corresponding [constitutional articles][constitution]. This map is an orientation, not a claim that all listed actions follow the same procedure.

Under Article 79 the President is part of **Parliament**, but is not a member of either House. This explains why a law's passage through the Houses and presidential assent are distinguishable steps, and why the President does not cast a vote in parliamentary divisions.

## 3. Sessions: summon, adjourn, prorogue and dissolve

These words describe different events:

- **Summoning** calls a House to meet. Article 85 requires that six months not intervene between its last sitting in one session and its first sitting in the next.
- **Adjournment** pauses a sitting; **adjournment sine die** ends sittings without fixing a date for the next sitting. These concern the presiding officer's conduct of business.
- **Prorogation** ends a session of a House, through presidential action within the parliamentary system.
- **Dissolution** ends the Lok Sabha's life. It is not another name for a lunch adjournment, and the Rajya Sabha is a continuing House.

Article 85 specifies the interval requirement; it does not prescribe three named annual sessions or a numerical minimum of sitting days per year. Parliamentary conventions about Budget, Monsoon and Winter Sessions are not identical to those constitutional requirements. [Articles 83–85][constitution]

**Existing PYQ connection:** [2020 local Q2][pyq2020] tests the President's formal summoning function, the absence of a constitutional rule fixing three sessions, and the absence of a constitutional annual minimum sitting-day count. Its local key accepts the first and third statements. Formal summoning authority must still be read with Article 74's advice framework.

Prorogation need not be treated as possible only after adjournment sine die: [2024 local Q41][pyq2024] distinguishes these procedures and asks about advice. Detailed exceptions surrounding dissolution when confidence is contested require context; they do not justify saying that routine prorogation is an independent personal presidential choice.

## 4. Assent depends on the kind of bill

### 4.1 Article 111 and Article 368 do different jobs

| Bill | Principal rule after presentation |
|---|---|
| Ordinary bill | Under Article 111, the President may assent or withhold assent, or return the bill for reconsideration as provided there. If the Houses pass it again, with or without amendment, the President shall not withhold assent. |
| Money Bill | Article 111's return-for-reconsideration route excludes a Money Bill. Do not infer a return power from the ordinary-bill rule. |
| Constitution Amendment Bill | Article 368 provides that, once duly passed through its required process and presented, the President shall give assent. Do not apply an ordinary-bill veto to undo that requirement. |

[Articles 111 and 368][constitution]. A preliminary recommendation for introduction and the later grant of assent are different procedural stages; they must not be treated as the same act.

Textbooks call withholding assent an **absolute veto**, returning a bill a **suspensive veto**, and prolonged non-decision without a numerical Article 111 deadline a **pocket veto**. These are explanatory labels, not three separately named constitutional articles. Reconsideration matters: once an ordinary bill is repassed, the constitutional instruction not to withhold assent applies. Article 111 contains no specified number of days for the initial decision; that textual observation should not be inflated into “all delay in every constitutional function is lawful”.

### 4.2 A State bill reserved for the President is another route

Under Articles 200–201, a Governor can reserve a State bill for the President's consideration. The President's Article 201 role must not be confused with Article 111's role in Union legislation. Nor may the President simply pick an unforwarded State bill and unilaterally reserve it. [Articles 200–201][constitution]

The **20 November 2025** Supreme Court advisory opinion, **Special Reference No. 1 of 2025, 2025 INSC 1333**, is a dated update of direct relevance. Its answers state that courts cannot prescribe presidential Article 201 timelines, that there is no “deemed assent” by substituting a court's action for the constitutional authority, and that the President need not make an Article 143 reference whenever a Governor reserves a bill. The opinion distinguishes an advisory reference from adjudication of a bill's contents before enactment. [Official answers, paragraphs 165.6–165.10][assent]

These conclusions concern the identified **Articles 200/201** setting. Do not automatically transplant every detail to Article 111 or claim that an enacted law is immune from constitutional challenge. Equally, an older note prescribing an automatic three-month presidential Article 201 deadline requires correction in light of this opinion, rather than being reproduced as timeless law. Read the full opinion before generalising from a headline about delay or judicial review.

## 5. Ordinances: temporary law-making with constitutional limits

### 5.1 When Article 123 can be used

An **ordinance** is a law promulgated by the President under Article 123 when its conditions are met. It is not a speech, departmental circular or constitutional amendment.

The power is available **except when both Houses are in session**, and requires satisfaction that circumstances make immediate action necessary. Therefore one House being out of session can satisfy the session condition; both need not be out. Conversely, parliamentary opposition or difficulty securing votes does not itself replace the constitutional conditions. Exercise operates within Article 74's advice framework. [Articles 74 and 123][constitution]

An ordinance has the same force and effect as an Act of Parliament, but the source, duration and accountability conditions differ. It may amend an ordinary Central Act within the competent legislative field and can, within constitutional limits, operate retrospectively. It cannot amend the Constitution through an alternative to Article 368, exceed Parliament's legislative competence or violate Fundamental Rights. For example, Article 20(1) limits retrospective creation of criminal liability or enhanced punishment. [Articles 13, 20, 123 and 368][constitution]

Do not confuse a lawful restriction allowed by a right's own terms with an unconstitutional abridgment. Saying “an ordinance cannot violate Part III” is accurate; saying “no ordinance can ever regulate an activity protected by a right” is too broad.

### 5.2 Laying, withdrawal and the six-week clock

Article 123 requires laying the ordinance before **both Houses**. It ceases to operate six weeks after Parliament reassembles, unless it ceases earlier through both Houses' disapproval resolutions; it may also be withdrawn. If the Houses reassemble on different dates, the six weeks run from the **later** date. A replacement Act follows the legislative process rather than magically making the original ordinance permanent. [Article 123(2) and explanation][constitution]

**Constructed timeline:** Lok Sabha reassembles on 1 August and Rajya Sabha on 8 August. Use **8 August**, not 1 August or the ordinance's promulgation date, as the starting point for the six-week reassembly clock. No actual ordinance or current session date is being reported here.

A fixed “six months plus six weeks” slogan obscures what the Constitution actually says. The rule is tied to session intervals and reassembly, and it remains subject to withdrawal and disapproval. Also avoid assuming that an ordinance's cessation mechanically answers every question about transactions already completed under it; those consequences require the relevant law and judicial reasoning.

### 5.3 Re-promulgation is not an alternative legislature

Repeatedly issuing substantially the same ordinance to evade legislative consideration undermines representative law-making. The Supreme Court's **Madras Bar Association judgment of 14 July 2021**, discussing **D.C. Wadhwa** and **Krishna Kumar Singh (2017)**, reiterates that re-promulgation threatens the legislature's role. This is not a legitimate way to continue executive-made law indefinitely without parliamentary scrutiny. [Official judgment, re-promulgation discussion][ordinance-case]

Use the relevant majority reasoning, rather than extracting a contrary sentence from a separate opinion and calling it the Court's holding. The constitutional conditions and laying requirement remain the starting point.

## 6. Clemency: a constitutional executive power, not a second trial

### 6.1 The scope of Article 72

**Clemency** is relief from punishment through constitutional executive authority. Article 72 covers:

1. Punishment or sentence by a **court martial**.
2. Offences against laws relating to matters within the **Union's executive power**.
3. **All death-sentence cases**, which is broader than only deaths imposed for Union-field offences.

Article 72 also preserves specified other powers, including the Governor's power concerning suspension, remission or commutation of death sentences under applicable law. Do not convert the President's all-death-sentence jurisdiction into a statement that no other authority can ever reduce or suspend a death sentence. Article 161's State-field power is a different provision, and it is not an all-courts-martial jurisdiction. [Articles 72 and 161][constitution]

### 6.2 Distinguish the forms of relief

| Term | Working meaning |
|---|---|
| Pardon | Clemency relieving the offender from punishment and its legal consequences to the extent recognised by law; not a declaration that the alleged event never occurred. |
| Commutation | Substitute a less severe form of punishment. |
| Remission | Reduce the period/amount of punishment without changing its essential character. |
| Reprieve | Temporarily postpone execution of a sentence. |
| Respite | Award lesser punishment because of a relevant special circumstance. |
| Suspension | Temporarily hold operation of the sentence in abeyance. |

Article 72 names the available constitutional powers; these short definitions help distinguish their functions. For instance, reducing the duration of a prison sentence is different from replacing a death sentence with imprisonment. Neither should be confused with an appeal reversing a conviction on judicial grounds.

### 6.3 Advice and limited judicial review

The President does not decide a mercy petition as a personal favour outside government. The Supreme Court in **Shatrughan Chauhan v. Union of India (21 January 2014)** explains the aid-and-advice requirement for Articles 72/161 and, discussing **Epuru Sudhakar**, the availability of limited judicial review. Grounds include lack of application of mind, mala fides, wholly irrelevant considerations, failure to consider relevant material, and arbitrariness. [Official judgment][clemency]

**Mala fides** means bad faith or misuse for an improper purpose. Limited review does not mean a routine rehearing of the criminal trial by the mercy authority or an unrestricted judicial substitution of its own mercy decision. Equally, Article 361's personal protection of the President does not make a resulting executive order wholly beyond review.

**Worked distinction:** A note says a mercy decision can never be questioned because the President is immune. This confuses the office-holder's personal constitutional protection with the legality of an executive decision. Another note says the President may grant mercy contrary to the government's advice merely from personal preference. That conflicts with the aid-and-advice framework. [Articles 74 and 361][constitution]; [Shatrughan Chauhan][clemency]

## 7. Other powers and safeguards in context

### 7.1 Emergency powers are separately conditioned

Articles **352, 356 and 360** concern national emergency, failure of constitutional machinery in a State and financial emergency respectively. Their grounds, approval periods, duration rules and effects differ. For Article 352, the constitutional requirement concerns the written decision of the **Union Cabinet**, not merely a loosely attributed view of any minister. [Articles 352, 356 and 360][constitution]

An emergency does not mean that all Fundamental Rights automatically disappear or that the President becomes an unrestricted personal ruler. The full distinctions belong to [036 — Emergencies and constitutional safeguards](036-emergencies-and-constitutional-safeguards.md), currently a planning placeholder. Retain this boundary instead of memorising incomplete emergency procedures here.

### 7.2 Article 143 and the Supreme Court

Under Article 143(1), the President may refer a question of law or fact of public importance for the Supreme Court's opinion; the Court may report its opinion after a hearing it considers appropriate. Article 143(2) addresses a distinct category of disputes with different wording. An advisory reference is not the same as an appeal by a losing litigant or a requirement before every presidential act. The 2025 assent opinion specifically rejects a compulsory Article 143 reference for each reserved State bill. [Article 143][constitution]; [2025 answers][assent]

### 7.3 Personal protection is not blanket legal impunity

Article 361 protects the President from personal answerability to courts for official functions, subject to its constitutional provisions, including the impeachment investigation setting. During the term, criminal proceedings cannot be instituted or continued against the President and court process for arrest/imprisonment cannot issue. Civil proceedings concerning personal acts are subject to the specified two-month notice condition. [Article 361][constitution]

Do not rewrite this as “no proceedings against anyone concerning presidential action” or “all personal civil conduct is permanently immune”. The article distinguishes protection of the office-holder, the impeachment framework, and proceedings against the appropriate government. Detailed litigation procedure is outside this chapter; the examination distinction is the scope of the protection, not a filing guide.

## 9. Common confusions

| Confusion | Correction |
|---|---|
| Every power vested in the President is a personal discretionary power. | Read its constitutional procedure together with Article 74. |
| Reconsideration lets the President reject the same advice forever. | Advice after reconsideration must be followed. |
| President, Parliament and either House are identical categories. | President forms part of Parliament but is not a member of either House. |
| An ordinary-bill return and a constitutional-amendment veto are the same. | Article 111 and Article 368 impose different requirements; duly passed amendments require assent. |
| An ordinance requires both Houses to be out of session. | The power is unavailable when both are in session; at least one out of session can satisfy that condition. |
| Its six weeks always start on promulgation or first House reassembly. | The clock starts on parliamentary reassembly, using the later date if Houses differ. |
| Retrospectivity means any retrospective criminal punishment is allowed. | Constitutional safeguards, including Article 20(1), still apply. |
| Mercy is personal discretion and immune from review. | Advice applies; limited judicial review is available. |
| A no-numerical-deadline statement proves every delay is lawful. | Identify the article, precise holding and review context; avoid universal conclusions. |

## 10. Summary of key concepts and takeaways

Presidential powers organise constitutional action within a parliamentary government. The formal authority to appoint, summon, assent or promulgate does not remove ministerial responsibility or the legal conditions attached to each power. Reconsideration is a constitutional checkpoint, not perpetual personal control of government policy.

Legislation illustrates the distinctions. Union assent, reserved State bills, constitutional amendments and ordinances each follow different provisions. The absence of one article's numerical deadline does not erase deadlines elsewhere. An ordinance has legislative force while remaining temporary, reviewable and subject to constitutional competence and rights.

Clemency and immunity require the same method: distinguish the decision's authority and limits from personal protection of the office-holder. The relevant court judgment, statutory context and article matter more than a broad label such as “absolute power”.

### 11.2 Dated current connections

- **Assent and reserved bills:** the **20 November 2025** Special Reference opinion is the checked source for Article 201's no-court-prescribed-timeline conclusion and rejection of deemed assent. Record the article and holding, not a generic “President may delay anything” slogan. Later controlling judgments or constitutional changes require review. [Official answers][assent]
- **Ordinance reporting:** when a new ordinance appears, identify its promulgation date, why Article 123 was available, the Houses' reassembly dates and its subsequent laying, withdrawal, disapproval or replacement Act. - **Mercy decisions:** use the **2014 Shatrughan Chauhan** explanation for advice and limited-review principles.
